## 0. Voice Signature

Patterns that show up over and over. Quoted examples are verbatim from THOMAS turns.

1. **Open with "Right?" as a conversational pivot.** Almost every step of every explanation begins or ends with "right?" — it's how he checks the student is tracking before he moves on.
   - "So the basics behind a Newman projection is, like this arrow shows, if you imagine yourself looking down this bond, right?"
   - "So, in this position it's axial, in this position it's equatorial, right?"

2. **"Does that make sense?" as a chapter-end ping.** He closes a beat with this exact phrase before progressing. It's not rhetorical — he waits.
   - "We must maintain the stereochemistry seen in our original object… does that make sense?"
   - "Naomi is a strong base. Very strong base. Does that make more sense?"

3. **Permission-to-not-get-it preface.** Before introducing something hard, he names it as hard and gives the student cover.
   - "You're probably like, I have no idea what the fuck you're talking about, that's okay. I just wanted to introduce the idea."
   - "I know I'm throwing a lot at you, you're gonna go back into this, and you're gonna see what I'm saying."

4. **Profanity as warmth, not aggression.** "Fuck," "shit," "what the hell" — used to bond with the student, never at them.
   - "let's get into some less fun shit, but more fun, DAT."
   - "fingerprint region… forget about it. It is nonsense."

5. **"Trust your gut" as a recurring directive.** He repeats this verbatim across sessions and frames it as a skill being trained.
   - "you're gonna hear me say, trust your gut so many times… we are forming that gut feeling."
   - "I like it, go with your gut."

6. **Names traps as personalities (anthropomorphizes reagents).** Reagents get nicknames so they stick.
   - "Naomi is my homie." / "This is the twin sister, Nioet. Right? Ethyl instead of a methyl."
   - "Naomi, basic bitch, she loves eliminating shit. That's her thing."
   - "Chromium Jones, right? That's how it is."

7. **Physical-world metaphors for invisible chemistry.** He maps abstract structure to bodies, boats, subways, fans.
   - Axial groups: "if you put something in the axial position, and it's kind of, like, sticking up… it kind of looks like a sail, right? And that thing is going to make it super top-heavy."
   - Steric hindrance: "just like we do as humans, we want our space… when we're sitting on a subway, right? We don't want somebody to come sit down right next to us."
   - Tert-butyl: "that little fan shape."
   - Misocompound symmetry: "We can fold those over. Right? Like a hot dog bun."

8. **Diagnoses misses by reading the student's reasoning, not the answer.** He almost never says "wrong" — he reconstructs the chain of thought that led there.
   - "I feel like you saw this, and you're like, I think that's gonna end up in carboxylic acid or something along those lines."
   - "You knew what you were doing, but in the opposite way."
   - "your decision makes sense, given that, right? But when we're talking about basicity…"

9. **"It just kind of happens" / "you just kind of go in a direction" — names what is NOT a rule.** Distinguishes things you choose from things that follow.
   - "axial and equatorial just kind of happen. The thing that we want to keep an eye on is our up and down."
   - "You just kind of go in a direction. You just kind of start, and you just follow through on that direction."

10. **Compresses a whole topic into one rule of thumb.** Always offers the cheat after teaching the long way.
    - "If you see a ring, think EAS."
    - "1500 and below is our fingerprint region, and nothing will exist in there."
    - "When you see HV, think radicals."
    - "If they're the same, they're identical… if it's exactly opposites, they're enantiomers."

11. **Frames timing for the right phase.** Constantly tells students *not* to time themselves yet.
    - "The only section you'd be worried about timing right now is reading… Everything else, we're trying to just get accuracy."
    - "Speed doesn't really pick up until you get to the practice test phase."

12. **"Count your carbons" as a verbal tic when converting structures.** He literally said he'd make a shirt of this.
    - "I am gonna make a shirt that says, count your carbons, because… you'll hear me say, count your carbons, count your carbons, count your carbons."

13. **"On the precipice" / "stacking wins" — momentum language during reviews.** Frames small score bumps as breakthroughs.
    - "you're like a practice test or two away from being exactly where we want to."
    - "now we gotta keep stacking good days, keep stacking wins."
    - "On the other side of the bad feeling is the good feeling that we're looking for."

14. **"Pray for that question" — names the gifts.** When the test serves up an easy one, he flags it as a freebie.
    - "this is a good question, because it makes you slow down for a second… this is a question, if given to us, we want to be like, thank you."
    - "we pray for the questions."

15. **Self-deprecating framing of his own teaching.** Lowers stakes mid-explanation.
    - "I hate drawing these things. Alright, this is… it's actually a decent chair."
    - "That probably confused the shit out of you. I don't deny that."
    - "excuse my terrible containers."

## 1. How Thomas Sets Up a Worked Problem

There's a consistent move sequence across sessions:

**Step A — Name the topic and lower the stakes.** Before walking through, he labels it ("These are tough" / "They're not that bad" / "I hate these things") so the student isn't guessing whether to be worried.

**Step B — Give the ONE thing to look for.** He compresses the topic to its single hinge before any mechanism. Examples:
- Newman stability: "Stability is key, right? They'll ask you, mainly, whether they bring up a Newman projection question, it's going to ask you about what's the most stable form or the least stable form."
- Titration: "We don't want to look at starting and ending point to answer this question. There's one thing we want to look at to answer this question… the equivalence point."
- Le Chatelier: "the number of moles on each side is what we're looking for."

**Step C — Walk it with constant "right?" check-ins, never more than one sentence between checks.**

**Step D — Diagnose where the student would have gone wrong.** He often pre-empts: "I feel like you saw this, and you're like, ___."

**Step E — Compress to a portable rule.** Closes with the cheat: "If you see ___, think ___."

**Step F — Hand it back.** "Now I'm gonna have you do one without me stepping in on this next one." He explicitly cues the handoff.

A second consistent move is the **long-way-then-shortcut** sequence (most explicit on redox):
- "I'm gonna show you the long way first… You gotta know how to do it, and then you get to know the shortcut."
- "If we just go to shortcuts and don't understand what's going on, then it could go wrong. I want you to be able to rely on the true way first."

## 3. How Thomas Teaches Mechanism (OChem)

### Stereo (Newman, chair, R/S, Fisher)

**Newman setup.** "if you were to look down that bond, right, we draw that first carbon as the center circle, and then this carbon behind it, we draw like this." He always grounds the abstraction in physical viewing first.

**Newman stability rule (compressed).** "When they're equally spaced, like you see in B, this is called our staggered confirmation. When they're kind of, like, looking like they're on top of each other, this is called our eclipsed conformation… Eclipsed, you can kind of think about it, like, what is a solar eclipse?"

**Chair conformation — the "boat with a sail" metaphor.** This is his signature image:
> "if you imagine this chair is kind of like a boat, right? It's like a flat boat, and if you put something in the axial position, and it's kind of sticking up, right? The boat is on the water, right? This axial position, it kind of looks like a sail, right? And that thing is going to make it super top-heavy and not stable whatsoever. Where if you put it in the equatorial position, and it's just dragging it… it's much more stable."

**Chair — the up/down vs axial/equatorial distinction.** "axial and equatorial just kind of happen. The thing that we want to keep an eye on is our up and down." Followed by: "if it's on a wedge in a wedge, right, we would say there's sin… they have to either both be up or both be down. They just have to be the same."

**R/S — the wedge flip.** "if it's pointing to the right, right? then we would say that is R, so you can have R, or R and S, right? … if the fourth priority group is on a wedge, we flip it." Repeated in Fisher form: "horizontal is the same thing as a wedge."

**Identifying chiral centers.** "the hint is where substituents are coming off, or where two rings are fused." Also: "If it can eventually be different, there'll be different groups."

**Meso compounds — the hot dog bun.** "miso compound is something that has at least, and typically is just two chiral centers. But has the internal plane of symmetry, which makes them achiral… We can fold those over. Right? Like a hot dog bun."

### SN/E

**E2 anti-periplanar.** "if we had the bromine like this, we would need the hydrogen to be on a dash. You need it to be on the opposite stereochemistry relationship, right? Or anti to each other."

He always restates the anti rule with a physical instruction: "we're rotating this… that right side and right alkanes can rotate, right? And we rotate that bromine to then be on a dash."

**Naomi family.** "Naomi is my homie" / "All it means is a strong base, and it's protic… Counterpart solvent." / "Most reaction does E2 reactions… Because it's small. It's a small, strong base." / "In rare cases. It can do SN2, but I'd much rather you default to E2."

### EAS

**The recognition shortcut.** "When you see EAS, you can be like, this is… this is Thomas' favorite. He loves these. And maybe that'll help you recognize that EAS is a big group of reactions that involve aromatics."

**The "don't memorize the mechanism" license.** "we do not have to know the intermediates… We just have to be able to recognize if we have a ring, and we're adding something to it."

**Substitution-disguised-as-addition.** "really, you're substituting that SO3H in for a hydrogen. So, like, it's technically a substitution, but yes, it does look like an addition. So, EAS, if you see a ring, think EAS."

**Tin/HCl nitro→amine — director flip.** "tin and HCl can change your nitro group to an amine group, which changes your directing group from a meta to ortho-para, which is so powerful."

### Carbonyl + enolates

**Ketal recognition.** "you should be able to recognize that when you have a ketone, and you have a group with multiple alcohols, right, we're gonna be doing a ketal formation." Visual cue: "these two hydrogens are gonna run off and form H2O."

**Enolate fundamentals.** "in every enolate reaction, we are deprotonating an alpha hydrogen, and then that negative charge is going to become your nucleophile, which then goes and attacks another carbonyl, the base of a carbonyl." Followed by his low-yield disclaimer: "honestly, they're so low yield, I don't want to emphasize them."

**Jones reagent.** "Chromium Jones, right? That's how it is." Rule: "if it's a primary alcohol… It forms carboxylic acid. If it was a secondary alcohol, though, it would only form a ketone."

**NaBH4 vs LiAlH4.** "NABH4 is a nice guy, is a weak reduction agent."

**Carbocation/alkene stability — distinct concepts warning.** "carbocation stability is tertiary… This is alkene stability, so we want a tetra substituted." And: "Carbocations and radicals have their own stability rules. Alkenes are different."

**Allylic/benzylic resonance.** "you can store a charge on multiple locations… we can share the load."

## 4. How Thomas Teaches Gen Chem

### Redox

**Acid-medium recipe.** "we gotta break this into two half-reactions… Balance all non O or H's. Then balance oxygen with H2Os. Then balance H's with H+. Then balance charge with electrons." His framing: "It's like a recipe, you know, once you know the recipe, you can make it whenever you want."

**Long way first.** "I'm gonna show you a little shortcut, but I'm gonna show you the long way first… You gotta know how to do it, and then you get to know the shortcut."

**Oxidation state shortcut.** "if you just look at the oxidation state of the non-oxygen or hydrogen, that's the shortcut." Followed by the worked check: "we still got 5 and 2, right?"

**Oxidation state vs charge.** "Oxidation state, right, that is the charge of a specific atom within a molecule… but the charge of MnO4 is minus 1."

**Direction check.** "if you somehow get electrons on the same side for both your half reactions, you did something wrong. You gotta have your electrons be on opposite sides."

### Electrochem

**Red Cat An Ox.** "Red Cat An Ox… reduction happens at the cathode. Oxidation happens at the anode."

**Visual electron cue.** "If they're on the reactant side, reduction. If they're on the product side, oxidation."

### Titration

**The one rule.** "We don't want to look at starting and ending point to answer this question. There's one thing we want to look at to answer this question… the equivalence point."

**The four cases.** "strong, strong, weak, weak, it's gonna be neutral. If it's weak base strong acid, it's gonna be slightly acidic, and then vice versa, strong base, weak acid, it's gonna be slightly basic."

### Le Chatelier (volume/pressure)

**The one thing to look for.** "the number of moles on each side is what we're looking for."

**The intuition.** "if you're increasing the volume, the side with more moles will eat up the space quicker… But when you go and reduce the volume, right, the side with the more moles is just more reactive… We're gonna push towards the more stable side."

### Empirical formula

**The mental setup.** "we want to imagine there's a 100 gram sample." Then molar ratio: "we gotta find the molar ratio."

**End rule.** "the empirical formula is the most reduced version."

### Density / unit panic

His coaching is mostly emotional reframing of unit-panic: "don't let any weird units throw you off. You know how to change units to match up." Followed by "mass over volume" basics. The shortcut he loved was the **reciprocal rule** (see below).

### Reciprocal rule for division (no-calc trick)

This is THE math trick from the Antonia session:
> "little trick I like, when you're dividing by a decimal or fraction, you can multiply by the reciprocal."

Worked example for 60 / 0.8: "0.8 we can rewrite as 8 tenths… or you can simplify that down even further to 4 over 5… if you multiply this by 5 fourths… much simpler than dividing by a .792."

Why: "when you're dividing something by a fraction or a decimal less than 1, you're actually going to make it a bigger number."

### Phase change graph (5-block setup)

"you're gonna have to set up 5 different parts, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5… Q equals MC delta T for temperature changes, and Q equals M delta H for the phase changes."

Signature add-on: "To vaporize something takes a ton more energy than it does to melt something."

### Sig figs

"if it didn't have the decimal trailing zero, right, you would have been correct… this is a question, if given to us, we want to be like, thank you, but then make sure we get it right."

### Nuclear

"Think about it kind of like a math equation, with the numbers." Then: solve for X in the mass-number balance. Closing rule: "It doesn't have to necessarily add up to be the mass number."

### VSEPR

Hammer-rule: "The major difference is the lone pair. That's it. The lone pair. As long as you can remember, there's a lone pair, it's pyramidal. If there's no lone pair, it's planar."

### Hund's vs Aufbau

"Hund's rule tells us that we gotta fill them out one at a time… Hund's rule is the filling out total, and then the off-ball principle tells us we need to have the opposite spin."

### NMR (CNMR symmetry, multiplicity, integration)

"With CNMR, right, they typically just ask you about symmetry-type questions."

Multiplicity: "neighboring hydrogens plus 1."
Integration: "Integration is literally telling you, at that singlet… The integration is the amount of hydrogens at that peak."

### IR — fingerprint region

"with these 1200s and 800s, right, we should be able to immediately rule those out, because everything 1500 and less is called the fingerprint region… scientists in the lab, right, they would put their finger, their thumb, over that region, because they're like, forget about it. It is nonsense."

### General Gen Chem framing

"a lot of Gen Chem is just, like, either definition-based, or, like, doing simple math."
"Redox and electrochem to me, those are the two that I see come up the most for people."

## 5. How Thomas Teaches PAT / QR Strategy

This is thinner than OChem/GC because two of the three sessions only touched PAT briefly. What's there:

### Grid method (hole punching)

**Why grids over mental flipping.** "you do it mentally, great, but then what if you forget what you did on the first flip? Right? That's the only thing that's the big downside."

**Reps argument.** "I practiced the grid method for 15 practice tests, so with that on practice, I just became automatic with it. And by the time I got to practice test 5, I was getting 14, 15 out of 15 on every hole punching, every cube counting."

**Don't ask your head to do too much.** "you take the pressure off of yourself of remembering shit, and the test is already asking enough of you, like, why? I don't want you to ask more of yourself than you need to."

**X vs O variation.** "she uses O's, like, for the hole punching. I use X's. The X's is what made the most sense to me, because I like to picture the half-holes."

**Pre-draw during the leftover time.** "after you finish the whole natural science section… those last 5-10 minutes you have, already start drawing the grids."

### Method matching

"what works for me may not work for you. And so, I will say with those two methods, though, they tend to be pretty universal." He'll let students drop a method after 2 tries: "after the first time, you know, you're like, I don't know, give it a second try… If after 2 times, you're like, fuck this, then we're gonna abandon it."

### Targets

"we need near perfect on those" (hole punching / cube counting).
On scoring: "you're like a practice test or two away from being exactly where we want to, from breaking through that 370, or no, sorry, 390 to 440, 450, like, you're on the precipice of it."

### Top front end is the hard one

"Top 49 is tough."
"top front end's the toughest."
He defers strategy for top-front-end and pattern folding to live coaching: "don't do Top Front End and Pattern Folding until we talk about it Friday, because I don't want you to just spin your wheels."

### PAT timing posture

"Don't worry about the timing yet with PAT… The only section you'd be worried about timing right now is reading. Everything else, we're trying to just get accuracy, and so if it takes you 2 minutes to do a Top 49 question, it's fine."

### Reading: 10-in-10

"the nice middle ground is holding yourself to the 10 in 10 method, where it's 10 minutes of reading as much as you can. And then 10 minutes of, like, modified search and destroy."

**Why he pushes away from pure search-and-destroy.** "It's good for the 80% of questions that are direct recall… But for those 20% that are the understanding of the passage, it's gonna be a lot… I don't want to set us off at a 20% disadvantage."

**Highlighting rules.** "something in the first sentence of every paragraph. Lists, whenever you have a list. Anything that's, like, a quantity, a date, a name, a who, what, where, when, why answer. A definition. Something — a word you don't know, highlight it."

**Why highlight at all (two-fold reason).** "A, it's to draw your attention back to it when you can see the question. B, you're more likely to remember something when you take the second to highlight it."

### QR

Light coverage in these three sessions. Main posture: "QR is something that we don't need to focus so much on early, because QR can be picked up quickly later on."

## 6. Study Strategy + Mindset

### The arc

"the content review phase is just to learn what they're testing you on. It's not to completely master things just yet."

"that's where the magic happens, right? That's where we can identify our weak spots, our strong spots. And then we maintain the strong spots while we work on the weak spots."

"To learn something, it takes some time, but then when you're testing it, you know… Then we put the time pressure on, which is a whole other fun part of the DAT."

### Move, don't perfect

"don't worry about perfection. Don't worry about mastery just yet. You're gonna get some questions wrong on the question banks. It's okay. Understand what happened, try to learn from them, but you don't need to redo them just yet."

"paralysis by analysis, right? If you start to overanalyze things, you paralyze yourself. We just want to move, move, move."

"Don't stick around too long."

### The 5 to 10 plateau

"when you start to get into 6 through 10, the tests get more difficult. The tests 1 through 5 are the most indicative of the actual exam. 6 through 10, they're just giving you extra practice. Now you may see your scores kind of plateau a little bit. That's okay. You're still getting better, the tests are also getting tougher."

### Why we take so many practice tests (two reasons)

"these practice tests have multiple reasons why we do them. A, so you can just… the content, right? Test yourself on the content. You review them and go through them. B, you're putting yourself in a position where, you know, there is stress, where there is pressure. And you need to be able to, in that moment, overcome that, and be able to go into your archive and pull out the information needed."

### Question banks vs practice tests

"question banks are not representative of the actual test. Question banks are for learning. The practice tests are representative of the actual test questions."

### After a bad score

"On the other side of the bad feeling is the good feeling that we're looking for, right? You gotta push through."

"don't get carried away, good or bad, right? We just gotta maintain our methods, and our structure, and our consistency. Consistency is the key."

### After a good score

"a lot of mistakes are, like, you know very quickly what's going on, or they're just, like, something that we talk about, and you're like, oh, duh."

"Take the little wins, don't get too comfortable, but then keep that feeling… now we gotta keep stacking good days, keep stacking wins."

Warning against complacency: "sometimes someone will score, like, a 440 on a practice test, like, I'm good, I don't have to keep studying… no, please, no, like, it goes so quick, like, on a test this big, if you don't stay active with it, you're gonna lose it quickly."

### Why fight panic

"our brains love to fuck with us, and they love to forget things when there's a time pressure… the more we can work through it, the more we can work through that feeling."

"consistency, and trusting yourself, and not allowing the monkey brain in us to overtake us, right? Not allowing the panic to send in, to be able to breathe through the moment."

### Comparison

"Comparison is the thief of joy."
"if you're gonna compare, make sure to remember the other side, too, so you don't just get down on yourself."
"there's always someone better… but are they better? Are they happier? Are they… like, who knows what's going on, right?"

### Becoming a test-taker

"we're not just learning things to learn things, we're learning things to become a good test taker. And to become a good test taker, we need to take as many tests as possible. That's the name of the game with standardized tests."

### When a student is discouraged

He leads with witness, not solutions. Tells his own story of failing out: "About as far as you can get away from a 4.0. A .6." Then: "it's not how many times you fall off the horse, it's about how many times you get back on."

"be easy on yourself, but understand that it doesn't matter why this question inherently got wrong. What matters is you didn't recognize what hydroboration oxidation is."

"that's the biggest thing that changed in my life, is I used to be so hard on myself, and now I'm easy on myself."

## 7. Candidate Coach-Card Authoring Material

Cards written in Thomas's voice using his actual phrasings. Each is a 2-4 sentence drop-in for an Ace Labs coach-card slot. Quote markers retained where the phrase is verbatim.

### Card 1
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Newman projections (stability question prompt)
- **When to use it:** Student is staring at a Newman MCQ asking about most/least stable.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Look, with Newmans, "stability is key." 9 times out of 10 the question is about most stable or least stable, and the rule is one line: the big groups want to be as far apart as possible. Staggered = elbow room = stable. Eclipsed is like a solar eclipse — they're stacked on each other, and "just like we do as humans, we want our space."
- **Source:** Marilyn 05-27, Newman intro.

### Card 2
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Chair conformations (axial vs equatorial intuition)
- **When to use it:** Student about to place groups on a chair and pick the more stable form.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Picture the chair like a flat boat on the water. A group in axial is a sail sticking straight up — top-heavy, unhappy. A group in equatorial is just dragging behind the boat — chill, stable. So bulky groups (think tert-butyl, the little fan shape) belong in equatorial. And remember: axial and equatorial just kind of happen — the thing you actually control is up vs down.
- **Source:** Marilyn 05-27, chair conformation teach.

### Card 3
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — R/S (the wedge flip)
- **When to use it:** Student assigning R/S with the 4th priority group on a wedge.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Number 1, 2, 3, draw the circle, ignore 4 for a second. If your 4th priority — usually the H — is on a dash, what you drew is what you get. If it's on a wedge, flip it. So "looks like R, but is actually S." Same rule for Fisher projections, just translate: horizontal IS a wedge.
- **Source:** Marilyn 05-27, R/S walkthrough + Fisher.

### Card 4
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Identifying chiral centers
- **When to use it:** Student counting chiral centers on a complex molecule.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** The hint: look where substituents branch off a ring, or where two rings are fused. Those are the chiral hot spots. And the gut check — "if it can eventually be different, there'll be different groups." Don't get stuck because two paths leave the same carbon; trace them out, see if they ever diverge.

### Card 5
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Meso compounds
- **When to use it:** Student sees two chiral centers and is about to call the molecule chiral.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Pause. Check for an internal plane of symmetry — can you fold it like a hot dog bun and have the two halves stack? If yes, that's a meso compound: chiral centers exist, but the molecule is achiral. They give people trouble. Be easy on yourself, and spend extra time here.

### Card 6
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — E2 anti-periplanar
- **When to use it:** Student facing an E2 product question with wedges/dashes.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** The leaving group and the hydrogen you're knocking off have to be anti — 180 degrees across from each other. If the bromine's on a wedge, the H you eliminate has to be on a dash. If they're not already lined up, rotate the back carbon until they are. The DAT loves to test this — one student told me she had two anti-periplanar questions in a row.
- **Source:** Marilyn 05-27 (anti-peri intro) + Luis/Kyra 05-27 (Naomi E2).

### Card 7
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — EAS recognition
- **When to use it:** Student sees a benzene ring + reagent and isn't sure what family it is.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Quick filter: if you see a ring, think EAS. That's the family — electrophilic aromatic substitution. You don't have to memorize the carbocation intermediate or every resonance step ("luckily, we don't have to know that"). You just have to recognize: ring + something getting added = EAS. It looks like addition, but it's really substitution — that hydrogen on the ring is what's getting kicked off.
- **Source:** Luis/Kyra 05-27, sulfonation question.

### Card 8
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Naomi (NaOMe / NaOEt)
- **When to use it:** Student sees NaOMe or NaOEt and isn't sure what it does.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Naomi is my homie. She's a small strong base in a protic solvent, and she loves doing E2 — Zaitsev, more substituted alkene. In rare cases she'll do SN2, but default to E2. You'll also see her in enolate reactions deprotonating the alpha hydrogen. "Basic bitch, she loves eliminating shit. That's her thing."
- **Source:** Antonia 05-26 + Luis/Kyra 05-27.

### Card 9
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Jones reagent (CrO3 / H2SO4)
- **When to use it:** Student sees Jones and is choosing the oxidation product.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Chromium Jones. He's a strong oxidizer. Primary alcohol → carboxylic acid (top of the food chain). Secondary alcohol → ketone. That's it. If you ever doubt yourself on this one, remember: it's not your job to redo organic chem, it's your job to spot the reagent and pick the product.
- **Source:** Luis/Kyra 05-27 (Jones celebration) + Antonia 05-26.

### Card 10
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Enolate fundamentals
- **When to use it:** Student is choosing whether a carbonyl can undergo an enolate reaction.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** First, find the alpha carbon — the one directly next to the carbonyl. Are there hydrogens ON that alpha carbon? If no alpha hydrogens, no enolate. If yes, the mechanism always starts the same way: deprotonate the alpha H, the carbon goes negative, and that negative carbon is now your nucleophile attacking another carbonyl. Even if you don't remember the rest, that one move gets you most of the partial credit.
- **Source:** Luis/Kyra 05-27 + Antonia 05-26.

### Card 11
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — Carbocation/alkene stability separation
- **When to use it:** Student conflating carbocation rules with alkene rules.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Two different ranking ladders, do not mix them up. Carbocation stability: primary < secondary < tertiary < allylic < benzylic — and allylic/benzylic only count when there's an actual double bond or aromatic ring nearby. Alkene stability: mono < di < tri < tetra-substituted. So when you see a Zaitsev question, you're picking the most substituted alkene — not the most stable carbocation.
- **Source:** Antonia 05-26, alkene stability sequence.

### Card 12
- **Engine + topic:** GenChem — Titration curves (acid/base strength)
- **When to use it:** Student sees a titration curve MCQ asking what type it is.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Forget the starting pH. Forget the ending pH. There's one thing to look at: the equivalence point — the middle of the vertical jump. Drop a line from there to the y-axis. If it lands at 7, it's strong/strong or weak/weak. Below 7 (like 5-6), weak base + strong acid. Above 7 (like 8-9), strong base + weak acid. That's the whole question.
- **Source:** Luis/Kyra 05-27 + Antonia 05-26.

### Card 13
- **Engine + topic:** GenChem — Le Chatelier (volume/pressure)
- **When to use it:** Student facing a Le Chatelier shift question.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Count moles on each side, that's the whole game. If you INCREASE volume, the side with MORE moles eats up the space — equilibrium shifts there. If you DECREASE volume (or increase pressure), shift toward the side with FEWER moles, because that side is more stable in tight quarters. "The side with less moles is just more stable, and the side with more moles is gonna be more reactive."
- **Source:** Luis/Kyra 05-27, Le Chatelier walkthrough.

### Card 14
- **Engine + topic:** GenChem — Phase change graph (heating curve calorimetry)
- **When to use it:** Student facing a multi-step heating curve problem.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Set up FIVE blocks. Heat solid (MCΔT, C of ice). Melt (M·ΔH_fusion). Heat liquid (MCΔT, C of water). Vaporize (M·ΔH_vaporization). Heat gas (MCΔT, C of steam). Watch the C value swap at each phase — three different specific heats. And don't forget: to vaporize takes way more energy than to melt. The equation is Q = MC ΔT (times, not plus — common slip).
- **Source:** Luis/Kyra 05-27, phase change graph.

### Card 15
- **Engine + topic:** GenChem — Redox shortcut
- **When to use it:** Student wants the fast oxidation-state route after learning the long way.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Once you know how to do the four-step long way — balance non-O/H, then O with water, then H with H+, then charge with electrons — you've earned the shortcut. Just find the oxidation state of the non-oxygen/hydrogen atom on each side. The difference is your electron count. Same answer, way less arithmetic. But do the long way two or three times first, "I want you to be able to rely on the true way first."
- **Source:** Antonia 05-26, redox shortcut.

### Card 16
- **Engine + topic:** QR — Reciprocal rule for division (no-calc)
- **When to use it:** Student facing division by a decimal under .1 or any awkward fraction.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Little trick: when you're dividing by a decimal or a fraction, multiply by the reciprocal. So 60 ÷ 0.8 becomes 60 × (10/8), which simplifies to 60 × (5/4) = 75. "Much simpler than dividing by .792." And remember why: dividing by a number less than 1 actually makes the result bigger, which trips people up.
- **Source:** Antonia 05-26, density problem.

### Card 17
- **Engine + topic:** OChem — IR fingerprint region rule-out
- **When to use it:** Student staring at an IR question with answer choices below 1500.
- **The card (in Thomas's voice):** Anything 1500 and below is the fingerprint region. Cross it out instantly. Scientists used to literally cover that range with their thumb — "forget about it. It is nonsense." For organic-compound ID, your real signals live above 1500: carbonyl around 1700, alcohols/amines way up in the 3000s, C=C aromatic right around 1600.

### Card 18

## Notes on Coverage and Gaps

- **OChem is the richest seam.** Stereo, EAS, E2/SN, enolates, carbonyl reagents, IR/NMR — all directly quotable.
- **GenChem is strong on redox, titration, Le Chatelier, phase-change, sig figs, VSEPR.** Thinner on kinetics, thermo beyond phase change, gas laws, acid-base equilibrium (Ka/Kb math).
- **PAT coverage is thin.** Only hole-punching/cube-counting strategy and high-level posture (don't time yet, near-perfect target, top-front-end is hardest). No verbatim teaching of keyholes, angle ranking, or pattern folding mechanics — those got deferred to a future session.
- **QR coverage is minimal.** Only the reciprocal-rule trick is fully captured; he says elsewhere he loves QR but he doesn't walk a QR problem in these three sessions.
- **Bio coverage is light.** Sarcomere visual + organelle photos + general "endocrine/muscle/renal are the hardest" framing. No verbatim teaching on biochem pathways, genetics, etc.

If the goal is to author cards across all engines from Thomas voice, OChem and GenChem are ready.
