# Would videos help? A plan for Ace Labs subtopic videos

Draft for Thomas, 2026-10-30. Answers "make every question have a detailed
explainer and a visual. Then I can start making videos if that would help too."

## The answer in one paragraph

Yes, as a targeted add-on, not a replacement. The research says video usually
helps when it is added to teaching students already have, and that short,
segmented, narrated videos beat long ones. It also says a clear static picture
with labels teaches "how it works" about as well as an animation, so the written
explainer and annotated visual on every question stay the base layer. Videos
earn their place where the thing to learn is a change over time or a procedure
(a mechanism, an unfold, a tree that branches, a feedback loop), where students
keep missing the same subtopic, and where a strategy has to be watched to be
copied (PAT). So: one 60 to 120 second video per subtopic, a screen recording of
Ace Labs' own visualizer in your voice, linked from the explainer's Watch line,
offered after a miss and in a lesson's learn step, never required. Start with
the 20 below.

## 1. What the learning research says

Every source below was read through web-search result excerpts. Direct fetches
to aera.net, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, learningscientists.org and wikicap.ulb.be
failed from this sandbox (DNS refusal), so effect sizes are as the excerpts and
secondary summaries report them; the DOIs are given so they can be checked.

| Finding | What it means for Ace Labs | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Across 105 randomized trials (7,776 students), replacing existing teaching with video gave a small gain (g = 0.28); adding video to existing teaching gave a large one (g = 0.80). Videos rarely hurt | Add videos on top of the explainers and lessons; do not swap the written explainer for a video | Noetel et al. 2021, Review of Educational Research 91(2), doi 10.3102/0034654321990713 |
| Animations beat static pictures by a small to medium amount on average (d = 0.37 in 2007; g = 0.23 overall in a 2016 update, larger with spoken narration, g = 0.34). A re-analysis found the advantage is large when the learner has to learn the specifics of a change | Use video for things that move or change step by step (mechanisms, unfolding, pathways, branching probability), not for static facts | Hoffler and Leutner 2007, Learning and Instruction 17(6); Berney and Betrancourt 2016, Computers and Education 101; Ploetzner, Berney and Betrancourt 2020 |
| Static annotated illustrations with printed text matched or beat narrated animations for explaining how systems work, because they cut extra processing | The annotated visual on every question is the right base layer; a video must add motion or a procedure to be worth recording | Mayer, Hegarty, Mayer and Campbell 2005, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied 11(4), doi 10.1037/1076-898x.11.4.256 |
| Transient information: what disappears off screen before it is processed is lost; long continuous animations add load. Learner-paced segments help, with small to medium gains for retention and transfer (56 studies) | Keep each video to one idea, cut it into chapters, let the student pause and replay; give each question a start offset into the right chapter | Leahy and Sweller 2011, Applied Cognitive Psychology 25; Rey et al. 2019, Educational Psychology Review 31, doi 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4 |
| Signaling (highlighting what the narration names) improves retention (g = 0.53) and transfer (g = 0.33) | Record the same highlights, outlines and rank numbers the explainer visual uses, and point at them as you say them | Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey 2018, Educational Research Review 23 |
| In 6.9 million viewing sessions of edX videos, engagement fell off after about 6 minutes; shorter was better, and informal, personal recordings held attention better than polished studio lectures | 60 to 120 seconds; your normal coaching voice, not a script read stiffly | Guo, Kim and Rubin 2014, Learning at Scale, doi 10.1145/2556325.2566239 |
| Guidelines for effective educational video: manage load (signal, segment, weed out extras, match narration to visuals), keep it short and conversational, and make viewing active with questions | No music or decoration; narration plus the visual; end on a question | Brame 2016, CBE Life Sciences Education 15(4), doi 10.1187/cbe.16-03-0125 |
| Short tests inside online lectures cut mind wandering roughly in half (about 40 percent of probes to about 20 percent) and improved learning; a later study complicates the mind-wandering part | End every video with "Your turn": the player serves one same-skill item right after | Szpunar, Khan and Schacter 2013, PNAS 110(16); Jing, Szpunar and Schacter 2016 |
| Video modeling examples (watching someone solve) alternated with practice problems beat practice alone for procedural skills; worked examples help novices most and lose value as expertise grows (expertise reversal) | Offer the video after a miss and in the learn step of a lesson, not to students who are already Confirmed on the skill | van Harsel et al. (Utrecht); Hoogerheide, Loyens and van Gog 2014; Kalyuga 2007, Educational Psychology Review 19 |
| Showing the instructor's face does not reliably improve learning; it changes motivation and load | Screen recordings of the visualizer with your voice; your face only for the welcome and section-intro videos (as the decisions doc already says) | Alemdag 2022 (meta-analysis of 20 studies); Polat 2022 (systematic review) |
| Elaborated feedback (an explanation) beats right/wrong feedback (0.49 against 0.05) and beats showing the correct answer alone (0.32) | The written explainer after every question is the main lever; videos are the second | Van der Kleij, Feskens and Eggen 2015, Review of Educational Research |
| Spatial skills respond to training (g = 0.47 across 217 studies) and the gains last | PAT strategy videos are worth making: show the method on the Studio views, then practice | Uttal et al. 2013, Psychological Bulletin 139(2), doi 10.1037/a0028446 |
| Fourteen evidence-based principles for instructional video, including conversational style (personalization) | Talk to one student ("you"), the way the voice codex describes | Mayer 2021, Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, doi 10.1016/j.jarmac.2021.03.007 |

## 2. Where videos add the most

### 2.1 What students actually miss

The Ace Log in the portal's Airtable base holds 1,046 entries students logged
for questions they missed. Counted by section and concept only (no student
fields were read, nothing was copied out):

| Section | Logged misses | Most-logged concepts |
|---|---|---|
| Biology | 680 | endocrine system and hormones 26; embryonic stages (blastula, gastrula, neurulation) 21; evidence of evolution 19; cell organelles 18; plant structure and physiology 15; bacteria and archaea 11; taxonomy 10; adaptive immunity 10; animal behavior 10; biomolecules 9; prokaryote vs eukaryote 9 |
| General Chemistry | 157 | atomic structure 8; nuclear chemistry 5; solutions 5; oxidation states and redox 4; stoichiometry 4; electrochemistry 3 (49 entries name no concept) |
| Organic Chemistry | 110 | LiAlH4 and NaBH4 reductions 5; 1H NMR 3; meso compounds 3; carbocation stability and rearrangements 3; Grignard 2; Fischer projections 2 (37 name no concept) |
| QR | 82 | basic probability 14; translation and setup (word to equation) 8; percentages 5; data interpretation 5 |
| RC | 7 | reading strategy, timing per passage, inference |
| PAT | 6 | hole punching 2; top-front-end, pattern folding, keyholes, angle ranking 1 each |

Limits: students log selectively (Biology is the habit, so its share is
inflated), the concept field is free text, and the Practice Test miss table
(PT Misses) holds only 26 records, too few to rank. Error types logged are
mostly "knowledge gap" in Biology and evenly split between gaps, mix-ups and
slips in QR, which fits the research: a video fixes a gap or a mix-up in
understanding, not a slip. Once the player logs misses per qid and per trap
family, this list should be rebuilt from that data every month.

### 2.2 The three places to spend recording time

1. Subtopics students keep missing for lack of understanding: the Biology
   physiology, development and diversity concepts above, QR probability and
   setup, General Chemistry atomic structure and redox.
2. Hard multi-step reasoning where the order of moves is the lesson: organic
   mechanisms (substitution versus elimination, carbocation shifts, carbonyl
   reductions), stereochemistry with a flat H, conditional probability, word
   problem setup, titration curves. These are the "specifics of change" cases
   where animation beats a still picture.
3. PAT strategies: a method you watch once and then copy (unfold in reverse,
   floor maps, read the choices first, the silhouette). Spatial skill is
   trainable, and these are the items least served by text.

Not worth a video: one-fact recall (a list of hormones and glands is better as
an Ace Card), items students miss by a slip, and anything the static visual
already shows completely.

## 3. Format

| Choice | Decision |
|---|---|
| Length | 60 to 120 seconds; about 150 to 300 spoken words |
| What is on screen | A screen recording of the Ace Labs visualizer or engine view the explainer links to (same states, same highlights), in the light explainer theme. No slides, no music, no face |
| Voice | You, in the voice codex: open with the move ("Here is the secret"), name the trap ("Trap."), close with "Lock it in" and "Your turn". Never "wrong"; misses, not mistakes |
| Structure (four chapters) | 1. The trap students fall into (10 to 15 s). 2. The move, shown on the visualizer (30 to 50 s). 3. A second, faster example (15 to 30 s). 4. Lock it in and your turn (5 to 10 s) |
| One per subtopic | The slot id equals the explainer subtopic (explainer-standard.md, section 6); content/videos/index.json maps it to the file, title, duration and chapter start offsets, so every question in the subtopic links to it and a question can open at its chapter |
| Where it shows | The explainer's Watch line (after Check, mostly after a miss); the Learn and play step of the lesson (decisions doc: optional video); never before a question, never required, no completion tracking shown to the student |
| After it plays | The player serves one same-skill item ("Your turn"), Practice mode, untimed |
| Captions | A WebVTT file from the script, plain ASCII, on by default with sound off |
| Recording | A Playwright script drives the visualizer through the script's beats and records a 1280 x 720 clip; you record the voice track over it; ffmpeg joins them. You can also drive the screen yourself; the script is the same |
| Files and hosting | MP4 (H.264, 720p, about 6 to 12 MB a video) self-hosted beside the Retold audio with the same immutable cache header; the site's CSP already allows same-origin media. Twenty videos come to roughly 150 to 250 MB |
| House rules | Record with no timer pill, score or streak on screen (the PAT Studios need their no-clock teach mode first); no em dashes or "wrong" in captions; true-to-life views signed off as usual |
| Sign-off | The same five checks as an explainer, plus: the video shows exactly what the explainer's visual shows, and every claim matches the explainer |

Measuring whether it helps: for each subtopic, compare the next same-skill
item for students who opened the video after a miss with those who did not
(both see the written explainer). Keep a video only if that retry does at least
as well; never show any of this to students as a score.

## 4. The first 20 videos

Ranked by: how often students miss the subtopic (section 2.1), whether the idea
is a process or a strategy that motion shows best, whether it appears in
Full-Length Test 1 and the diagnostic (the free tier), and whether the Ace Labs
view to record already exists. Every script outline below is a starting draft
in your voice for you to change.

### 1. Conditional probability with a crowd (slot `qr.probability.conditional`, 90 s)

Why: the most-logged QR miss (basic probability, 14), a multi-step setup, and
the pilot's QR-3 already has the tree. Record: QR engine, Conditional view
(qrProbTable) set to prevalence 2, sensitivity 99, specificity 90, then the
pilot's 10,000-person tree. Outline: open on the trap, "the test is 99 percent
accurate, so a positive means 99 percent sick? Trap. That is the condition
flipped." Here is the secret: turn percents into people. Fill 10,000 people,
split 200 sick and 9,800 healthy, then split each by the test, saying each rate
as you drag it. Outline the 198 true positives, bracket every positive, 198 of
1,178, about 0.17. Second example fast: prevalence 10 percent, watch the answer
jump. Lock it in: given B means only the B branch counts. Your turn.

### 2. Hormones: who signals whom (slot `bio.physiology.endocrine`, 120 s)

Why: the most-logged miss in the whole Ace Log (26). Record: the Physiology
hub's endocrine visualizer (initEndocrine) and the endocrine table. Outline:
the trap is memorizing glands as a list. Here is the secret: every hormone
question is one of two moves, follow the axis or follow the receptor. Walk the
hypothalamus to anterior pituitary to target gland to hormone, then the negative
feedback arrow back up; show what happens to TSH when T4 is high. Then the
receptor move: peptide hormones bind at the surface and use a second messenger,
steroids cross the membrane and act on DNA, so steroids are slower and last
longer. Second example: cortisol. Lock it in: axis down, feedback up; peptides
knock, steroids walk in. Your turn.

### 3. Hole punching: unfold in reverse (slot `pat.holepunch.unfold-in-reverse`, 75 s)

Why: logged PAT misses and the clearest strategy to copy; the pilot's PAT-2
unfold strip exists. Record: the Hole Punching Studio's step-through (after the
no-clock teach mode is added) and the pilot strip. Outline: the trap is
unfolding in the order the folds were made. Here is the secret: last fold
first. Open fold 2, mirror only the holes on its flap, count; open fold 1,
mirror again, count. Point at the crease each time and say "same distance from
the crease". Second example with a diagonal fold. Lock it in: last fold first,
one crease at a time. Your turn.

### 4. Substitution or elimination: the four questions (slot `oc.subelim.sn1-sn2-e1-e2`, 120 s)

Why: the most-tested organic decision (192 bank items) and a pure order-of-moves
problem. Record: the Tree's substitution and elimination modules (t4-subelim,
t5-sn-e). Outline: the trap is picking the mechanism from the reagent alone.
Here is the secret: ask four questions in order, the substrate (methyl,
primary, secondary, tertiary), the nucleophile or base (strong or weak, bulky or
not), the solvent, the heat. Run three substrates through the four questions on
screen, each landing on SN2, E2 or SN1/E1 with the product drawn. Lock it in:
substrate first, then base strength and bulk, then solvent and heat. Your turn.

### 5. From one cell to three layers (slot `bio.devo.embryonic-stages`, 120 s)

Why: 21 logged misses on embryonic stages, plus germ layers and fertilization.
Record: the Development hub's embryo timeline (initEmbryoTimeline) and cleavage
types. Outline: the trap is mixing the order or the layers. Here is the secret:
it is one story, and each stage solves a problem: zygote, cleavage (more cells,
no growth), morula, blastula (a hollow ball), gastrulation (cells move in to
make three layers), neurulation (the neural tube from ectoderm). Scrub the
timeline and name each layer's main products as they appear. Second pass at
double speed. Lock it in: ecto outside and nerves, meso middle and muscle and
blood, endo inside and gut lining. Your turn.

### 6. Cube counting: the floor map (slot `pat.cubes.hidden-cubes`, 90 s)

Why: the pilot's PAT-3 showed the hidden cube nobody counts; the method is
visual. Record: the Cube Counting Studio (no clock) and the pilot's floor-1
cutaway and floor maps. Outline: the trap is counting only the faces you can
see. Here is the secret: paint every exposed side except the bottom, and count
floor by floor. Lift off the upper floors, write each cube's number on the map,
show the hidden cube under the tower with its one painted back side. Then read
the answer for one, two and three painted sides off the same maps. Lock it in:
bottom up, write it down, hidden cubes count. Your turn.

### 7. Evidence of evolution (slot `bio.evolution.evidence`, 90 s)

Why: 19 logged misses. Record: the Evolution hub's evidence visualizer
(initEvidence) and comparative embryos. Outline: the trap is calling every
similar structure homologous. Here is the secret: ask where the similarity came
from. Same ancestor, different job: homologous (forelimbs). Different ancestors,
same job: analogous (bird and insect wings, convergent evolution). Left over:
vestigial. Then molecular evidence: more shared sequence, more recent common
ancestor. Sort six examples on screen. Lock it in: homologous means shared
ancestry, analogous means shared job. Your turn.

### 8. Carbocation shifts (slot `oc.carbocations.rearrangement`, 90 s)

Why: a logged OChem miss and the pilot's OC-2 mechanism stepper exists (2026
set item ch-07). Record: the Tree's combined mechanism module at ch-07 and the
energy diagram. Outline: the trap is stopping at the first cation. Here is the
secret: every time you make a cation, look next door. Step the arrows:
protonate Markovnikov, secondary cation, hydride slides over, tertiary cation,
bromide attacks; then show the energy diagram dropping. Second example: a methyl
shift. Lock it in: a cation shifts when the move makes it more stable, then the
nucleophile adds. Your turn.

### 9. Reading a titration curve (slot `gc.acid-base.titration`, 90 s)

Why: acids and bases appear in the logged practice-test misses, the curve is a
change over volume, and the pilot's GC-3 curve is computed exactly. Record: the
GC engine's titration view and the pilot curve. Outline: the trap is treating
every equivalence point as pH 7. Here is the secret: the curve has three
landmarks. Drag the base in: the start (weak acid alone), the halfway point
(pH = pKa, mid-buffer), the equivalence point (above 7 for a weak acid, since
acetate is a base). Then estimate pKa from Ka without a calculator. Lock it in:
halfway, pH = pKa. Your turn.

### 10. The cell as a city (slot `bio.cell.organelles`, 90 s)

Why: 18 logged misses on organelles plus 9 on prokaryote versus eukaryote.
Record: the Cell hub's cell atlas (initCellAtlas) and the prokaryote and
eukaryote comparator. Outline: the trap is learning organelles as a list. Here
is the secret: follow one protein out of the cell. Nucleus (the gene),
ribosome on the rough ER (made and threaded in), Golgi (tagged and shipped),
vesicle, membrane. Then flip to the prokaryote: no nucleus, no ER, ribosomes
smaller, DNA in a nucleoid. Lock it in: follow the protein, and you have the
organelles in order. Your turn.

### 11. Turning words into an equation (slot `qr.word-problems.setup`, 90 s)

Why: 8 logged misses on translation and setup, and setup errors spread into
every QR word problem. Record: the QR engine's Word Problem Decoder. Outline:
the trap is starting to compute before naming the unknown. Here is the secret:
three moves, name it (let x be what is asked), translate phrase by phrase ("is"
becomes =, "of" becomes times, "more than" adds), then check the units. Decode
two problems on screen, one rate and one percent. Lock it in: name, translate,
check, then compute. Your turn.

### 12. Angle ranking: read the choices first (slot `pat.angles.close-pairs`, 75 s)

Why: the pilot's PAT-1 showed students argue the easy pair and guess the hard
ones; the fan view exists. Record: the Angle Ranking Studio (no clock) and the
pilot's equal-arm fan. Outline: the trap is judging by arm length. Here is the
secret: read the choices first; they tell you which close calls matter. Compare
only those pairs at the corner, ignore the arms, then lay all four on one
vertex with equal arms. Lock it in: the opening at the vertex, never the arms.
Your turn.

### 13. R and S when H lies flat (slot `oc.stereo.r-s`, 90 s)

Why: meso and Fischer misses are logged, and the pilot's OC-3 found a picture
case the usual rule does not cover. Record after the stereo pictures are fixed
(production-plan.md section 7): the Roots wedge-and-dash root and the
explainer's stereo cards. Outline: the trap is reading a flat H as if it were in
back. Here is the secret: get H onto the wedge or dash first, swapping it with
the group there, and count your flips. Do C2 (swap with the dash: one flip) and
C3 (swap with the wedge: two flips cancel). Then the meso check. Lock it in:
every swap flips the letter. Your turn.

### 14. Plant life: alternation of generations (slot `bio.diversity.plants`, 90 s)

Why: 15 logged misses on plant structure and physiology plus plant
reproduction. Record: the Diversity hub's plant life cycle and plant evolution
visualizers. Outline: the trap is mixing which generation is haploid. Here is
the secret: two generations take turns, the haploid gametophyte makes gametes
by mitosis, the diploid sporophyte makes spores by meiosis. Then the trend
across moss, fern, conifer, flowering plant: the sporophyte grows and the
gametophyte shrinks. Lock it in: spores by meiosis, gametes by mitosis. Your
turn.

### 15. Redox and the galvanic cell (slot `gc.electrochem.galvanic`, 90 s)

Why: oxidation states, redox and electrochemistry are logged misses together;
electron flow is a direction to watch. Record: the GC engine's galvanic cell
and Nernst views. Outline: the trap is mixing anode and cathode. Here is the
secret: an ox, red cat, oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode, and
electrons run from anode to cathode through the wire. Assign oxidation states
on one reaction, find who lost electrons, build the cell, add the salt bridge.
Lock it in: an ox, red cat; electrons flow anode to cathode. Your turn.

### 16. Domains and the tree of life (slot `bio.diversity.domains`, 90 s)

Why: bacteria and archaea 11, taxonomy 10, protists 6, viruses 5, fungi 4.
Record: the Diversity hub's bacteria visualizer and cladogram builder. Outline:
the trap is treating archaea as odd bacteria. Here is the secret: sort by three
features, nucleus, membrane lipids and cell wall. Build the three-domain tree,
place archaea closer to eukaryotes, then hang protists, fungi, plants and
animals off the eukaryote branch, with viruses outside the tree. Lock it in:
three domains, and archaea are cousins to us. Your turn.

### 17. Hydride reductions and Grignards (slot `oc.carbonyl.hydride-grignard`, 90 s)

Why: LiAlH4 and NaBH4 reductions are the most-logged organic miss (5), Grignard
next to it. Record: the Tree's carbonyl module. Outline: the trap is giving
NaBH4 the power of LiAlH4. Here is the secret: rank by strength, NaBH4 reduces
aldehydes and ketones, LiAlH4 also takes esters, acids and amides; a Grignard
adds carbon and needs a dry flask. Run four carbonyls through both reductants,
then one Grignard to a tertiary alcohol. Lock it in: borohydride for the easy
two, aluminum hydride for all. Your turn.

### 18. Atomic structure and the quantum address (slot `gc.atomic.quantum-numbers`, 90 s)

Why: atomic structure is the most-logged General Chemistry concept (8), with
nuclear chemistry next (5). Record: the GC engine's Atomic Structure and Quantum
Numbers views. Outline: the trap is treating quantum numbers as four facts to
memorize. Here is the secret: it is an address, shell (n), subshell (l), orbital
(ml), spin (ms), and each is limited by the one before. Fill an orbital diagram
for oxygen and read the last electron's address. Then the nucleus: protons set the element,
neutrons set the isotope, and a decay changes one or both. Lock it in: n limits
l, l limits ml. Your turn.

### 19. B cells and T cells (slot `bio.physiology.immune`, 90 s)

Why: adaptive immunity 10, plus lymphatic and blood misses. Record: the
Physiology hub's immune visualizer (initBodyImmune). Outline: the trap is mixing
which cell does what. Here is the secret: follow one invader. MHC I shows
inside trouble to cytotoxic T cells; MHC II shows outside trouble to helper T
cells, which switch on B cells to make antibodies. Then memory cells and the
faster second response. Lock it in: MHC I to killers, MHC II to helpers,
helpers turn on B cells. Your turn.

### 20. Keyholes: the silhouette (slot `pat.keyholes.silhouette`, 75 s)

Why: keyholes open the PAT, appear in the practice-test misses, and the
strategy is visual. Record: the Keyholes Studio (no clock), using the engine's
truth view. Outline: the trap is matching features instead of the outline.
Here is the secret: the object passes through the opening that matches its
outline exactly, size and proportions included. Trace the silhouette from the
viewing direction, then rule out choices by one feature at a time: an added
notch, a dropped feature, the right shape at another size. Lock it in: trace the
outline, then eliminate. Your turn.

### Also: the DAT screen tour (welcome, 90 s, not a subtopic slot)

The interface study suggests one short tour of the DAT-style player: Previous,
Next, Mark, Review with its three modes, Exhibit, Calculator, highlight and
cross-out. Recorded from the Ace Labs player (not from ADA or Prometric
material), with you on camera for the opening, as the decisions doc allows for
welcome videos. It belongs on the tutorial step before section 1.
