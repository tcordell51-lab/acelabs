The one move

Forget the acid. Judge what it leaves behind.

Acidity = how stable is the conjugate base?

An acid gives up a proton (H+). What’s left is the conjugate base, holding the leftover negative charge. A strong acid is one whose leftover anion is comfortable holding that charge.

The picture — hot potato

Treat the charge like a hot potato. The more hands it can be passed to — a bigger atom, resonance, a nearby group tugging — the comfier the anion, and the stronger the acid. Crammed on one atom = unstable = weaker acid. Spread out = stable = stronger acid.

Trap #1

Judge the conjugate base, never the neutral acid. Pull the proton, draw the anion, then ask how stable it is.

The move · the biggest lever, drawn

Resonance is the one to see once.

A carboxylate’s charge lives half on each of two identical oxygens — far comfier than a full charge on one. That’s why acetic acid (~5) crushes ethanol (~16) from the same O–H bond.

The pKa anchors — know where these live

Left = strong (loves to give up H+); right = barely acidic. Lower pKa = stronger acid.

Answer key · check after you commit

The six, decided.

Q1 → ACharge. Ammonium loses H+ to give neutral NH₃ — reaching neutral is a big stabilization (pKa ~9), so it beats every neutral N–H and C–H acid here.
Q2 → AAtom, across a row. The most electronegative atom holds the charge best: F > O > N > C. HF wins (3.2 vs 15.7 vs 38 vs ~50).
Q3 → AAtom, down a column. Size beats electronegativity going down; big iodide spreads the charge, so HI is strongest (~ -10). HF is actually the weakest halide — the classic trap.
Q4 → AResonance. Half a charge on each of two equivalent oxygens beats a full charge on one. Ethoxide has no such escape.
Q5 → AInduction. Same resonance in both, so R ties. The three Cl atoms drain charge off the anion through the bonds and space (the D/I check) — not resonance or mass.
Q6 → AOrbital. The sp carbon is 50% s-character, holding the pair close (~25). More s-character = more acidic: sp > sp² > sp³.
