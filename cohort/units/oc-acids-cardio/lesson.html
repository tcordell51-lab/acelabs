I do · the one idea

Forget the acid. Judge what it leaves behind.

Acidity = how stable is the ?

Tap the word that finishes the one-liner — that is the whole topic in a sentence.

An acid gives up a proton (H+). What’s left is the conjugate base, holding the leftover negative charge. A strong acid is simply one whose leftover anion is comfortable holding that charge. Everything else today is just a different reason that anion is more or less comfortable.

The whole topic, one line

stronger acid  ↔  more stable conjugate base  ↔  lower pKa.

I do · the picture

Treat the charge like a hot potato.

Same O–H bond, same element holding the charge — the only difference is how many hands the charge gets passed to. Ethoxide is stuck holding a whole negative on one oxygen; acetate splits it over two identical oxygens. Comfort of the anion is the entire game.

Say it backCharge crammed on one atom = = acid; spread over many = stable = stronger acid.
I do · the move

Take the proton off first. Then look.

Never rank acids by staring at the neutral molecule. Pull the proton, draw the anion, then ask how stable it is. Whatever stabilizes that conjugate base is exactly what made the acid stronger.

Trap #1

Judge the conjugate base, never the neutral acid. This one habit prevents most ranking mistakes.

I do · walk the letters
C
Charge — factor 1 of 5

Neutral beats a full charge on one atom.

First question every time: what charge is the conjugate base left holding? A base that comes out neutral is far comfier than one stuck with a whole negative on a single atom. That is exactly why a cationic acid — whose conjugate base is neutral — is such a strong acid.

Pull a proton off ammonium and you get neutral ammonia — no charge to hold, totally comfortable. Pull one off ammonia itself and you dump a full negative on one nitrogen (amide) — miserable. Same element, wildly different comfort, decided entirely by the charge left behind.

Say it backNH4+ lets go of H+ to leave a conjugate base — comfier than a full charge on one N, so the cation is the acid.
I do · walk the letters
A
Atom — factor 2 of 5

Which atom holds the charge? Two rules.

When the charge sits on different atoms, the periodic table decides. Two directions, two rules — and the column rule is the classic DAT trap because it runs opposite to the row.

Two opposite-looking rules, one letter. Across a row, the more electronegative atom is happier holding the charge (F > O > N > C). Down a column, the bigger atom wins because it spreads the charge over more volume — so HI is a far stronger acid than HF, the reverse of the row rule.

Two rules, one letterAcross a row the more atom wins; down a column, the atom wins (HI > HF).

R
Resonance — factor 3 of 5

R is the one to see once.

Resonance moves the needle further than anything else on this topic, so it’s worth drawing. A carboxylate’s charge doesn’t sit on one oxygen — it lives half on each of two identical oxygens:

watch the pair move

The same green pair — and the negative charge — never settles. It is shared, riding between two identical oxygens.

the truth

Two identical one-and-a-half bonds; half a charge on each O. Far comfier than a full charge on one — which is why acetic acid (pKa ~5) crushes ethanol (~16), over a hundred billion times more acidic from the same O–H bond.

Why it winsAcetate spreads its charge over equivalent oxygens — far more stable than ethoxide, which has none.

D/I
Dipole / Induction — factor 4 of 5

A neighbor can pull the charge away — or push it back.

Most DAT acidity items aren’t about the acidic atom itself — they’re about the groups around it. An electron-withdrawing group (EWG) drains density off the conjugate base, so the acid is stronger. An electron-donating group (EDG) pushes density in, so it’s weaker. Same move as always: judge the anion.

One line

EWG → pulls density away → stabler anion → stronger acid (lower pKa).  EDG → pushes density in → weaker acid.

EWG · strengthen the acid
strongest pull first
–NO₂> –CN> –CF₃> –C=O> –X (F>Cl>Br>I)
EDG · weaken the acid
strongest donor first
–O⁻> –NH₂> –OR / –OH> –NHCOR> –alkyl
Say it backAn EWG electron density away — a stable anion — a acid.
The flip side–CH3 is an — a acid.

Induction is additive. Each chlorine drains a little more density off the carboxylate through the σ bonds. Two mechanisms are in play everywhere: induction (through σ bonds, any EWG, fades fast with distance) and resonance (through π, only –NO₂/–C=O/–CN).

O
Orbital — factor 5 of 5

More s-character holds the pair tighter.

The last tiebreaker — it decides only when the charge sits on the same kind of atom in every option. An sp orbital is 50% s, so it hugs its electrons close to the nucleus and holds the charge happily; sp3 is only 25% s and holds loosely. So acidity runs sp > sp2 > sp3.

Same element (carbon), same neutral-looking C–H — the only difference is the orbital the pair lives in. The alkyne’s sp carbon (50% s) sits astronomically left of the alkane on the ladder: pKa ~25 vs ~50, the O in CARDIO doing its quiet work.

Last tiebreakerMore holds the pair tighter, so acidity runs sp > sp2 > sp3 — the terminal C–H is the acidic one.
