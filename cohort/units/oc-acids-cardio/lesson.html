Anchor · the one idea

Forget the acid. Judge what it leaves behind.

Acidity = how stable is the conjugate base?

An acid gives up a proton (H+). What’s left is the conjugate base, holding the leftover negative charge. A strong acid is simply one whose leftover anion is comfortable holding that charge. Everything else today is just a different reason that anion is more or less comfortable.

The whole topic, one line

stronger acid  ↔  more stable conjugate base  ↔  lower pKa.

Anchor · the picture

Treat the charge like a hot potato.

The more hands the charge can be passed to — a bigger atom, resonance, a nearby group tugging — the comfier the anion, and the more willingly the acid let its proton go. Comfort of the anion is the entire game.

Anchor · the move

Take the proton off first. Then look.

Never rank acids by staring at the neutral molecule. Pull the proton, draw the anion, then ask how stable it is. Whatever stabilizes that anion is exactly what made the acid stronger.

Trap #1

Judge the conjugate base, never the neutral acid. This one habit prevents most ranking mistakes.

The Move · the biggest lever, drawn

R is the one to see once.

Resonance moves the needle further than anything else on this topic, so it’s worth drawing. A carboxylate’s charge doesn’t sit on one oxygen — it lives half on each of two identical oxygens:

Two identical oxygens, half a charge on each — far comfier than a full charge on one. That single fact is why acetic acid (pKa ~5) crushes ethanol (~16), over a hundred billion times more acidic, from the same O–H bond.

The Move · anchors to know cold

A handful of pKa’s frame every ranking.

Lower pKa = stronger acid. Learn where these few reference points live and you can place almost anything between them.

Left = strong (loves to give up H+); right = barely acidic. Notice the alkyne (~25) sits astronomically left of the alkane (~50) — that gap is the O in CARDIO doing its work.
