Part 1 · The anchor

1The one move

An acid gives up a proton (H+). What’s left behind is the conjugate base, and it carries the leftover negative charge. The entire topic collapses to one line:

stronger acid  ↔  more stable conjugate base  ↔  lower pKaEvery rule below is just a different reason that leftover anion is more (or less) comfortable.

Picture it — the hot potato

An acid is only as willing to hand off its proton as its leftover anion is comfortable holding the negative charge. Treat that charge as a hot potato. The more hands it can pass to (resonance), the bigger the palm it lands in (a larger atom), or the more someone nearby keeps tugging it away (induction) — the comfier the anion, and the more happily the acid let the proton go.

Name the move

Never judge an acid by staring at the neutral molecule. Take the proton off first, then ask how stable the anion is. Whatever stabilizes that anion is what made the acid stronger.

Now you hold the pen

Deprotonate ethanol on paper: CH3CH2O−. Now deprotonate acetic acid: CH3COO−. Push the arrows on the second one. Which anion can spread its charge, and which is stuck holding all of it? That single difference is the whole reason acetic acid is over a hundred billion times more acidic than ethanol. Hold that question — it is the move you’ll run ten times below.

2The CARDIO ladder

There are six letters — really five ideas, since D and I are one idea with two doorways — and they spell CARDIO. Walk the two anions letter by letter; the first real difference between them is your answer — and your explanation.

C — Charge
Neutral, or charge spread thin, beats a full charge stacked on one atom. A cationic acid (its conjugate base comes out neutral) is much stronger than its neutral relative.
NH4+ (9) » NH3 (38)
A — Atom
Which atom holds the charge? Across a row the more electronegative atom wins; down a column the bigger atom wins.
row: HF > H2O > NH3 > CH4
column: HI > HBr > HCl > HF
R — Resonance
Spread the charge over several atoms and the anion relaxes. The single biggest lever on the DAT.
acetate (2 equal O): 4.8
vs ethanol 16
D — Dipole
A polar bond’s field steadies the charge through space. The through-space twin of induction.
the field-effect half of D/I
I — Induction
Electron-withdrawing groups drain charge through the sigma bonds. More groups and closer groups pull harder — and it fades fast with distance.
Cl3CCOOH 0.7 vs CH3COOH 4.8
O — Orbital
More s-character holds a lone pair tighter to the nucleus. The last tiebreaker, not the first.
sp (25) > sp2 (44) > sp3 (50)

D and I are one idea with two doorways — the through-space dipole/field and the through-bond induction — both just “pull the charge away.” The live engine writes them as a single D/I check, and so should you.

How to run it

Two acids to compare → deprotonate both → say the letters: Charge… Atom… Resonance… Dipole/Induction… Orbital. The moment one anion wins a check, stop — that’s your reason. Most questions are built so one factor clearly dominates; your job is to find which one.

3The pKa ladder — anchors worth knowing cold

Lower pKa = stronger acid. A handful of reference points frame every ranking question. Learn where these live and you can place almost anything between them.

Left = strong (loves to give up H+). Right = barely acidic at all. Notice a terminal alkyne (~25) is astronomically more acidic than an alkane (~50) — that gap is the O in CARDIO doing its work.

The highest-yield factor, drawn

Resonance is the biggest lever, so it’s worth seeing once. A carboxylate’s charge lives half on each of two identical oxygens:

The two oxygens are identical by resonance — half a charge on each is far comfier than a full charge on one. That is why RCOOH (~5) crushes ROH (~16).

5The habit that carries into the hammer

Say the letters out loud

When a ranking question shows up, draw each conjugate base and walk CARDIO out loud: “Charge… Atom… Resonance… Dipole/Induction… Orbital.” The first letter that separates them is your reason. If you can name the letter, you can defend the answer — and a defended answer is one you own.

The five traps waiting below

  1. Judge the conjugate base, never the neutral acid.
  2. Across a period electronegativity wins; down a group size wins (HI > HF).
  3. Resonance only counts if the charge can actually delocalize in the anion.
  4. Induction fades with distance — position, not just presence.
  5. Orbital (hybridization) is the last tiebreaker, not the first.
