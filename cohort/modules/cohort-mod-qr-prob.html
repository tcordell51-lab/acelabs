1The core ratio — favorable over total

Every probability is one fraction. Count the outcomes you want, count all the equally likely outcomes, and divide.

P(event) = favorable outcomes / total outcomesAlways a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). If your answer is above 1, you counted wrong.

Picture it

You are pulling a name out of a hat. The chance you pull yours is your slips over everybody’s slips. That is the whole idea — count the winners, count the room, divide.

Trap

The denominator is everybody, not everybody-else. If 3 of 10 marbles are blue, blue is 3/10 — not 3/7 (blue over non-blue).

2AND multiplies, OR adds

Two events chained together are either an AND (both must happen) or an OR (either one will do). That single word tells you the operation.

WordOperationThe rule
AND (both)MultiplyIndependent events: P(A) × P(B)
OR (either)AddMutually exclusive (can’t both happen): P(A) + P(B)
OR, but they can overlapAdd, then remove overlapP(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

Picture it

AND is a hallway of doors you must clear in a row — each door shrinks what gets through, so you multiply. OR is two separate lanes to the same finish line — either gets you there, so you add the lanes. (Just don’t count the spot where the lanes cross twice.)

Trap

Multiplying two probabilities makes the number smaller; adding makes it bigger. If an “AND” answer came out larger than the pieces, you added by mistake.

3Independent vs dependent — does the pool reset or shrink?

When you draw more than once, ask one question: after the first pick, is the pool the same or smaller?

SetupTypeWhat happens to the denominator
With replacement (put it back)IndependentPool resets — same probability every draw
Without replacement (keep it)DependentPool shrinks — top and bottom drop by one each draw
3 white left of 9 total → next white = 3/9, not 4/10Start with 4 white of 10; remove one white and both the whites remaining and the total remaining go down.

Picture it

A deck you deal from never heals: every card you take is gone. That is “without replacement” — the pool shrinks under your hand. Rolling a die is the opposite; the die has no memory, so the pool resets every roll.

Trap

The classic slip: shrinking the numerator to 3 but leaving the denominator at 10. Both move together — 4/10 × 3/9.

4The complement trick — “at least one”

When a problem says “at least one,” stop. Adding up one, two, three… is slow and error-prone. Flip it: find the single case you don’t want and subtract from 1.

P(at least one) = 1 − P(none)“None” is one clean case to compute; “at least one” is everything else.

Picture it

“At least one head in three flips” has a messy front door (one head, or two, or three). The back door is tiny: the only way to fail is all tails. Compute that one case, subtract from 1, done.

Trap

Finish the flip. P(none) is the case you rule out — you still owe the “1 − ” step. Handing in P(none) is the most common miss on these.

5When to count — principle, permutation, combination

Some problems ask “how many ways?” before any probability. Three tools, and one question sorts them: does order matter?

ToolUse it whenFormulaTrigger words
Counting principleIndependent choices at each stagemultiply the counts“one of each”, menus, outfits
PermutationOrder mattersnPr = n! / (n − r)!arrange, rank, seat, 1st/2nd/3rd, offices
CombinationOrder does not matternCr = n! / [ r!(n − r)! ]choose, group, committee, handful, team

Picture it

Ask: if I swap two of my picks, is it a different result? Seating people in numbered chairs — yes, swapping changes it, that is a permutation. Grabbing a handful of people for a committee — no, same group either way, that is a combination. Same numbers, but a permutation is always the bigger count because it counts every rearrangement.

The link

A combination is just a permutation with the duplicate orderings divided out: nCr = nPr / r!. Pick 3 of 6 courses = 6P3 / 3! = 120 / 6 = 20.

The five traps

  1. Denominator is everybody, not everybody-else.
  2. AND is multiply, OR is add — a bigger “AND” answer means you added.
  3. Without replacement, shrink both numerator and denominator.
  4. “At least one” means finish the 1 −; don’t hand in P(none).
  5. Order matters → permutation (bigger); order-free → combination (smaller). Never repeat a person unless the problem allows it.
