Two decisions, run the same way every time

Most of the organic section is two questions wearing different clothes. What is the relationship between these two drawings, and which pathway does this reaction take. Neither one is a memory problem. Both are decision trees, and once you run them in order you stop guessing.

Decision one

What is the relationship between these two molecules?

Start with the same definition every time. A chiral center is a carbon with four different groups on it. Find them, assign R or S at each one, and then the relationship falls out of the comparison.

The ladder

Compare the two molecules center by center. There are only four answers, and which one you get depends entirely on how many centers changed.

Identicalnothing changed
Same R or S at every center. Same molecule, drawn twice.
Enantiomerseverything changed
Every center flipped. All the R's became S and all the S's became R. Mirror images that cannot be superimposed.
Diastereomerssome changed
At least one center flipped and at least one stayed put. This is the in-between answer, and it is the most common one.
Mesochanged, but symmetric
The molecule has chiral centers but an internal mirror plane cancels them out. Optically inactive. A meso compound is achiral even though it is full of stereocenters.

2,3-dibromobutane, all three at once

Same molecular formula, same connectivity, three different molecules. This one example holds the whole ladder.

The walk-around check

The hardest call is enantiomer versus identical. When the molecule is symmetric and every center flipped, you have not made a mirror image, you have made the same thing back. Walk around it in your head. If it looks the same from the back, it is identical.

How many stereoisomers exist

2 to the n, where n is the number of stereocenters. Two centers gives four, three gives eight.

Then correct it for symmetry. In 2,3-dibromobutane n is 2, so 2 to the 2 predicts four. But two of those four are the same meso compound, so the real answer is three. Any time a molecule can be meso, the count comes down.

A double bond counts toward n as well, because it has an E option and a Z option just like a center has an R option and an S option.

The decision tree, in order

Run these five questions top to bottom. Stop at the first one that answers you.

  1. Same molecular formula? If no, they are not isomers at all. Done.
  2. Same connectivity? If no, they are constitutional isomers, also called structural isomers. Check that the substituents come off the same numbered carbons before you decide this.
  3. Any stereocenters or any E and Z double bonds? If no, they are identical. Done.
  4. Same at every center? If yes, identical. If no, keep going.
  5. Opposite at every center? If yes, enantiomers. If no, diastereomers.

E and Z

Cis is Z, same side. Trans is E, opposite sides. The priority rules are the ones you already know from R and S: higher atomic number wins, and you walk outward until they differ.

Degrees of unsaturation

Two ways to get the same number, and you should be able to run both.

From the formula

(2C + 2 + N minus H minus X) divided by 2, where C is carbons, N is nitrogens, H is hydrogens and X is halogens.

From the drawing

Count the pi bonds, count the rings, add them. A benzene ring is four: three pi bonds plus the ring itself.

Decision two

SN1, SN2, E1 or E2?

Three questions, always in this order. The substrate narrows it, the reagent usually finishes it, and the solvent only gets a vote when the substrate left the door open.

  1. Look at the substrate first. This is the only question that can eliminate two answers at once.
    • Methyl or primary: SN2 or E2 only. A primary carbocation is too unstable to form, so both of the 1 pathways are off the table.
    • Tertiary: SN1, E1 or E2 only. Far too crowded for a backside attack, so SN2 is out.
    • Secondary: all four are still live. Keep going.
  2. Look at the reagent.
    • Strong bulky base, meaning tBuOK, LDA or NaH: E2, whatever the substrate is.
    • Non-basic nucleophile, meaning a halide or a sulfur: substitution only, no elimination.
    • Strong small anionic nucleophile: pushes toward SN2.
    • Weak neutral nucleophile, often the solvent itself: pushes toward SN1 or E1.
  3. Look at the solvent, but only if the substrate was secondary. On a primary or tertiary substrate the solvent is not the deciding vote.
    • Protic, meaning water, an alcohol or ammonia: SN1 and E1. Protic solvents have an O-H or N-H to donate, and that stabilizes the carbocation.
    • Polar aprotic, meaning DMSO, THF or acetone: SN2 and E2. Nothing to donate, so the nucleophile stays naked and reactive.

SN1 against SN2, side by side

SN1SN2
StepsTwo. The leaving group goes first and makes a carbocation, then the nucleophile attacks it.One. The nucleophile attacks and the leaving group leaves in the same motion.
IntermediateA carbocation, flat and open on both faces.None. A transition state, never an intermediate.

Which alkene you get

Once you have decided elimination, there is a second question: which hydrogen comes off.

An ordinary base takes the hydrogen that gives the more substituted alkene, because that alkene is more stable. That is the Zaitsev product, and it is the default.

tBuOK is the exception, and it is the one they test. It is too bulky to reach the crowded interior hydrogen, so it takes the one on the outside and gives the less substituted alkene. That is the Hofmann product. The way to remember which is which: tBuOK is T-Boffman.

One thing you can stop worrying about

SN1 against E1 on a tertiary substrate in a protic solvent with a weak nucleophile comes down to temperature, and the DAT almost never makes you call it. If a question hands you that exact setup, heat favors elimination. Then move on.
