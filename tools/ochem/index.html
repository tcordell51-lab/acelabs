CIP priority rules
1. Higher atomic number = higher priority (I > Br > Cl > F > O > N > C > H).
2. If tied, move to next atom out; compare the sets.
3. Double/triple bonds count atoms twice/thrice (phantom atoms).

Spectroscopy

📊 IR + NMR Trainer

The DAT spec section rewards pattern recognition. Master IR by the 5 key peaks; master NMR by running the 4-step strategy on every problem. Practice below.

The universal spec-solving algorithm (run in this order every time)
1. Count degrees of unsaturation first if you have the molecular formula: (2C + 2 + N − H − X) / 2. Each degree = 1 ring or 1 π bond.
2. Scan the IR for the 5 key peaks to identify the functional group (below).
3. Run the NMR 4-step — count peaks, integration, splitting, chemical shift.
4. Propose a structure that matches IR + NMR + formula. Sanity-check with degrees of unsaturation.
5. Eliminate answer choices that violate any rule. Pick what's left.
The IR Decision Flow
Is there a peak around 1700 cm⁻¹?
YES — you have a C=O. Now narrow it:
  • + broad OH overlapping C-H (2500-3300) = carboxylic acid
  • + two C-H stretches at 2720 & 2820 = aldehyde (the "aldehyde fingerprint")
  • + sharp N-H above 3300 = amide
  • + strong C-O at 1100-1300 = ester
  • none of the above = ketone
NO — no C=O. Look for:
  • Broad O-H at 3200-3600 = alcohol
  • Sharp N-H (1 or 2 peaks at 3300-3500) = amine (2 peaks = 1°, 1 peak = 2°)
  • Sharp C-H at ~3300 + weak C≡C at 2100-2150 = terminal alkyne
  • Medium-strong sharp peak at 2200-2260 = nitrile (C≡N)
  • C-H above 3000 (sp²) + peaks at 1500 & 1600 = aromatic ring
  • Only C-H below 3000 (sp³) + nothing else obvious = alkane / ether
The NMR Decision Flow
First, count peaks = number of chemical environments.
If n peaks = n unique H environments. Fewer peaks = more symmetry. 1 singlet for an unsymmetric molecule tells you all H's are equivalent (possibly by symmetry or by being chemically identical).
Then read integration.
Integer ratios. (3:2:3) = CH₃ : CH₂ : CH₃. (9:1) = 9 equivalent H (t-butyl) : something else.
Then splitting (n+1 rule).
singlet = 0 neighbors · doublet = 1 · triplet = 2 · quartet = 3 · septet = 6. Classic patterns: triplet + quartet = ethyl. septet + doublet = isopropyl. Large singlet at ~1.3 (9H) = t-butyl.
Finally chemical shift to locate each environment.
0-1.5 alkyl · 1.5-2.5 α to C=O or allylic · 2.5-3.5 next to EN atom · 3.5-4.5 on O · 4.5-6.5 vinyl · 6.5-8 aromatic · 9-10 aldehyde · 10-13 acid OH.
Mnemonic: The "4 Zones" of IR — top to bottom
Zone 1 (3200-3600) — X-H stretches. Broad round = O-H alcohol; BROADER reaching 2500 = carboxylic acid; 2 sharp = 1° amine; 1 sharp = 2° amine; sharp 3300 = terminal alkyne ≡C-H.
Zone 2 (2800-3100) — C-H stretches. Above 3000 = sp² (alkene/aromatic); below 3000 = sp³. Aldehyde gives twin small peaks at 2720 & 2820.
Zone 3 (2100-2260) — Triple bonds. Nitrile C≡N ≈ 2245 (strong); alkyne C≡C ≈ 2120 (weak).
Zone 4 (1650-1750) — C=O. Ester 1740 · Aldehyde 1725 · Ketone 1715 · Acid 1710 · Amide 1680. Always check here first.

Diagnostic priority
Always check for C=O around 1700 cm⁻¹ first — if it's there, narrow to aldehyde/ketone/acid/ester/amide using other peaks. If not, look for broad OH (3200-3600) or sharp N-H (3300-3500). Then triple bonds (2100-2260). C-H below 3000 = sp³; above = sp² or sp.
The 4-step NMR strategy
1
Count peaks — each signal = one chemical environment. More peaks = less symmetry.
2
Read integration — peak area = proton ratio. (3, 2, 1) = CH₃, CH₂, CH, etc.
3
Count splitting (n+1 rule) — singlet = 0 neighbors, doublet = 1, triplet = 2, quartet = 3, etc.
4
Read chemical shift — 0-1.5 sp³ alkyl · 2-3 next to C=O or ring · 3-4.5 attached to O/N · 4-6 vinyl · 6-8 aromatic · 9-10 aldehyde · 10-12 acid OH.

Real ¹H and ¹³C NMR Spectra — the patterns the DAT tests
How to read a real NMR
Left side = deshielded (high ppm, downfield). Right side = shielded (low ppm, upfield). TMS (tetramethylsilane, Si(CH₃)₄) is the 0-ppm reference — it's super-shielded because Si is electropositive, so every real proton in your sample appears to its left. Integration ratios are shown as numbers above each ¹H peak. For ¹³C, each unique carbon environment gives ONE line (broadband-decoupled — no splitting). DAT trick: count lines in ¹³C to count unique carbons; symmetry reduces the count.

Why the alkene is the center of the universe
  • Alkenes are the most common synthetic "branch point" — they can be made from alkyl halides (E2), alcohols (dehydration), and alkynes (Lindlar/Na,NH₃).
  • From an alkene you can reach almost any other functional group in ONE step: alkane (H₂), alkyl halide (HX), alcohol (H₂O), epoxide (mCPBA), diol (OsO₄), carbonyl (O₃).
  • If a DAT roadmap question looks impossibly long, check whether you can go through an alkene — it's usually the shortest path.

Hub 01

Alkanes — the inert starting material

Alkanes are sp³ and saturated. They're famously unreactive — the DAT essentially tests two reactions: free-radical halogenation and combustion. Their real job on the test is as the product of hydrogenation, not as a reactant.

Plate II — The Alkane Hub
Starting material: propane. Only two reactions radiate out — everything else enters the alkane (via hydrogenation).
1
Free-Radical Halogenation
Radical
▾
Under UV light or heat, Cl₂ or Br₂ replaces an alkane H with a halogen. Selectivity is the whole story: Br₂ is highly selective (3° >> 2° > 1°); Cl₂ is much less selective.
R–H + X₂  →hν  R–X + HX
⚡ Step Mode
Radical Halogenation · Full Chain MechanismInitiation → Propagation → Termination · Br₂ with 2-methylpropane · watch the radical count
1/5
Fishhook vs regular arrows
The single-barbed fishhook arrow means "one electron moves." Contrast with the two-barbed arrow used in polar mechanisms (SN, EAS, carbonyl addition) which means "a pair of electrons moves." This is the only DAT mechanism where you exclusively see fishhooks.
Regioselectivity
3° > 2° > 1° (most stable radical wins)
Stereochem
Planar radical → racemic at new stereocenter
Br vs Cl
Br = very selective; Cl = sloppy (kinetic)
Hammond's Postulate
For endothermic steps (Br•), the transition state looks like the product → radical stability strongly controls the outcome. For exothermic steps (Cl•), the TS looks like the reactant → less selectivity.
DAT Trap
Benzylic and allylic positions are far more reactive than normal 3°. If you see a benzyl CH with Br₂/hν, that's where the halogen lands — resonance stabilizes that radical.
2
Combustion
Redox
▾
Complete oxidation of an alkane to CO₂ and H₂O with excess O₂. Rare on DAT as a reaction, common on the general chemistry balancing questions.
CnH2n+2 + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → n CO₂ + (n+1) H₂O
Balancing shortcut
Balance C first, then H, then O last. For C₅H₁₂: 5 CO₂ needs 10 O; 6 H₂O needs 6 O → total 16 O → 8 O₂.
Alkane takeaways
  • Only two alkane reactions you need: radical halogenation and combustion.
  • Radical stability: benzylic ≈ allylic > 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl.
  • Br is selective (gives the most stable product); Cl is not (product distribution close to statistical).
  • Alkanes matter most as products of hydrogenation — when you see H₂/Pt, you're landing on this hub.

12-methylbutane is treated with Br₂ and hν (1 equiv). The major monobromination product is:
Explanation
B is correct. Br is highly selective (3° >> 2° > 1°). The single 3° H at C2 gets abstracted fastest because the resulting 3° radical is most stable. Cl₂ would give a sloppy mix; Br₂ picks the winner.

Explanation
D is correct. Benzylic C-H bonds are abstracted preferentially because the resulting benzylic radical is resonance-stabilized into the ring (more stable than any plain 1°/2°/3° radical). Ring bromination needs Br₂/FeBr₃ (an entirely different, electrophilic mechanism).
3The rate-determining propagation step of Br₂/hν with an alkane is:
Explanation
A is correct. H abstraction is the slow (rate-determining) propagation step — its transition state closely resembles the alkyl radical (Hammond's postulate), which is why radical stability controls selectivity so strongly. The second propagation (R• + Br-Br) is fast and non-selective.
4Complete combustion of propane (C₃H₈) requires how many O₂ molecules per propane?
Explanation
C is correct. C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O. Check: 3 C needs 3 O₂ (→ 3 CO₂); 8 H needs 2 O₂ (→ 4 H₂O). Total = 5 O₂. Formula for any CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: need (3n+1)/2 O₂.
5Why does Cl₂/hν give a messy distribution of monochlorination products but Br₂/hν gives a clean one?
Explanation
B is correct. Hammond's postulate: endothermic steps have TS resembling products → radical stability matters a lot → high selectivity. Cl's H-abstraction is exothermic → TS resembles reactant → little selectivity. This is a favorite DAT "why" question.
Hub 02 · Central

Alkenes — the center of the universe

The π bond is a nucleophile looking for an electrophile. Almost every reaction here is electrophilic addition: something electrophilic attacks the π bond first, then a nucleophile closes in. Master regioselectivity (Markovnikov vs anti-Markovnikov) and stereoselectivity (syn vs anti) and you own this hub.

💡 Pattern shortcuts for this hub:
Jump to: 🗺️ Atlas 🧩 3-Bucket Classifier ⚗️ 11 Reactions ⚡ Hydroboration Mechanism 📋 Regio/Stereo Summary
Plate III — The Alkene Hub
Starting material: propene (CH₃–CH=CH₂). Ten branches show the actual product of each reagent. Stereo wedges are drawn where relevant.
Foundational — Markovnikov's Rule
In acid-catalyzed addition of HX or H₂O, the H adds to the carbon with more H's (the less substituted carbon) so that the resulting carbocation lands on the more substituted carbon. In short: "the rich get richer" — the carbon that already has H's gets another H, and X/OH lands on the more substituted carbon. Driven by carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1°.
The 3-Bucket Mechanism Classifier
Bucket #1
Carbocation
  • Follows Markovnikov
  • Rearrangements YES
  • Stereo: racemic (planar cation)
Reactions in this bucket:
HX H₃O⁺ / H₂SO₄
If you see an open carbocation in the mechanism → this bucket.
Bucket #2
3-Membered Ring
  • Can be Markov or anti-Markov
  • Rearrangements NO
  • Stereo: anti (backside attack)
Reactions in this bucket:
X₂ X₂ / H₂O Hg(OAc)₂ / NaBH₄ mCPBA
If the intermediate is a 3-membered cyclic cation → this bucket.
Bucket #3
Concerted
  • Often anti-Markov (or no regio)
  • Rearrangements NO
  • Stereo: syn (same-face)
Reactions in this bucket:
H₂ / Pt BH₃ → H₂O₂/OH⁻ OsO₄ (syn diol)
If both new atoms add to the same face in one step → this bucket.
How to use the 3-Bucket Classifier
Instead of memorizing 11 alkene reactions as separate facts, classify each by its mechanism type. When the DAT shows you an unfamiliar reagent, you ask: "which bucket does this fall into?" — and the regiochemistry, stereochemistry, and rearrangement risk all follow automatically. HBr/ROOR is the one outlier (radical mechanism, anti-Markov) that doesn't fit the three buckets.
1
Hydrohalogenation (HX addition)
Markov
▾
HCl, HBr, or HI adds across the π bond. The halogen lands on the more substituted carbon. Goes through an open carbocation, so rearrangements are possible (hydride or methyl shifts if a more stable cation is adjacent).
R₂C=CHR' + H–X  →  R₂C(X)–CH₂R'
Mechanism — 2 steps
1
π bond attacks H of HX
The alkene's π electrons form a new C–H bond on the less substituted carbon. The H–X bond breaks heterolytically, releasing X⁻. Net: one sp² carbon becomes sp³ with a new H; the other becomes a carbocation on the more substituted carbon.
2
X⁻ attacks cation
X⁻ attacks the carbocation from either face → racemic at that carbon. Bond formation gives the final alkyl halide.
Regio
Markovnikov (X on more substituted C)
Stereo
Not stereospecific → racemic
Rearr.
YES — if a more stable cation is adjacent
Trap — Rearrangement
If an adjacent carbon has a H or methyl that can shift to convert a 2° cation into a 3° cation, it will. On an exam, any 2° cation adjacent to a quaternary center is a rearrangement red flag.
2
HBr + Peroxides (anti-Markov)
Radical
▾
Only HBr (not HCl, not HI) flips to anti-Markovnikov with peroxides. Mechanism is radical, not cation, so there's no rearrangement.
R₂C=CH₂ + HBr / ROOR  →  R₂CH–CH₂Br
Mechanism — radical chain
1
Initiation
Peroxide homolysis → 2 RO•. RO• + HBr → ROH + Br•.
2
Propagation
Br• adds to the less substituted carbon (so the resulting carbon radical is the more stable tertiary/secondary one). That alkyl radical abstracts H from HBr → final product + new Br•.
Why only HBr?
HCl: C–Cl BDE too high → Cl• can't abstract H efficiently. HI: H–I too weak → I• won't add to the alkene. Only HBr has the right energy match for both propagation steps.
3
Acid-Catalyzed Hydration (H₂O/H⁺)
Markov
▾
H₂O adds across the π bond with H₂SO₄ catalyst. Gives the Markovnikov alcohol. Same cation mechanism as HX → rearrangements possible.
R₂C=CHR' + H₂O / H⁺  →  R₂C(OH)–CH₂R'
1
Protonation
π bond grabs H⁺ → carbocation on more substituted C.
2
Water attacks cation
H₂O (weak nucleophile, but in excess) attacks the cation → oxocarbenium (R–OH₂⁺).
3
Deprotonation
A second water removes H⁺ → neutral alcohol. Regenerates catalyst.
4
Oxymercuration–Demercuration
Markov · no rearr.
▾
Hg(OAc)₂ / H₂O, then NaBH₄. Gives the same Markovnikov alcohol as H₂O/H⁺ but without rearrangement because the intermediate is a bridged mercurinium ion, not an open cation.
R₂C=CHR'  →1. Hg(OAc)₂/H₂O · 2. NaBH₄  R₂C(OH)–CH₂R'
1
Mercurinium formation
Hg²⁺ (from Hg(OAc)₂) bridges across the π bond → 3-membered mercurinium ion. Partial positive charge is larger on the more substituted carbon.
2
Water opens the ring
H₂O attacks the more substituted carbon (Markov) from the side opposite Hg → anti addition.
3
NaBH₄ demercuration
Replaces –HgOAc with –H. Net: Markovnikov alcohol, no rearrangement.
When to pick this over H₂O/H⁺
Whenever the problem hints that rearrangement would give a better carbocation. Oxymercuration is your "clean Markov" reagent.
5
Hydroboration–Oxidation
anti-Markov · syn
▾
BH₃·THF adds across the alkene with B (electrophile) on the less hindered carbon. Oxidation with H₂O₂/NaOH replaces B with OH with retention → anti-Markovnikov alcohol, syn addition, no rearrangement.
R₂C=CHR'  →1. BH₃/THF · 2. H₂O₂/OH⁻  R₂CH–CH(OH)R'

6
Catalytic Hydrogenation
syn
▾
H₂ over Pt, Pd/C, or Ni delivers both H's from the same face of the alkene → syn addition. Reduces alkene to alkane. Can be poisoned (Lindlar) to stop at cis-alkene for alkynes.
R₂C=CHR' + H₂ / Pt  →  R₂CH–CH₂R'
Selectivity
Carbonyls and aromatic rings are not reduced by standard H₂/Pt (you need more aggressive conditions). Useful for selectively hydrogenating alkenes in a polyfunctional molecule.
7
Halogenation (X₂)
anti
▾
Br₂ or Cl₂ in an inert solvent (CCl₄, CH₂Cl₂) gives a vicinal dihalide with anti stereochemistry via a bridged halonium intermediate.
R₂C=CHR' + Br₂  →  R₂CBr–CHBrR'
1
Halonium ion
π bond attacks Br₂ → 3-membered cyclic bromonium ion + Br⁻.
2
Backside Br⁻ attack
Br⁻ attacks from the opposite face → anti vicinal dihalide. On a cyclic alkene, this gives a trans-dihalide.
Visual test
Red-brown Br₂ in CCl₄ decolorizes on mixing with an alkene. Classic qualitative test for unsaturation.
8
Halohydrin Formation (X₂/H₂O)
Markov · anti
▾
Same halonium intermediate, but water (present in excess) attacks instead of X⁻. OH ends up on the more substituted carbon, X on the less substituted, anti.
R₂C=CH₂ + Br₂ / H₂O  →  R₂C(OH)–CH₂Br
Why OH lands on the more substituted C
The bromonium ion is asymmetric: the more-substituted carbon carries more + charge, so H₂O (nucleophile) attacks there. Still backside → anti.
9
Epoxidation (mCPBA)
syn
▾
A peroxy acid (mCPBA most common) transfers one oxygen to the alkene in a concerted, syn step → epoxide. Retains alkene geometry (cis-alkene → cis-epoxide).
R₂C=CHR' + mCPBA  →  epoxide (R₂C–CHR' bridged by O)
Follow-up
Open with H₃O⁺ (acid) → anti-Markov diol, trans. Or open with nucleophile/base → anti diol again, but nucleophile attacks the less hindered carbon.
10
Syn Dihydroxylation (OsO₄ or cold KMnO₄)
syn diol
▾
OsO₄ (or cold, dilute KMnO₄/OH⁻) adds two OH groups to the same face of the alkene → syn (cis) diol.
R₂C=CHR'  →OsO₄ then NaHSO₃  R₂C(OH)–CH(OH)R' (cis)
Cold vs hot KMnO₄

The Diels-Alder Reaction — the 3-arrow concerted cycloaddition
Three curved arrows, one concerted step
All three arrows move at the same time around the 6-atom ring being formed. No intermediate. No stopping. 2 new σ bonds form · 2 π bonds break · 1 π bond shifts.
Alkene dienophile vs. Alkyne dienophile
Alkene dienophile
Dienophile has 1 π bond. All 3 of the diene's π electrons get used up on forming the ring.
Bond bookkeeping: 3 π in → 1 π + 2 σ out. Net: lose 2 π, gain 2 σ. Energetically favorable (σ bonds are stronger).
Alkyne dienophile
Dienophile has 2 π bonds. One is used in the reaction; the other survives in the product.
Bond bookkeeping: 4 π in (2 from diene + 2 from alkyne) → 2 π + 2 σ out. The alkyne's "second" π doesn't participate — it just sits there through the whole reaction.
The single test-day question to drill
DAT pattern: "Diene + X → ?" where X is either alkene or alkyne. Count double bonds in the product: alkene dienophile ⇒ 1 C=C (cyclohexene); alkyne dienophile ⇒ 2 C=C's (1,3-cyclohexadiene). That's the whole question. You don't need to draw the 3 arrows under time pressure — count π bonds in and π bonds out.
The 4 rules that cover every DAT Diels-Alder question
1. Diene must be s-cis. Cyclic dienes (cyclopentadiene, furan) are locked s-cis → super-reactive. Acyclic dienes sit s-trans at rest and have to rotate; steric blockers that prevent rotation kill the reaction.
2. Dienophile usually has an EWG (C=O, CN, NO₂). EWG lowers its LUMO → faster. Ethylene reacts slowly; maleic anhydride is the standard fast dienophile.
3. Stereospecific — cis stays cis, trans stays trans. Substituents on a cis dienophile end up cis on the ring; trans stays trans. The reaction is syn/suprafacial on both partners — no atoms flip sides during the single concerted step.
4. Endo preference. When there's an EWG on the dienophile, it prefers the endo (pointing under the new ring) position over exo. Kinetic product, not thermodynamic.
Chair Conformations — Axial vs. Equatorial
The one rule you need
Big groups prefer equatorial. When a substituent is axial, it crashes into the two other axial H's on the same side of the ring (1,3-diaxial interaction). Equatorial position points "out" and avoids this clash. For a ring flip, the winning chair has the largest group equatorial.
Cis/trans in chairs
On a cyclohexane, cis = both substituents on the same side of the ring (one up, one up — or one down, one down); trans = opposite sides. After a ring flip, every axial becomes equatorial and vice versa — but cis/trans never changes. Memorize: 1,4-cis has 1 ax + 1 eq; 1,4-trans has both eq (or both ax). Trans-1,4-di-tert-butylcyclohexane is the stablest configuration because both t-Bu's can be equatorial at once.

Alkynes — double trouble

Alkynes are two π bonds stacked. Most of their reactions are the alkene reactions done twice (or stopped halfway). The exam-unique twists are terminal alkyne acidity (pKa ≈ 25) and the tautomerization of enols to ketones after hydration.

Hub 06

Ethers & Epoxides — the strained ring twist

Ethers are boring — they're inert under most conditions. Epoxides are the opposite: strained three-membered rings open readily with both nucleophiles and electrophiles. The direction of attack flips between acid and base — master that flip and you've mastered this hub.

Plate VII — Ether & Epoxide Reactions
Ethers are mostly inert. Epoxides split into two reactions depending on conditions — this flip is the hub's centerpiece.
1
Ether Cleavage (HI, HBr)
Acidic
▾
Only strong HX (HI > HBr) cleaves ethers. Mechanism: protonate O, then I⁻ (great nucleophile) attacks. 1° goes SN2 at the less substituted carbon. 3° goes SN1, breaking on the more substituted side.
R–O–R' + 2 HI → R–I + R'–I + H₂O (excess HI)
or R–O–R' + HI → R–I + R'–OH (1 eq)
2
Epoxide — Acidic Ring Opening
Markov · anti
▾
Protonate the O → protonated epoxide (O⁺). Nucleophile attacks the more substituted C because the TS has cation-like character there. Still backside → anti.
epoxide + H₃O⁺  →  anti diol (Nu/OH on more substituted C)
1
Protonation
H⁺ protonates the oxygen → positively charged three-membered ring.
2
Nu attacks more substituted C
The more substituted C has more positive character (better cation). H₂O (or other Nu) attacks it backside. Ring opens.
3
Deprotonate
Remove H⁺ → neutral anti diol.
3
Epoxide — Basic (Nu⁻) Ring Opening
anti-Markov · anti
▾
Nucleophile (RO⁻, CN⁻, R–MgX, RLi, OH⁻) attacks the less substituted C directly (classic SN2 sterics) → anti product. Works because of ring strain (epoxides are unique among ethers).
epoxide + Nu⁻ → anti product, Nu on less substituted C, OH on other
Grignard + epoxide
Hugely useful: R–MgX + ethylene oxide → R–CH₂–CH₂–OH. Adds a two-carbon hydroxyethyl unit to any Grignard. Exam favorite.
Ether/epoxide takeaways
  • Ethers are inert except under harsh HX (HI, HBr) which cleaves them.
  • Epoxides open readily because of ring strain. Acid → Markov. Base/Nu → anti-Markov. Both give anti (backside).
  • Grignard + ethylene oxide is the standard way to tack two carbons and an OH onto a Grignard.

Hub 07

Aromatic — Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

Benzene is a nucleophile (electron-rich π system), but it refuses to add because that would destroy aromaticity. Instead, it substitutes: electrophile attacks → arenium ion → loses H⁺ → aromatic again. Five core reactions, plus directing effects that decide where a second group lands.

💡 Pattern shortcuts for this hub:
Plate VIII — The EAS Hub
Starting material: benzene. All 5 core reactions follow the same 3-step arenium ion mechanism — only the E⁺ changes.
Universal EAS mechanism (all 5 reactions)
Step 1: Generate the electrophile E⁺ (with the appropriate Lewis acid catalyst if needed).
Step 2: π system of benzene attacks E⁺ → arenium ion (sp³ carbon with + charge delocalized over the other 5 ring carbons). Aromaticity temporarily broken.
Step 3: A base (usually the leaving group from step 1) removes the H from the sp³ carbon → aromaticity restored. Net: Ar–H → Ar–E.
1
Halogenation (X₂/FeX₃)
EAS
▾
Br₂/FeBr₃ or Cl₂/FeCl₃. Lewis acid polarizes X–X to make a strong electrophile. I₂ too weak; F₂ too reactive.
Ar–H + Br₂ / FeBr₃ → Ar–Br + HBr
2
Nitration (HNO₃/H₂SO₄)
EAS · → NH₂
▾
Electrophile is the nitronium ion (⁺NO₂). Gives Ar–NO₂ which is easily reduced to Ar–NH₂ by Sn/HCl, Zn/HCl, Fe/HCl, or H₂/Pd. Key synthesis route to anilines.
Ar–H + ⁺NO₂ → Ar–NO₂   →Sn/HCl or H₂/Pd   Ar–NH₂
⚡ Step Mode
EAS — Nitration of BenzeneTemplate for ALL electrophilic aromatic substitution · 4 steps
1/4
This mechanism applies to all 5 EAS reactions
Halogenation, sulfonation, Friedel-Crafts alkylation and acylation all follow this same three-act template: (1) generate E⁺, (2) π attacks E⁺ → arenium, (3) base removes H⁺ → rearomatize. Only the E⁺ identity changes (Br⁺, SO₃, R⁺, or RC≡O⁺ respectively).
3
Sulfonation (SO₃/H₂SO₄)
Reversible
▾
Fuming H₂SO₄ (SO₃ dissolved in H₂SO₄). Electrophile is SO₃. Reversible: hot dilute H₂SO₄ removes the SO₃H group. Used as a blocking group to force para substitution, then removed.
4
Friedel-Crafts Alkylation (R-X/AlCl₃)
Watch rearrangement!
▾
AlCl₃ pulls X⁻ off R–X → carbocation E⁺. Rearrangements are common (just like any free cation). Also fails on strongly deactivated rings (e.g., nitrobenzene won't alkylate).
Traps
(1) Cation rearrangement — n-propyl chloride + benzene → isopropylbenzene. (2) Polyalkylation — product is more activated than starting material, so di/trialkylation happens easily. (3) Deactivated rings — rings with NO₂, SO₃H, CF₃, carbonyls won't do FC.
5
Friedel-Crafts Acylation (RCOCl/AlCl₃)
No rearr.
▾
Electrophile = acylium ion RC≡O⁺, stabilized by resonance → no rearrangement, no polyacylation (product is a deactivated ketone). The synthesis workhorse.
Ar–H + RCOCl / AlCl₃ → Ar–CO–R + HCl
Clemmensen or Wolff-Kishner
To get a straight alkyl group onto a ring without rearrangement: acylate first (RCOCl/AlCl₃), then reduce the ketone to CH₂ with Zn(Hg)/HCl (Clemmensen) or NH₂NH₂/KOH (Wolff-Kishner). This is the go-to "n-propylbenzene without rearrangement" strategy.
6
Side-Chain Oxidation
To COOH
▾
Hot KMnO₄ (or K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄) oxidizes any benzylic C-H all the way to –COOH, regardless of alkyl chain length. No benzylic H (e.g., t-butylbenzene) → no reaction.
Ph–CH₂–CH₃  →KMnO₄ · Δ  Ph–COOH + CO₂
Ortho/Para vs Meta — why substituents direct where they do
o/p director
Aniline (–NH₂)
Donor with lone pair
Why o/p? The N lone pair can donate into the ring through resonance. The electron pair flows to ortho and para positions (push an arrow from N through π, land at those C's), raising electron density there. When E⁺ attacks, the resulting arenium ion carries + charge — but the + can sit right next to N, where N shares its lone pair to stabilize it. Attack at m doesn't benefit from this.
Arenium ion for para attack — stabilized by N lone pair
m director
Nitrobenzene (–NO₂)
Withdrawer via π bond
Why m? The C=N of –NO₂ has a π bond to an electronegative atom, which pulls electron density out of the ring at o/p by resonance. Those positions are electron-poor — exactly where E⁺ doesn't want to land. When E⁺ attacks m, the resulting + charge avoids being placed next to N (which would be disastrous, two + next to each other).
Arenium ion for ortho attack — forbidden: + next to δ+ N
The exception Halogens: o/p directors but deactivators
σ-effect (through bonds)
Halogens are very electronegative → they withdraw density inductively → deactivates the ring (slower EAS).
vs
π-effect (lone pair donation)
Halogens also have lone pairs that can donate into the ring → stabilize + at o/p → o/p director.
The σ-withdrawal wins for speed (slower than benzene) but the π-donation wins for regio. Net: o/p directing deactivator — the only substituent class that's split.

The 4-word rule

Every DAT directing-effects question boils down to these four cases.
Multi-step synthesis traps
Order matters. If you want meta-bromonitrobenzene:
1. Nitrate first (NO₂ is a m-director) → nitrobenzene.
2. Then brominate → m-bromonitrobenzene.
Reversing the order gives o/p because Br is an o/p director. The DAT will test this.
⚠ Friedel-Crafts won't work on these rings
Both FC alkylation and FC acylation fail on:
  • Strongly deactivated rings — nitrobenzene, benzoic acid, pyridine, anything with –NO₂, –CN, –SO₃H, –CF₃, –COR, –NR₃⁺ already on it. The ring is too electron-poor to attack the electrophile.
  • Anilines (–NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂) — the basic nitrogen complexes with AlCl₃, deactivating the catalyst AND protonating the ring into a ⁺NR₃ m-director. Workaround: acetylate the nitrogen first (→ –NHCOR), run FC, then hydrolyze the amide.
Rule of thumb: if the ring is less reactive than a halobenzene, FC won't go.
Aromatic takeaways
  • All 5 core EAS reactions share one mechanism: E⁺ generated, ring attacks, arenium ion, lose H⁺.
  • Lone pair → o/p. Pi bond to EN atom → m. Halogens are o/p directors but deactivators.
  • FC alkylation rearranges and polyalkylates. FC acylation does neither — use it plus Clemmensen/Wolff-Kishner for clean alkyl chains.
  • Nitration + reduction = standard route to anilines; side-chain oxidation with hot KMnO₄ = benzoic acid regardless of chain length.
  • Order of substitution determines the product. Always check directing effects of what's already on the ring.

Hub 08 · Largest

Aldehydes & Ketones — the carbonyl hub

Two worlds in one hub. The C=O carbon is electrophilic → nucleophiles attack it (nucleophilic addition). The α-carbon is acidic (pKa ≈ 20) → bases form enolates that act as nucleophiles. Every DAT carbonyl question is one of these two stories.

💡 Pattern shortcuts for this hub:
Plate IX — The Carbonyl Hub
Starting material: acetaldehyde (CH₃–CHO). Each branch shows the real skeletal product — two reaction modes: addition to electrophilic C or enolization at the α carbon.
Universal nucleophilic addition template
Step 1: Nu attacks the δ⁺ carbonyl carbon from above or below the plane → tetrahedral alkoxide.
Step 2: Protonation (or further steps) gives the product. Under acid, the carbonyl is protonated first (making C even more electrophilic) before attack.
1
Hydration → Hydrate (gem-diol)
Reversible
▾
Water adds across C=O to give a gem-diol (two OH on the same C). Equilibrium favors the carbonyl for most ketones; aldehydes are closer to 50/50; formaldehyde and Cl-containing aldehydes (e.g., chloral) favor the hydrate.
R₂C=O + H₂O ⇌ R₂C(OH)₂
2
Acetal Formation (2 ROH, H⁺)
Protecting group
▾
Aldehyde/ketone + 2 equiv alcohol + H⁺ → acetal (geminal diether). Goes through a hemiacetal intermediate. Water must be removed to drive it forward.
R₂C=O + 2 R'OH  ⇌H⁺  R₂C(OR')₂ + H₂O
⚡ Step Mode
Acetal Formation MechanismAcid-catalyzed · PADPED · acetaldehyde + 2 methanol → dimethyl acetal · 5 steps
1/5
PADPED — the universal acid-carbonyl mnemonic
Protonate · Add · Deprotonate · Protonate · Eliminate · Deprotonate. This same 6-letter dance runs Fischer esterification, acetal formation, imine formation, and most acid-catalyzed carbonyl chemistry. Memorize it once, apply it everywhere.
Acetals as protecting groups
Acetals are stable to base, Grignards, LiAlH₄, NaBH₄, nucleophiles, but hydrolyze back to the carbonyl under aqueous acid. Use to mask a C=O while doing chemistry elsewhere.
3
Imine Formation (1° amine)
C=N
▾
R₂C=O + R'NH₂ → R₂C=NR' + H₂O. Optimum pH ≈ 4–5 (acid needed to catalyze water loss, but too much acid protonates the amine).
R₂C=O + R'NH₂  ⇌H⁺  R₂C=NR' + H₂O
1
Amine attacks
R'NH₂ lone pair attacks the C=O carbon → tetrahedral N-protonated intermediate → proton transfer → carbinolamine (R₂C(OH)(NHR')).
2
Lose water
Protonate OH, water leaves → iminium ion (R₂C=NHR'⁺). Deprotonate N-H → imine (R₂C=NR').
Variants
HONH₂ → oxime. H₂N–NH₂ → hydrazone. H₂N–NHPh → phenylhydrazone. H₂N–NHC(=O)NH₂ → semicarbazone. All same mechanism, just different N-substituent.
4
Enamine Formation (2° amine)
C=C–N
▾
Same mechanism until the iminium step — but a 2° amine (e.g., pyrrolidine, morpholine) has no N-H to remove, so the carbonyl loses an α-H instead → C=C double bond → enamine.
R–CO–CH₂R'' + R₂NH  ⇌H⁺  R–C(NR₂)=CHR'' + H₂O
5
Cyanohydrin Formation (HCN)
C–C bond
▾
CN⁻ (from KCN or NaCN with a trace of acid) attacks C=O → alkoxide → protonation → cyanohydrin (HO–C–CN).
R₂C=O + HCN → R₂C(OH)(CN)
Follow-ups
Hydrolyze CN → α-hydroxy carboxylic acid (e.g., glycolic acid). Reduce CN → β-amino alcohol. This is a classic one-carbon homologation.
6
Reduction: NaBH₄ vs LiAlH₄
H⁻ addition
▾
Both deliver H⁻ to the carbonyl. Workup is H₃O⁺ or H₂O. Aldehyde → 1° alcohol. Ketone → 2° alcohol.
R₂C=O  →1. NaBH₄ · 2. H₂O  R₂CH–OH
Selectivity
NaBH₄: mild, reduces aldehydes and ketones only. Leaves esters, amides, acids alone.
LiAlH₄: powerful, reduces anything with a C=O: aldehyde, ketone, ester (→ 1° alcohol), amide (→ amine), carboxylic acid (→ 1° alcohol), nitrile (→ 1° amine), epoxide (→ alcohol).
DIBAL-H (1 equiv, cold): reduces ester or nitrile just to the aldehyde — stops halfway.
7
Grignard / Organolithium Addition
C–C bond
▾
R'–MgX (carbanion equivalent) attacks C=O → alkoxide → acid workup → alcohol with a new C–C bond.
R'–MgX + R₂C=O → R'R₂C–O⁻ MgX⁺  →H₃O⁺  R'R₂C–OH

Formaldehyde (HCHO) + RMgX → 1° alcohol.
Other aldehyde (R'CHO) + RMgX → 2° alcohol.
Ketone (R'R''C=O) + RMgX → 3° alcohol.
Grignard killers
Grignards attack any acidic proton (pKa < ~30): –OH, –NH, –COOH, –C≡CH, –NH₂, –SH will destroy the reagent before it can react with your carbonyl. Protect or avoid.

C=C bond
▾
Phosphorus ylide (Ph₃P=CHR') — made from Ph₃P + R'–CH₂–X, then strong base — attacks C=O, forms oxaphosphetane, collapses to C=CHR' and Ph₃P=O. Net: C=O is replaced with C=C.
R₂C=O + Ph₃P=CHR' → R₂C=CHR' + Ph₃P=O
Ylide types
Unstabilized ylides (CH₂ group, no EWG) → Z-alkene. Stabilized ylides (CH next to EWG like COOR, CN) → E-alkene. Also useful: you can tell where the double bond came from by "un-Wittig-ing" a target alkene retrosynthetically.
9

α-reaction
▾

2

10
α-Halogenation (X₂ with acid or base)
α-reaction
▾
In acid: monohalogenation (stops at mono because first X deactivates further enolization). In base: polyhalogenation (first X makes next α-H more acidic).

Methyl ketones (R–CO–CH₃) + excess X₂/NaOH → RCOO⁻ + CHX₃. Iodoform (CHI₃, yellow ppt) is the classic positive test for a methyl ketone or a 2° methyl carbinol (R–CH(OH)–CH₃).
11
Oxidation: Aldehyde → Acid
Easy
▾

  • Two attack modes: Nu → electrophilic C (addition) or α-H removed → nucleophilic enolate.
  • Aldehydes > ketones in reactivity (less steric, more δ⁺). Ketones = less electrophilic but still do everything on this list.
  • NaBH₄ is chemoselective (only aldehydes/ketones); LiAlH₄ reduces anything (esters, amides, acids, nitriles).
  • Aldol = enolate + second carbonyl → β-hydroxy carbonyl, which dehydrates to α,β-unsaturated product.
  • Imine with 1° amine; enamine with 2° amine. Both via carbinolamine.
  • Wittig swaps C=O for C=C. Clemmensen/Wolff-Kishner remove C=O entirely → CH₂.

Hub 09

Carboxylic Acids & Derivatives — the reactivity ladder

Carboxylic acids (RCOOH) and their "derivatives" — acid chlorides, anhydrides, esters, amides, nitriles — share the same mechanism: nucleophilic acyl substitution. A nucleophile attacks the carbonyl, kicks out the leaving group. The only question is: which direction is thermodynamically downhill?

Plate X — The Reactivity Ladder
Arranged by the leaving group ability of each substituent. Top is easiest to kick out, bottom is nearly impossible. Going down is spontaneous; up needs activation.
1
Fischer Esterification
Reversible · PADPED
▾
R–COOH + R'OH + H⁺ → ester + H₂O. Reversible; drive with excess R'OH or distill off water. Mechanism is the classic PADPED: Protonate, Add, Deprotonate, Protonate, Eliminate, Deprotonate.
R–COOH + R'OH  ⇌H⁺  R–COOR' + H₂O
⚡ Step Mode
Fischer EsterificationAcetic acid + methanol · full PADPED · 4 consolidated steps
1/4
Fischer vs Saponification
Fischer uses H⁺ catalysis and goes through a tetrahedral intermediate — reversible. Saponification uses OH⁻ as a stoichiometric base and also goes through a tetrahedral intermediate — but the final carboxylate product is unreactive (RCOO⁻), so it's irreversible. Same molecular choreography; opposite thermodynamics.
2
Saponification (ester + NaOH)
Irreversible
▾
Base hydrolysis of an ester. Irreversible because the RCOO⁻ product is not electrophilic (the base locks the acid as a carboxylate).
R–COOR' + NaOH → R–COO⁻ Na⁺ + R'OH
1
OH⁻ adds to carbonyl
Hydroxide attacks C=O → tetrahedral alkoxide.
2
OR' leaves
Tetrahedral intermediate collapses, ejecting OR'⁻ → carboxylic acid.
3
Proton transfer
OR'⁻ is more basic than RCOO⁻, so it deprotonates the acid → carboxylate + alcohol (irreversible).
3
Acid Chloride Formation (SOCl₂)
Activation
▾
R–COOH + SOCl₂ (or PCl₃, PCl₅) → R–COCl + SO₂ + HCl. The acid chloride can then react with anything: water, alcohols, amines, ring (FC acylation), H⁻ reducers, R₂Cu (→ ketone).
R–COOH + SOCl₂ → R–COCl + SO₂ + HCl
4
Acid Chloride → Anything
Universal
▾
R–COCl is the "king" of carbonyls. Reactions:
• + H₂O → R–COOH (+ HCl)
• + R'OH → ester R–COOR'
• + NH₃ or R₂NH → amide R–CONR'₂ (need 2 equiv — the second neutralizes HCl)
• + R'COO⁻ → anhydride
• + LiAlH₄ → 1° alcohol ; + LiAlH(OtBu)₃ → aldehyde (stops halfway)
• + R'₂CuLi (Gilman) → ketone (stops at ketone)
• + benzene/AlCl₃ → aryl ketone (FC acylation)
5
Amide Hydrolysis
Harsh
▾
Amides are the most stable carbonyl derivative — requires hot, concentrated acid or base to hydrolyze. Products: carboxylic acid (or carboxylate) + amine (or ammonium salt).
R–CONR'₂  →H₃O⁺ · Δ  R–COOH + HNR'₂
6
LiAlH₄ Reduction of COOH/Ester/Amide/Nitrile
Redox
▾
The LiAlH₄ reductions to memorize:
• R–COOH → R–CH₂OH (1° alcohol)
• R–COOR' → R–CH₂OH + R'OH
• R–CONR'₂ → R–CH₂–NR'₂ (amide becomes amine, not an alcohol — the N stays)
• R–CN → R–CH₂–NH₂ (nitrile to 1° amine)
• R–NO₂ → Not reduced by LiAlH₄ (use H₂/Pd or Sn/HCl)
7
Decarboxylation
β-keto / malonic
▾
Carboxylic acids don't decarboxylate under normal conditions. But β-keto acids and malonic acids (β-diacids) lose CO₂ on gentle heat via a cyclic 6-membered TS that forms an enol.
R–CO–CH₂–COOH  →Δ  R–CO–CH₃ + CO₂
Malonic ester synthesis
CH₂(COOR)₂ → deprotonate with NaOR → alkylate with R'X → saponify → β-diacid → decarboxylate → RCH₂COOH. Synthetic tool to make substituted acetic acids. Same idea with acetoacetic ester for methyl ketones.
Carboxylic acid takeaways
  • Reactivity ladder: acid chloride > anhydride > acid ≈ ester > amide. Going "down" is easy; going "up" requires making the acid chloride first.
  • Fischer (H⁺, ROH) is reversible; saponification (NaOH) is irreversible — locks as carboxylate.
  • LiAlH₄ reduces everything: acid/ester → 1° alcohol; amide → amine (N stays); nitrile → 1° amine.
  • Amides are the most stable → require hot concentrated acid/base to hydrolyze.
  • β-keto and β-diacids decarboxylate on heat — the key to malonic/acetoacetic ester syntheses.

Hub 10

Amines — nitrogen chemistry

Amines are basic (pKa of R₃NH⁺ ≈ 10) and nucleophilic. They're tough to make cleanly by direct alkylation (over-alkylation) — so DAT-tested methods use a trick: go through an azide, phthalimide, or nitrile, or use reductive amination.

💡 Pattern shortcuts for this hub:
Plate XI — Amine Synthesis & Reactions
Six syntheses feed in on the left; four reactions branch out on the right. Direct RX + NH₃ is avoided — it over-alkylates.
1
Azide Synthesis (cleanest 1° amine)
SN2 · reduce
▾
R–X + NaN₃ (SN2) → R–N₃, then LiAlH₄ (or H₂/Pd) reduces N₃ → NH₂. No over-alkylation because N₃ is not nucleophilic once installed.
R–X → R–N₃ → R–NH₂
2
Gabriel Synthesis
Phthalimide
▾
Potassium phthalimide (a nitrogen nucleophile tied into a ring) does SN2 on R–X → N-alkylphthalimide. Hydrazinolysis (NH₂NH₂) kicks out the amine. Gives only 1° amines.
Limitation
Works only on 1° alkyl halides (SN2). 3° gives E2 with the bulky phthalimide anion.
3
Reductive Amination
Imine → amine
▾
C=O + R'NH₂ → imine → reduce in situ with NaBH₃CN (selective, doesn't touch the carbonyl) → 2° amine. Most versatile way to extend an amine by one alkyl group cleanly.
R₂C=O + R'NH₂  →NaBH₃CN, pH 4-5  R₂CH–NHR'
⚡ Step Mode
Reductive Amination MechanismAcetone + methylamine + NaBH₃CN · imine then H⁻ · 3 steps
1/3
Why NaBH₃CN?
NaBH₄ would reduce the carbonyl before the amine could form an imine. NaBH₃CN is mild enough at pH 4-5 to leave the carbonyl intact — it only reduces the more electrophilic iminium ion. This selectivity is why reductive amination is one-pot.
4
Hofmann Elimination
Quat ammonium · less subst. alkene
▾
Exhaustively methylate an amine (excess CH₃I) → quaternary ammonium salt R–N⁺(CH₃)₃. Heat with Ag₂O / H₂O → the "⁺NMe₃" is a bulky leaving group → E2 giving the less substituted alkene (anti-Zaitsev, a.k.a. Hofmann product).
R–NH₂ → R–N⁺(CH₃)₃ I⁻ → alkene (Hofmann, less substituted)
5
Diazonium Chemistry (ArNH₂ + NaNO₂/HCl)
Aryl only
▾
Aryl amines (ArNH₂) + NaNO₂/HCl at 0 °C → aryl diazonium salt (Ar–N₂⁺). Powerful because N₂ is a spectacular leaving group. Reactions:
• + CuCl → Ar–Cl. + CuBr → Ar–Br. + CuCN → Ar–CN. (Sandmeyer)
• + KI → Ar–I. + H₃PO₂ → Ar–H (removes the N₂ entirely).
• + H₂O/Δ → Ar–OH (phenol).
• + Ar'–OH (coupling) → azo dye Ar–N=N–Ar'.
Amine takeaways
  • Direct alkylation of NH₃ with R–X over-alkylates → use azide, Gabriel, nitrile, or reductive amination to get clean 1° amines.
  • Reductive amination (C=O + amine + NaBH₃CN) adds one R group cleanly — best general method for 2°/3° amines.
  • Hofmann elimination (exhaustive methylation, Ag₂O/Δ) = less substituted alkene.
  • Aryl amines + NaNO₂/HCl at 0 °C = diazonium → swap N₂ for almost anything (Sandmeyer, OH, H, aryl coupling).
  • Amides reduce to amines with LiAlH₄ (N stays, C=O goes).
