Tier 2 · Node 16 of 40

DNA replication: build the fork. Watch it move.

DNA replicates semi-conservatively — every new double helix is one parent strand and one freshly synthesized daughter. The trick isn't memorizing the players; it's seeing why direction forces the lagging strand into Okazaki fragments.

The trap

Students memorize that DNA polymerase synthesizes 5′ → 3′ and stop there. Then a question asks why the lagging strand has Okazaki fragments and they freeze.

The rule

DNA polymerase only adds nucleotides to a free 3′-OH. The two template strands are antiparallel. So as the fork moves one way, only one daughter strand can be built continuously. The other has to be built backward, in fragments.

The move

Find the fork direction. Identify the leading template (read 3′→5′ in fork direction). The other one is lagging. Predict Okazaki + RNA primer + DNA pol I cleanup + ligase.

Polymerase only adds to 3′-OH. Antiparallel forces Okazaki.

From Coach Coach notes · the depth behind the rule

This is the foundation of the molecular biology tier.

The bacterial model is what the DAT tests. Anchor the difference between DNA pol III (the bulk replicating polymerase, no 5′→3′ exonuclease) and DNA pol I (cleans up RNA primers via its 5′→3′ exonuclease activity, then fills with DNA). Both proofread via separate 3′→5′ exonuclease activity. In eukaryotes the equivalents are pol α (primer + initial extension), pol δ (lagging strand), pol ε (leading strand), with FEN1 + RNase H handling primer removal and DNA ligase I sealing — but at DAT level these are rarely tested by name.

The end-replication problem (eukaryotic linear chromosomes lose telomere ends each replication cycle, addressed by telomerase) is a separate node, but it's worth flagging when a student asks "why don't bacteria have this problem?" — bacterial circular chromosomes have no ends to lose.

Common student misconceptions
  • "DNA polymerase makes DNA from scratch." No — it requires a free 3′-OH. Primase lays a short RNA primer first, then pol III extends from there.
  • "Both daughter strands are continuous." Only the leading strand. The lagging strand is built as Okazaki fragments because of the antiparallel + 5′→3′ constraints.
  • "Pol III proofreads but pol I doesn't." Both have 3′→5′ exonuclease activity for proofreading. Pol I additionally has 5′→3′ exo for primer removal — that's its distinct role.
  • "Helicase opens DNA in one direction only." At a single origin, replication is bidirectional — two forks move outward in opposite directions. Each fork itself is unidirectional once formed.
See also Transcription Translation · both apply the same 5′→3′ directionality rule to different polymerases.

From CoachCoach notes · molecular techniques (restriction · PCR · gel)

Three workhorse techniques. Cut · amplify · sort.

Molecular biology comes up on every DAT, and three techniques cover almost all of it: restriction enzymes (CUT DNA at specific sequences), PCR (AMPLIFY a target sequence millions of times), gel electrophoresis (SORT fragments by size). Together they form the workflow for cloning, paternity tests, forensics, COVID tests, and CRISPR engineering.

Restriction enzymes

Bacterial defense enzymes that recognize specific 4-8 bp DNA sequences (usually palindromes like GAATTC) and cut. Two cut styles: STICKY ENDS (overhanging single strands — EcoRI cuts GAATTC between G and AATTC, leaving complementary 4-base overhangs) and BLUNT ENDS (no overhang — SmaI cuts CCCGGG straight across). Sticky ends are gold for CLONING because complementary overhangs from two different sources can base-pair and ligate together — used to build recombinant DNA. Originally evolved to chew up invading viral DNA; the bacterium protects its own DNA by methylating its restriction sites.

PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)

Amplifies a target DNA sequence exponentially. Three repeated steps per cycle: DENATURE (95 °C — strands separate), ANNEAL (~55 °C — primers bind their complement at the target boundaries), EXTEND (72 °C — Taq polymerase synthesizes from the primer 5'→3'). Each cycle doubles the target → 2n copies after n cycles → 30 cycles = ~10⁹ copies from 1. Uses heat-stable Taq polymerase (from Thermus aquaticus, a hot-springs bacterium — discovered in Yellowstone) because regular polymerase would denature at 95 °C. Applications: amplify trace DNA for forensics, paternity tests, gene cloning, COVID PCR diagnostics.

Gel electrophoresis

SORTS DNA (or RNA, or protein) fragments by SIZE. DNA is negatively charged (phosphate backbone), so it migrates toward the POSITIVE electrode in an electric field through an agarose gel mesh. Smaller fragments move FASTER (less resistance through the pores) → end up FARTHER from the well. Larger fragments stay closer to the well. A ladder (mix of known-size fragments) runs alongside as a ruler. Stain with ethidium bromide (binds DNA, fluoresces under UV) to visualize. The diagnostic readout of every restriction digest, PCR product, or DNA fingerprint.

Restriction = CUT at palindromic sites (sticky vs blunt). PCR = AMPLIFY (denature / anneal / extend, 2ⁿ copies). Gel = SORT by size (small runs FAR, large stays NEAR the well, DNA goes toward the POSITIVE electrode).
Common student misconceptions
  • "PCR uses normal DNA polymerase." No — normal pol would denature at 95 °C. PCR requires THERMOSTABLE polymerase, classically Taq (from Thermus aquaticus). This is why PCR was a major breakthrough — Taq made cyclic amplification practical.
  • "On a gel, big fragments travel farther." Opposite — SMALL fragments travel FAR (less obstruction); LARGE fragments stay closer to the wells. Memorize: small = fast = far.
  • "DNA migrates toward the negative electrode." Opposite — DNA is negatively charged (phosphate backbone), so it migrates toward the POSITIVE electrode. Sometimes phrased "DNA runs to the red" (positive terminal is conventionally red on electrophoresis rigs).
  • "Restriction enzymes always cut at exactly one site." They cut at EVERY occurrence of their recognition sequence in the DNA. A long DNA has many cuts, producing many fragments — that's WHY you need a gel to sort them.
  • "Sticky ends are weaker than blunt ends." Sticky ends are PREFERRED for cloning because the complementary overhangs can re-anneal to each other (specific re-joining). Blunt ends require non-specific ligation and are inefficient — but useful when you can't find a matching restriction site in both source and destination.

Transcription: DNA → mRNA, one strand at a time.

RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3′→5′ and synthesizes mRNA 5′→3′. Promoter says "start," terminator says "stop." Same directionality rule as replication, single-stranded RNA product, eukaryotes splice + cap + tail.

The trap

Students mix up template (read by RNA pol) with coding (matches mRNA). They write a wrong base sequence on every transcription question.

The rule

RNA pol reads template 3′→5′, builds mRNA 5′→3′. Coding strand = same as mRNA (T→U). Template strand = complementary, antiparallel.

The move

Find promoter to lock orientation. Identify template (read 3′→5′). Build mRNA from base-pair complement (A→U, T→A, G→C, C→G).

From Coach Coach notes · coding vs template, and what eukaryotes add

The single most-missed concept: coding vs template.

Students see "coding strand" and assume it's the one being read. It isn't. The coding strand has the same sequence as the mRNA it codes for (just with T instead of U) — it's the strand that "matches" the message. The template strand is the antiparallel complement, and it's what RNA polymerase actually reads (3′→5′). The cleanest way to teach this: have the student write the mRNA from a coding strand sequence by inspection (just swap T→U). They'll get it right. Then derive the template by complement + reverse, and connect the dots.

Eukaryotes layer on three things prokaryotes don't have:

  • Three RNA polymerases: pol I makes rRNA in the nucleolus, pol II makes mRNA (the one DAT cares about most), pol III makes tRNAs and small RNAs.
  • Three processing steps: 5′ cap (7-methylguanosine, protects from exonucleases + helps ribosome bind), poly-A tail (~200 A residues, protects + signals nuclear export), intron splicing by the spliceosome.
  • No coupled transcription-translation: in prokaryotes, ribosomes start translating mRNA before transcription finishes. In eukaryotes, mRNA must be processed and exported from the nucleus first.
Common student misconceptions
  • "RNA polymerase needs a primer." No — it can start synthesis from scratch. This is one of the few enzymes that doesn't need a primer.
  • "Eukaryotic mRNA can be translated immediately after transcription." No — it needs the cap, tail, and splicing first, then nuclear export. This is why prokaryotes can do coupled translation but eukaryotes can't.
  • "All RNA in a cell is mRNA." mRNA is <5% of total cellular RNA. Most is rRNA (ribosomal). Also tRNA, snRNA, miRNA, lncRNA, etc.

Tier 1 · Cell biology

RNA processing: eukaryote-only mRNA maturation.

In eukaryotes, the freshly transcribed pre-mRNA is NOT yet ready for translation. It must be capped at the 5' end (7-methylguanosine), tailed at the 3' end (poly-A, ~200 As), and spliced (introns removed by the spliceosome, exons joined). All three modifications happen in the nucleus before export. Prokaryotes skip processing entirely — transcription and translation are coupled.

The trap

Students forget RNA processing is eukaryote-only. They confuse exon (kept) with intron (removed). They miss that capping/tailing PROTECT the message and signal export.

The rule

Three modifications: (1) 5' cap — 7-methylguanosine added to protect from exonucleases and signal ribosome binding. (2) 3' poly-A tail — ~200 adenines added; protects against degradation, signals nuclear export. (3) Splicing — spliceosome removes introns and joins exons; the intron is released as a lariat.

The move

For "what kind of RNA exits the nucleus?" → mature mRNA (capped, tailed, spliced). For "why are eukaryotic transcription and translation separated?" → processing happens in between. For "what removes introns?" → spliceosome (snRNPs).

Cap + tail protect the ends. Splicing removes introns. All in the nucleus, eukaryote-only.

From CoachCoach notes · the three modifications + why they matter

Eukaryotes process. Prokaryotes don't.

5' cap (7-methylguanosine). Added co-transcriptionally as the first ~25 nucleotides emerge from RNA polymerase II. The cap is a methylated guanosine attached "backward" via a 5'-5' triphosphate linkage. Functions: (1) protects the 5' end from exonucleases, (2) recruits the small ribosomal subunit during translation initiation, (3) signals nuclear export.

3' poly-A tail. After RNA polymerase encounters the AAUAAA polyadenylation signal, the transcript is cleaved and ~200 adenosines are added by poly-A polymerase. Functions: (1) protects against 3' exonuclease attack, (2) export signal, (3) the tail shortens with each round of translation — once it falls below ~30 As, the message is degraded.

Splicing. Pre-mRNA contains introns (intervening sequences, removed) and exons (expressed sequences, kept). The spliceosome — built from snRNPs (small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, U1/U2/U4/U5/U6) and many proteins — cuts introns out and ligates exons together. The removed intron forms a lariat (lasso-shaped) structure via a 2'-5' bond at the branch-point adenine, then is degraded.

Why coupling fails in eukaryotes. The pre-mRNA must be capped, tailed, AND spliced before it can leave the nucleus. Translation requires the mature transcript. So in eukaryotes, transcription happens in the nucleus, processing happens in the nucleus, then mature mRNA is exported to the cytoplasm where translation occurs. In prokaryotes, no nucleus → ribosomes can latch onto the mRNA while RNA polymerase is still transcribing it.

Alternative splicing (concept-level only): different combinations of exons can be joined from the same pre-mRNA, producing different protein isoforms from the same gene. One reason eukaryotes have far more proteins than genes.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Both prokaryotes and eukaryotes splice." Prokaryotes do NOT splice (their genes don't have introns). Splicing is a eukaryotic feature.
  • "The cap and tail are part of the protein." No — the cap and tail are NOT translated. They are non-coding modifications that protect the message and regulate its lifetime.
  • "Introns get translated into the protein." Backwards — introns are intruders that are removed; exons are expressed in the protein. Mnemonic: introns are intervening; exons exit (into the mature mRNA).
  • "Splicing happens after the mRNA leaves the nucleus." No — splicing is nuclear. Mature mRNA leaves the nucleus already spliced.
See also Transcription Translation · processing happens between transcription (which makes pre-mRNA) and translation (which reads mature mRNA).

Tier 2 · Node 18 of 40

Translation: mRNA → protein, three bases at a time.

Ribosomes read mRNA in codons (3 nt each). tRNAs deliver amino acids whose anticodons base-pair antiparallel + complementary. Three sites — A, P, E — choreograph the dance.

The trap

Students forget codons run 5′→3′ and that anticodons are antiparallel. They write the anticodon backward and miss every mRNA-to-protein question.

The rule

Ribosome reads mRNA 5′→3′. Each codon = 3 nt = 1 amino acid. Anticodon antiparallel + complementary to codon. AUG = start. UAA / UAG / UGA = stop.

The move

Locate AUG. Read codons in order. For each, write antiparallel anticodon. Build polypeptide N→C. Stop at UAA/UAG/UGA.

From Coach Coach notes · the APE order + ribosome architecture

The APE mnemonic locks site order. Reinforce it relentlessly.

Three sites on every ribosome — A (Aminoacyl/Acceptor), P (Peptidyl), E (Exit). tRNAs flow A → P → E during elongation. The mnemonic 'APE' is exactly the order. The single exception: the initiator Met-tRNA goes directly to the P site at initiation — bypassing A. That's the only time A is empty during normal translation. Reinforce by walking through one cycle: tRNA arrives in A → peptide bond shifts the chain to A's tRNA → ribosome translocates one codon → the now-deacylated old P-tRNA moves to E and exits.

Ribosome size differences are also DAT-relevant:

  • Prokaryotic ribosome: 70S total = 30S small + 50S large. Site of antibiotics like streptomycin, tetracycline.
  • Eukaryotic ribosome: 80S total = 40S small + 60S large. Mitochondrial and chloroplast ribosomes are 70S (endosymbiotic theory evidence).

The "S" stands for Svedberg unit (sedimentation coefficient) — not additive, which is why 30S + 50S = 70S not 80S.

Common student misconceptions
  • "tRNA delivers any amino acid to any codon." Each tRNA is specific to one amino acid — charged by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, which has the highest specificity in the cell. The wrong amino acid on a tRNA = a wrong protein, every time.
  • "Stop codons code for a 'stop' amino acid." No — stop codons don't code for any amino acid. Release factors recognize them and trigger hydrolysis of the bond between polypeptide and tRNA.
  • "Eukaryotic ribosomes are the same as prokaryotic." Different sizes (80S vs 70S), different rRNA, different antibiotic susceptibility. This is why we can target bacterial ribosomes with antibiotics without killing our own cells.
  • "Translation reads codons 3′→5′." Wrong direction. Ribosome reads mRNA 5′→3′, building the polypeptide N-terminus to C-terminus.

Mitosis: one cell into two, with chromosome integrity preserved.

Mitosis is the somatic-cell division process. One parental nucleus becomes two genetically identical daughter nuclei. Five phases (PMAT + cytokinesis), each defined by a specific chromosome state. The trick is the order, not the chemistry.

The trap

Students confuse mitosis with meiosis (which has two divisions, four daughter cells). They mix up prophase and prometaphase, or anaphase and telophase.

The rule

Mitosis = one division. Daughters genetically identical to parent. Five phases in fixed order: Prophase → Prometaphase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase, with cytokinesis overlapping late telophase.

The move

Memorize PMAT. For any chromosome state described, identify the phase. For any phase named, predict the chromosome state.

PMAT — five phases, identical daughters, fixed order.

From Coach Coach notes · the cell cycle context + the meiosis pivot

Mitosis is somatic. Meiosis is gametic. Don't conflate.

Mitosis: 2n → 2n (identical). One division, two diploid daughters, used for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction. Meiosis: 2n → n (haploid). Two divisions, four genetically unique haploid gametes, used for sexual reproduction. Daughters of meiosis are NOT identical — crossing-over (prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I) generate variation.

The cell cycle context: G1 (cell grows) → S phase (DNA replicates — this is when the chromosome doubles, BEFORE mitosis) → G2 (cell prepares to divide) → M phase (mitosis) → cytokinesis (cell physically splits) → back to G1. Some cells exit to G0 (quiescence) — mature neurons stay there permanently.

Cytokinesis is technically separate from M phase. Animal cells form a cleavage furrow via actomyosin contraction (think of tightening a drawstring). Plant cells form a cell plate from vesicles fusing in the middle — they can't pinch because of the cell wall.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Mitosis produces 4 daughter cells." No — that's meiosis. Mitosis = 2 daughters.
  • "Sister chromatids separate in metaphase." No — they ALIGN in metaphase. They separate in anaphase.
  • "Mitosis includes DNA replication." No — DNA was replicated in S phase, BEFORE mitosis. Each chromosome enters mitosis already as two sister chromatids.
  • "Plant cells have a cleavage furrow." No — their cell wall prevents pinching. Plants form a cell plate that grows outward into a new wall.

Glycolysis: 1 glucose → 2 pyruvate, net 2 ATP, 2 NADH.

From Coach Coach notes · investment phase, payoff phase, regulation

Glycolysis has two halves: investment and payoff.

Investment phase (steps 1-5): the cell spends 2 ATP to phosphorylate glucose, destabilizing it for breakdown. Glucose → G6P (hexokinase, uses 1 ATP) → F6P → F1,6BP (phosphofructokinase = PFK, uses 1 ATP, RATE-LIMITING) → split into 2 G3P (3-carbon).

Payoff phase (steps 6-10): 2 G3P → 2 pyruvate. Generates 4 ATP and 2 NADH. Net: 4 - 2 = 2 ATP. Plus 2 NADH that head to the electron transport chain (if oxygen available).

Regulation: PFK is allosterically controlled.

  • Inhibited by: ATP (high cellular energy = no need to break down more glucose), citrate (means Krebs is full).
  • Activated by: AMP, ADP (low energy state, need ATP fast), F2,6BP (insulin signal).

Without oxygen: pyruvate → lactate (animals, regenerates NAD+) or ethanol + CO2 (yeast). This regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue running. With oxygen, pyruvate enters the mitochondria for the Krebs cycle.

Fermentation in detail (the DAT tests this): the entire reason fermentation exists is to regenerate NAD+ from the NADH glycolysis produced. Without NAD+, the GAPDH step (step 6) stalls and glycolysis halts. So when oxygen isn't around to accept electrons via the ETC, the cell uses pyruvate itself as the electron sink:

  • Lactic acid fermentation (animals, exercising muscle, lactic-acid bacteria): pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD+. One enzyme: lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). No CO2 released. Lactate exported to the liver (Cori cycle — gluconeogenesis converts lactate back to glucose).
  • Alcoholic fermentation (yeast, some bacteria): pyruvate → (loses CO2) acetaldehyde → (NADH used) ethanol + NAD+. Two enzymes: pyruvate decarboxylase then alcohol dehydrogenase. CO2 is released — that's why bread rises and beer carbonates.
  • Net ATP from fermentation: still just 2 ATP per glucose — same as anaerobic glycolysis. Fermentation does NOT generate additional ATP. Its job is to recycle NAD+.
  • Compare to aerobic respiration: ~30-32 ATP/glucose. Fermentation is ~15× less efficient — but it's the only option when O2 is absent.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Glycolysis produces 4 ATP." 4 are produced but 2 are invested first. Net is 2 ATP.
  • "Glycolysis requires oxygen." No — it's anaerobic. Glycolysis runs in the cytoplasm without oxygen. Fermentation handles the NADH problem when oxygen is unavailable.
  • "Hexokinase is the rate-limiting enzyme." No — it's PFK (step 3). Hexokinase phosphorylates glucose first, but PFK is the committed step where regulation happens.
  • "Lactate is a waste product." It's a temporary regeneration of NAD+. The Cori cycle recycles lactate in the liver back to glucose.

Typical question wording

From CoachCoach notes · the four levels in detail

Four levels, each adding complexity. Each can be disrupted independently.

Primary structure: the linear sequence of amino acids, joined by peptide bonds (amide linkage between α-carboxyl of one + α-amino of next, releasing H₂O). Encoded by mRNA codon order. Permanent unless protease cleaves.

Secondary structure: local folding patterns — α-helix (right-handed coil, H-bonds every 4 residues, R-groups point outward) and β-pleated sheet (parallel or antiparallel strands, H-bonds between strands). Both are stabilized by backbone H-bonds — not R-group dependent.

Tertiary structure: the overall 3D fold of one polypeptide. Driven by R-group interactions: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds (salt bridges), hydrophobic clustering (drives folding in aqueous environment), disulfide bridges (covalent, between cysteine -SH groups). Determines function.

Quaternary structure: assembly of multiple polypeptide subunits. Hemoglobin = 4 subunits (2 α + 2 β). Not all proteins have quaternary — only multimers.

Denaturation: heat, pH change, organic solvents, mechanical stress disrupt H-bonds + R-group interactions but not peptide bonds. Primary structure preserved; secondary/tertiary/quaternary lost. Function lost. Often irreversible.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Denaturation breaks the peptide bonds." No — only the H-bonds + R-group interactions. Sequence is preserved (peptide bonds are covalent, very stable).
  • "Hydrogen bonds form tertiary structure." Partly — but tertiary is dominated by R-group interactions including hydrophobic effect (the strongest force in folding) + disulfide bridges. Secondary is what's purely H-bonded.
  • "All proteins have quaternary structure." Only multi-subunit proteins. Many functional proteins are monomers (myoglobin, lysozyme, single enzymes).

A competitive inhibitor's effect on enzyme kinetics is best described as:
From CoachCoach notes · the kinetics that matter

Enzymes lower activation energy. They don't change ΔG or equilibrium.

Enzymes provide an alternate reaction pathway with a lower activation energy (Ea). They don't change ΔG of the reaction or the equilibrium constant — they just speed BOTH the forward and reverse reactions equally. They're not consumed; they're regenerated after each catalytic cycle.

Enzyme-substrate binding:

  • Lock-and-key (Fischer 1894): rigid active site shape complementary to substrate. Useful first model but oversimplified.
  • Induced fit (Koshland 1958): active site flexes to optimally bind substrate. More accurate — active site reshapes to enhance catalysis.

Michaelis-Menten kinetics:

  • Vmax: max reaction rate when all enzyme is saturated with substrate.
  • Km: substrate concentration at which V = Vmax / 2. Inverse measure of binding affinity — low Km = high affinity, high Km = low affinity.

Inhibition:

  • Competitive: inhibitor binds active site. Substrate competes. Outcome: increase substrate → overcome inhibitor. Km rises (need more substrate). Vmax unchanged (still reachable). Lineweaver-Burk: same y-intercept, different x-intercept.
  • Noncompetitive: inhibitor binds allosteric site. Doesn't compete with substrate. Outcome: Vmax drops (effective enzyme amount reduced). Km unchanged. Lineweaver-Burk: different y-intercept, same x-intercept.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Enzymes are used up after catalyzing a reaction." No — they're regenerated. One enzyme molecule can catalyze thousands to millions of reactions.
  • "Enzymes change ΔG." No — they don't change thermodynamics. Just kinetics (rate). Equilibrium constant Keq is unchanged.
  • "Low Km means low affinity." Opposite. Low Km = high affinity (enzyme reaches half Vmax at low [S], meaning it binds substrate tightly).

Cytoskeleton: three filaments, three jobs.

The cytoskeleton is the cell's internal scaffolding plus its highway system. Three filament types, easily distinguished by SIZE, MONOMER, and FUNCTION: microfilaments (smallest, ~7 nm, actin) handle cell shape and movement (muscle, cytokinesis); microtubules (largest, ~25 nm, tubulin) form the mitotic spindle, cilia/flagella, and intracellular transport tracks; intermediate filaments (mid-sized, ~10 nm, various proteins like keratin) provide mechanical strength.

The trap

Students mix up the three. Worst confusion: which is the spindle made of? (Microtubules.) Which makes the cleavage furrow? (Microfilaments / actin.) Which gives skin its tensile strength? (Intermediate filaments / keratin.)

The rule

Microfilaments = actin = SMALLEST = movement (muscle contraction, cytokinesis cleavage furrow, amoeboid motility). Microtubules = tubulin = LARGEST = transport tracks + spindle + cilia/flagella (9+2 axoneme). Intermediate filaments = various (keratin, vimentin, lamin) = MIDDLE = mechanical resilience (skin, hair, nuclear envelope).

The move

For any cell event, identify which fiber. Spindle in mitosis? microtubules. Cytokinesis ring? microfilaments. Cilia/flagella? microtubules. Skin tensile strength? intermediate (keratin). Nuclear envelope support? intermediate (lamin).

From CoachCoach notes · the three filaments + their motors

Three filaments. Memorize size, monomer, function for each.

Microfilaments (actin filaments)

  • Monomer: globular actin (G-actin) polymerizes into filamentous actin (F-actin), a double helix.
  • Size: ~7 nm diameter (smallest of the three).
  • Polarity: yes — barbed (+) end grows fast, pointed (−) end shrinks. Treadmilling possible.
  • Motor protein: myosin walks along actin (powers muscle contraction, cytokinesis).
  • Functions: muscle contraction (with myosin); cytokinesis cleavage furrow (the contractile ring); amoeboid movement (lamellipodia + filopodia); microvilli core; cell shape near membrane.

Microtubules

  • Monomer: alpha- and beta-tubulin heterodimers polymerize into hollow tubes.
  • Size: ~25 nm outer diameter (largest of the three).
  • Polarity: yes — (+) end grows away from centrosome / MTOC; (−) end is anchored. Dynamic instability (rapid growth/shrinkage controlled by GTP hydrolysis).
  • Motor proteins: kinesin walks toward (+) end; dynein walks toward (−) end. They carry vesicles and organelles.
  • Functions: mitotic spindle (separates chromosomes); cilia and flagella (9+2 axoneme arrangement); intracellular transport highways; centrioles in animal cells.

Intermediate filaments

  • Monomer: tissue-specific — keratin (epithelium, skin, hair), vimentin (mesenchyme), neurofilaments (neurons), lamin (nuclear envelope).
  • Size: ~10 nm (middle).
  • Polarity: NO. They are not polar — assembly is symmetric and they don't use motor proteins.
  • Functions: mechanical resilience — resist stretching and tearing. Hold tissues together (desmosomes anchor keratin filaments). Stabilize the nuclear envelope (nuclear lamina = lamins).

9+2 axoneme: the cross-section of a cilium or flagellum shows 9 outer doublets of microtubules surrounding 2 central microtubules. Dynein arms between adjacent doublets cause sliding, which produces bending. Centrioles and basal bodies show 9 triplets and no center pair (9+0).

Common student misconceptions
  • "The spindle is made of intermediate filaments." No — spindle = microtubules.
  • "Cytokinesis is driven by microtubules." No — cytokinesis cleavage furrow = microfilaments (actin contractile ring + myosin). Microtubules drive karyokinesis (chromosome separation).
  • "Intermediate filaments use ATP and motor proteins." No — they are non-polar and have no motors. They are static support, not dynamic transport.
  • "Cilia and flagella are made of intermediate filaments." No — both are microtubule-based (9+2).

From CoachCoach notes · the five transport modes

Always ask: does the cell pay ATP?

  • Simple diffusion: small, nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, lipid-soluble drugs) pass directly through the bilayer. Down gradient. No protein needed.
  • Facilitated diffusion: polar / charged molecules (glucose, ions) need a protein channel or carrier. Still down gradient. Still no ATP. Still passive.
  • Osmosis: water moves through aquaporins or directly through bilayer to balance solute concentrations. Always down water's gradient (= up the solute gradient on the other side). No ATP.
  • Active transport: against the gradient. Burns ATP. Classic example: Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pumps 3 Na out + 2 K in per ATP, maintaining the membrane gradient that powers nerve cells.
  • Bulk transport: endocytosis (cell takes in big stuff: phagocytosis = cells, pinocytosis = liquid, receptor-mediated = specific) and exocytosis (cell secretes vesicle contents). Both use ATP for the membrane work.

Tonicity terms: compare the solute concentration outside the cell to inside.

  • Hypertonic surroundings: more solute outside → water leaves → cell shrinks (crenates in animals, plasmolyzes in plants).
  • Hypotonic surroundings: less solute outside → water enters → cell swells (lyses in animals, becomes turgid in plants).
  • Isotonic: equal → no net water flow.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Facilitated diffusion uses ATP." No — it's passive. The protein just provides a polar tunnel for polar molecules. Direction is dictated by gradient.
  • "Hypertonic = high water." Backwards. Hypertonic = HIGH solute = LOW water. Water moves from hypotonic (high water) to hypertonic (low water).
  • "Channels work like pumps." Channels are passive (gated open/closed); pumps are active (use ATP to move ions against gradient).

From CoachCoach notes · the four phases of cellular respiration

Glycolysis → PDH → Krebs → ETC. Each phase has a location and a yield.

  • Glycolysis (cytoplasm): glucose → 2 pyruvate. Net 2 ATP + 2 NADH. No oxygen needed (anaerobic-capable).
  • Pyruvate decarboxylation (PDH, matrix): 2 pyruvate → 2 acetyl-CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 NADH. Each pyruvate loses one carbon as CO₂ and joins coenzyme A.
  • Krebs cycle / citric acid cycle (matrix): runs twice per glucose (one per acetyl-CoA). Per turn: 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP + 2 CO₂. So per glucose: 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ + 2 GTP + 4 CO₂. Now all 6 carbons of glucose have left as CO₂.
  • Electron transport chain + chemiosmosis (inner mitochondrial membrane): NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to complexes I → III → IV (NADH path) or II → III → IV (FADH₂ path). Electrons end at O₂ → H₂O. Energy from electron flow pumps H⁺ from matrix to intermembrane space, building a proton gradient. ATP synthase lets H⁺ flow back through, driving ATP synthesis. ~28 ATP via this stage. Per NADH: ~2.5 ATP; per FADH₂: ~1.5 ATP.

Total per glucose: ~32 ATP (older textbooks say 36-38; refined ~30-32 accounting for shuttle costs).

Common student misconceptions
  • "O₂ is used to make ATP." O₂ doesn't directly drive ATP synthesis. It's the FINAL electron acceptor at complex IV — without it, the chain backs up + the cycle halts.
  • "Krebs cycle is anaerobic." It is technically — doesn't use O₂ directly — but it stops if ETC stops, because it needs to recycle NAD⁺ and FAD.
  • "The proton gradient is across the outer mitochondrial membrane." It's across the INNER membrane (between matrix and intermembrane space). The outer membrane is freely permeable to small molecules.

Anaerobic muscle exports its problem. Liver buys it back.

During intense exercise (or in red blood cells anytime), muscles run faster than O₂ can be delivered. The mitochondrial ETC can't keep up → NADH backs up → glycolysis would halt for lack of NAD+. MUSCLE (anaerobic): glucose → 2 pyruvate (glycolysis, 2 ATP) → 2 lactate (via lactate dehydrogenase; regenerates NAD+). Lactate exported to blood.
BLOOD: carries lactate to liver.
LIVER: lactate → pyruvate → glucose via GLUCONEOGENESIS (uses 6 ATP per glucose — net cost of running the cycle).
BLOOD: carries glucose back to muscle.
Net: muscle gets 2 ATP per glucose; liver spends 6 ATP per glucose to recycle it. The liver eats the energy cost so muscle can keep contracting without waiting for O₂.

The traps

(1) Lactate is NOT just a waste product or the cause of muscle "burn." Lactate is the muscle's solution to the NAD+ problem. The burn during exercise is mostly H+ accumulation (from ATP hydrolysis) — not lactate per se.
(2) RBCs use the Cori cycle continuously, not just under exercise — they have no mitochondria, so they ONLY make ATP anaerobically.
(3) Muscle CANNOT do gluconeogenesis (it lacks glucose-6-phosphatase). Liver is the only major gluconeogenic organ.

Why it matters

The Cori cycle is the canonical interorgan metabolism story on the DAT — tests integration of glycolysis (muscle) + gluconeogenesis (liver) + transport (blood) + the chemistry of NAD+ regeneration. If the DAT asks "how does muscle keep going when O₂ runs out?" or "where does the lactate go?" — Cori cycle is the answer.

Muscle: glucose → lactate (regen NAD+, gets 2 ATP). Liver: lactate → glucose (gluconeogenesis, costs 6 ATP). Liver eats the cost.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Lactate is a waste product / causes muscle burn." Lactate is the solution to the NAD+ shortage. It's actively recycled back to glucose by the liver. The "burn" is acidosis from H+ — not lactate.
  • "Muscle does gluconeogenesis." Muscle LACKS glucose-6-phosphatase, so it cannot release free glucose. Liver is the only major gluconeogenic organ.
  • "Fermentation makes ATP." Fermentation (lactate or ethanol) makes ZERO ATP by itself — its purpose is to regenerate NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue. The ATP comes from glycolysis alone (2 net per glucose).
Tier 1 · Node 12 of 40

Photosynthesis: light → sugar, in two stages.

Plants + algae + cyanobacteria capture light energy + fix CO₂ into glucose. Light reactions (thylakoid membrane): split water + make ATP + NADPH. Calvin cycle (stroma): use them to fix CO₂ via RuBisCO → G3P → glucose.

The trap

Students think the Calvin cycle needs light directly. It doesn't — it uses the ATP + NADPH that the light reactions produced. They confuse the products: O₂ from water, not from CO₂.

The rule

Light reactions: H₂O split → O₂, ATP, NADPH (thylakoid). Calvin cycle: 3 CO₂ + 9 ATP + 6 NADPH → 1 G3P (stroma). 6 turns → 1 glucose.

The move

Locate: thylakoid (light) or stroma (Calvin). Identify input vs output: O₂ from water; CO₂ into sugar.

From CoachCoach notes · the two stages and what each makes

Photosynthesis is respiration in reverse, mostly. CO₂ goes in; O₂ comes out.

Overall equation: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂.

Light reactions (thylakoid membrane):

  • Photosystem II (P680): light excites electrons, splits H₂O → O₂ + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻. The O₂ we breathe came from water!
  • Electron transport chain (cytochrome b₆f): electrons flow from PSII to PSI, pumping H⁺ into thylakoid lumen. Builds proton gradient.
  • Photosystem I (P700): light re-excites electrons. They reduce NADP⁺ → NADPH.
  • ATP synthase: H⁺ flows from lumen to stroma through ATP synthase → ATP. (Photophosphorylation.)
  • Net products: ATP + NADPH (for Calvin cycle). O₂ (released).

Calvin cycle (stroma) — light-independent:

  • 1. Carbon fixation: CO₂ + RuBP (5C) → 2 × 3-PGA (3C). Catalyzed by RuBisCO — the most abundant enzyme on earth.
  • 2. Reduction: 3-PGA → G3P, using ATP + NADPH from light reactions.
  • 3. Regeneration: 5/6 of G3P regenerates RuBP. 1/6 leaves to make glucose. 6 cycle turns + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH = 1 glucose.
Common student misconceptions
  • "O₂ comes from CO₂." No — O₂ comes from water. PSII splits water; CO₂ gets fixed into sugar (carbons end up in glucose).
  • "The Calvin cycle requires light." It uses ATP + NADPH, but doesn't directly need light. It runs as long as those reactants are available. Older texts called it the "dark reactions" — misleading.
  • "Plants only do photosynthesis." Plants do both photosynthesis (chloroplasts, daytime) AND cellular respiration (mitochondria, all the time). They need to break down their own glucose for energy too.
