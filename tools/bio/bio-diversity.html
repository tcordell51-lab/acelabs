Reading phylogenies + character types

Cladistics groups organisms by shared derived characters (synapomorphies). The DAT tests four big distinctions.

Homologous structures

Same evolutionary origin, possibly different functions. Show common ancestry. Used to build phylogenies.

EX: tetrapod limbs (human arm, bat wing, whale fin, cat leg — same bone plan, different jobs)
Analogous structures

Same function, independent evolutionary origin. Result of convergent evolution. Do NOT indicate common ancestry.

EX: bird wings vs insect wings, dolphin vs shark body shape, cactus vs euphorbia spines
Synapomorphy (shared derived)

Trait present in common ancestor of a clade + all its descendants — but absent in outgroups. Defines monophyletic groups.

EX: amniotic egg = synapomorphy of reptiles + birds + mammals
Plesiomorphy (shared ancestral)

Old trait inherited from a deep ancestor, present in many groups. Doesn't help define small clades.

EX: vertebral column is plesiomorphic for all vertebrates — doesn't distinguish among them
Monophyletic (clade)

Common ancestor + ALL its descendants. The only valid group for cladistics.

EX: Mammalia, Aves, Tetrapoda
Paraphyletic

Common ancestor + SOME descendants (excludes some). Older Linnaean groupings often paraphyletic.

EX: "Reptilia" as traditionally defined excludes birds — paraphyletic. "Fish" excludes tetrapods.
Polyphyletic

Group of organisms WITHOUT a common ancestor in the group. Based on superficial shared traits, not ancestry.

EX: "warm-blooded animals" (mammals + birds — endothermy evolved separately)
Outgroup

Taxon outside the clade of interest, used to polarize characters (decide which is ancestral vs derived) and root the tree.

EX: when comparing primates, a non-primate mammal serves as outgroup
Cladistics quick rules
What the DAT asks

"Which is monophyletic?" → look for ancestor + ALL descendants. If birds are missing from "reptiles" → paraphyletic.

"Homologous or analogous?" → same origin = homologous. Same function only = analogous (convergent).

"Most recent common ancestor?" → trace back from both taxa to the closest branch point.

"Sister taxa?" → two taxa whose nearest branch point is to each other (no other taxon in between).

Section 04 · Diversity

Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.

Carl Woese's rRNA-based classification (1977) replaced the 5-kingdom system. Three domains, with Eukarya splitting further into 4 kingdoms (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia). Archaea are closer to Eukarya than to Bacteria — a key DAT trap.

The trap

Students think Archaea are "weird bacteria" because both are single-celled prokaryotes. They miss that Archaea share key molecular features (histones, multiple RNA pol, Met start) with Eukarya.

The rule

Bacteria: peptidoglycan walls, ester lipids, fMet start, single RNA pol. Archaea: pseudopeptidoglycan, ether-linked branched isoprenoid lipids, Met start, histones. Eukarya: nucleus + organelles, 80S ribosomes, linear chromosomes.

The move

Identify the domain by lipid linkage (ether = Archaea), start codon (fMet vs Met), peptidoglycan presence, organelle compartmentalization.

Archaea + Eukarya share more than Archaea + Bacteria.

From CoachCoach notes · the molecular distinguishers

Don't classify by appearance — classify by molecules.

Bacteria (Eubacteria): peptidoglycan cell walls (gram + thick / gram − thin + outer LPS membrane). Ester-linked membrane lipids. fMet start codon. Single RNA polymerase. 70S ribosomes. No introns, no histones (some histone-like proteins). Sensitive to antibiotics targeting peptidoglycan or 70S.

Archaea: NO peptidoglycan (pseudopeptidoglycan or other walls). Ether-linked branched isoprenoid lipids — the unique molecular signature. Met start codon (like Eukarya). Multiple RNA polymerases (like Eukarya). Often have histones (like Eukarya). Found in extremes: thermophiles, halophiles, methanogens.

Eukarya: membrane-bound nucleus + organelles. 80S cytoplasmic ribosomes (70S in mito/chloroplast — endosymbiotic clue). Linear chromosomes wrapped in histones. Introns common.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Archaea = bacteria." No — molecularly closer to Eukarya. Domain difference, not kingdom.
  • "Antibiotics work on Archaea like bacteria." Most don't — Archaea lack peptidoglycan + have different ribosomal targets.
  • "Eukaryotes are always multicellular." Yeast + protists are unicellular eukaryotes.
See also Prokaryote vs eukaryote · mitochondria's 70S ribosomes + circular DNA support endosymbiotic origin from a bacterial ancestor.

Section 04 · Diversity of Life

Plants: angio vs gymno, monocot vs dicot, transport, C3/C4/CAM.

Plants alternate between haploid (gametophyte) and diploid (sporophyte) phases. Bryophytes: gametophyte dominant. Vascular plants: sporophyte dominant. Gymnosperms = naked seeds (cones). Angiosperms = flowers + fruit. Xylem moves water UP via cohesion-tension. Phloem moves sugar via pressure-flow. C3 vs C4 vs CAM differ in CO2 capture strategy.

The trap

Monocot vs dicot at-a-glance: monocot = 1 cotyledon, parallel veins, scattered vascular bundles, fibrous roots, flower parts in 3s. Dicot = 2 cotyledons, net veins, ringed vascular bundles, taproot, flower parts in 4s/5s. C4 plants AREN'T just better — they're an adaptation to HOT, dry conditions. C4 has anatomical separation (Kranz); CAM has temporal separation (night vs day).

The rule

Xylem = dead, hollow. Pulls water passively via transpiration → cohesion-tension. Phloem = living sieve cells. Active loading at source → high osmotic pressure → flow to sink. C3 = standard Calvin (most plants). C4 = preconcentrate CO2 in bundle sheath (corn, sugarcane). CAM = open stomata at NIGHT, store malate (cacti, succulents).

The move

For monocot/dicot: count cotyledons, look at veins/roots. For photosynthesis variant: hot/dry biome → suspect C4 or CAM. For transport: water pulled UP, sugar pushed FROM source.

Xylem = up (water). Phloem = source to sink (sugar).

From CoachCoach notes · angio/gymno, monocot/dicot, photosynthesis variants

Plants solved water + carbon problems many ways.

Major plant groups (in evolutionary order):

  • Bryophytes (mosses, liverworts): no vascular tissue. Gametophyte (n) dominant. Need water for sperm to swim.
  • Pteridophytes (ferns): vascular but no seeds. Sporophyte (2n) dominant. Still need water for fertilization.
  • Gymnosperms (conifers, ginkgo): naked seeds in cones. Pollen carries sperm via wind.
  • Angiosperms (flowering plants): flowers + fruit, double fertilization (one sperm + egg → embryo, other + 2 polar nuclei → 3n endosperm).

Monocot vs dicot:

  • Monocot: 1 cotyledon, parallel leaf veins, scattered vascular bundles in stem, fibrous roots, flower parts in MULTIPLES OF 3. Examples: grasses, lilies, palms, corn.
  • Dicot: 2 cotyledons, net (reticulate) veins, ringed bundles in stem, taproot, flower parts in 4s or 5s. Examples: roses, oaks, beans, sunflowers.

Transport — xylem (water UP): dead tracheids/vessel elements form continuous tubes. Cohesion-tension theory: transpiration at leaves pulls water up via H-bond cohesion + adhesion to xylem walls. Negative pressure (tension) — like a straw.

Transport — phloem (sugar source-to-sink): sieve tube elements (living, no nucleus, supported by companion cells). Pressure-flow: active loading of sucrose at source (leaf) → water follows osmotically → high pressure → bulk flow → unloading at sink (root, fruit).

Photosynthesis variants — all use Calvin cycle (CO2 → glucose), but differ in CO2 capture:

  • C3 (~85% of plants): RuBisCO directly fixes CO2 in mesophyll. Problem: in heat, stomata close → low CO2 → RuBisCO fixes O2 instead → photorespiration (wasteful).
  • C4 (corn, sugarcane, ~3%): CO2 first fixed by PEP carboxylase in mesophyll → 4-carbon malate → shipped to BUNDLE SHEATH cells (Kranz anatomy) → CO2 released for RuBisCO. Spatial separation.
  • CAM (cacti, pineapple, succulents): stomata open at NIGHT → CO2 fixed as malate → stored in vacuoles. Day: stomata close, malate releases CO2 for Calvin cycle. Temporal separation.

Plant hormones: auxin (IAA — apical dominance, phototropism, low conc → roots), gibberellins (stem elongation, seed germination), cytokinins (cell division, anti-senescence), abscisic acid (stomatal closure, dormancy), ethylene (fruit ripening, leaf abscission — gas).

Common student misconceptions
  • "Xylem actively pumps water up." Xylem cells are DEAD — no active transport. Cohesion-tension is passive (driven by transpiration).
  • "CAM and C4 are the same." C4 = SPATIAL separation (Kranz anatomy). CAM = TEMPORAL separation (night CO2 capture).
  • "Gymnosperms have flowers." No — gymnosperms have CONES. Flowers are exclusive to angiosperms.
  • "Endosperm is diploid." In angiosperms, endosperm is TRIPLOID (3n) from 1 sperm + 2 polar nuclei.
See also Cell Lab · Photosynthesis · C3/C4/CAM differences pivot on the same Calvin cycle from photosynthesis.

Section 04 · Diversity of Life

Animals: protostome vs deuterostome, major phyla.

Animals are multicellular heterotrophs without cell walls. Major split: protostomes (mouth first, spiral cleavage, schizocoely — most invertebrates) vs deuterostomes (anus first, radial cleavage, enterocoely — echinoderms + chordates). Body plans go from no symmetry → radial → bilateral; no tissue → tissues; no body cavity → coelom.

The trap

Mouth-first naming is counterintuitive: protostomes form the MOUTH first from the blastopore (proto = first; stoma = mouth). Deuterostomes form the ANUS first. Echinoderms (despite radial adult symmetry) are deuterostomes — they have bilateral larvae.

The rule

Protostome: blastopore → mouth. Spiral cleavage. Schizocoely (coelom forms from splitting mesoderm). Includes molluscs, annelids, arthropods, flatworms. Deuterostome: blastopore → anus. Radial cleavage. Enterocoely (coelom forms from gut pouches). Includes echinoderms, chordates.

The move

For phylum questions: ID symmetry, body plan, segmentation, cleavage type. Echinoderms = deuterostome despite adult radial.

Proto = mouth first. Deutero = anus first.

From CoachCoach notes · phyla and body plan splits

Body plans evolved in stages — track them on the tree.

Major animal phyla (rough phylogenetic order):

  • Porifera (sponges): no symmetry, no tissues, no organs. Filter feeders. Choanocytes.
  • Cnidaria (jellyfish, hydra, corals): radial symmetry, true tissues. Polyp + medusa. Cnidocytes (stinging cells). Diploblastic (2 germ layers).
  • Platyhelminthes (flatworms, planaria, tapeworms): bilateral, acoelomate, gastrovascular cavity (1 opening = mouth/anus same).
  • Nematoda (roundworms): bilateral, pseudocoelomate, complete digestive tract. Often parasites.
  • Mollusca (snails, clams, octopus): coelomate, mantle + foot + visceral mass. Some highly cephalized.
  • Annelida (segmented worms — earthworms, leeches): coelomate, segmented body.
  • Arthropoda (insects, crustaceans, arachnids): coelomate, segmented, jointed appendages, exoskeleton (chitin), open circulation. Most diverse phylum.
  • Echinodermata (sea stars, urchins): deuterostome, water vascular system, tube feet, adult radial / larval bilateral.
  • Chordata: notochord, dorsal nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, post-anal tail (at some stage). Includes vertebrates.

Protostome vs deuterostome split (key DAT topic):

  • Cleavage: proto = spiral (cells offset). Deutero = radial (cells stacked).
  • Cell fate: proto = determinate (cell fate set early). Deutero = indeterminate (cells totipotent — twins possible).
  • Coelom formation: proto = schizocoely (split in mesoderm). Deutero = enterocoely (gut outpocketing).
  • Blastopore fate: proto = mouth. Deutero = anus.

Body cavity types:

  • Acoelomate: no cavity (flatworms).
  • Pseudocoelomate: cavity present, but mesoderm doesn't fully line it (roundworms).
  • True coelomate: cavity fully lined by mesoderm (most "advanced" phyla).

Germ layers:

  • Diploblastic (cnidarians): ectoderm + endoderm only.
  • Triploblastic (most): ecto + meso + endo.

Vertebrate trends: jaws (gnathostomes), tetrapods (4 limbs onto land), amniotic egg (reptiles, mammals), endothermy (birds + mammals).

Common student misconceptions
  • "Echinoderms are protostomes because they have radial symmetry." They are DEUTEROSTOMES — radial adult is a derived feature; larvae are bilateral.
  • "Sponges have tissues." Porifera are unique among animals in having NO true tissues.
  • "All worms are in the same phylum." Three different phyla: Platyhelminthes (flat), Nematoda (round), Annelida (segmented).
  • "Arthropods have endoskeletons." Arthropods have EXOSKELETONS (chitin). They must molt to grow.

Section 05 · Diversity

Vertebrate classes: five living groups, four key innovations.

Chordata includes invertebrate chordates (tunicates, lancelets) and Vertebrata. Within Vertebrata, five major living classes: jawless fish (lampreys, hagfish), cartilaginous fish (sharks, rays — Chondrichthyes), bony fish (Osteichthyes), amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. Four big evolutionary innovations distinguish them: jaws, tetrapod limbs, amniotic egg, and endothermy + hair/feathers.

The trap

Students miss that amniotic egg is the key innovation that lets reptiles, birds, and mammals reproduce on dry land (amphibians still need water for eggs). They also confuse cartilaginous (sharks) vs bony (most fish) skeletons.

The rule

Innovations stack: jaws (jawed fish onward) → tetrapod limbs (amphibians onward) → amniotic egg (reptiles onward) → endothermy + hair/feathers (birds and mammals diverged from reptilian ancestors). All vertebrates share the chordate features (notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, post-anal tail) at some life stage.

The move

For any vertebrate, identify which innovations it has. Shark = jaws, no limbs (still aquatic), no amnion. Frog = jaws + tetrapod limbs, no amnion (eggs need water). Lizard = jaws + limbs + amnion (true land vertebrate). Bird/mammal = all four + endothermy + hair or feathers.

Innovations stack: jaws → tetrapod limbs → amniotic egg → endothermy + hair/feathers.

From CoachCoach notes · the five vertebrate classes + their innovations

Each innovation opens a new niche.

Chordate features (all vertebrates have these at some life stage): notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, post-anal tail, endostyle/thyroid. In adult vertebrates, the notochord is replaced by the vertebral column.

1. Jawless fish (Agnatha — lampreys, hagfish): the most primitive living vertebrates. No jaws, no paired fins. Cartilage skeleton. Filter feeders or parasitic.

2. Cartilaginous fish (Chondrichthyes — sharks, rays, skates): first vertebrates with jaws (evolved from gill arches). Cartilage skeleton (no bone). Paired fins. Internal fertilization. Lateral line system for water-pressure sensing.

3. Bony fish (Osteichthyes — salmon, tuna, most fish): bone skeleton. Operculum covers gills. Swim bladder for buoyancy (not in cartilaginous). Most numerous vertebrate group.

4. Amphibians (frogs, salamanders, caecilians): first vertebrates with tetrapod limbs. Moist skin used for gas exchange. Eggs MUST be laid in water (no amnion to prevent dessication). Larval stage (tadpole) is aquatic; adult is often terrestrial.

5. Reptiles (turtles, lizards, snakes, crocodilians): first vertebrates with amniotic egg (yolk sac, amnion, chorion, allantois) → can reproduce on land. Keratin scales prevent water loss. Ectothermic ("cold-blooded"). Internal fertilization.

6. Birds (Aves): evolved from reptilian (theropod dinosaur) ancestors. Endothermic ("warm-blooded"). Feathers (modified scales, used for insulation + flight). Hollow bones, air sacs, four-chambered heart, beak (no teeth in modern birds).

7. Mammals: also evolved from reptilian ancestors (separately from birds). Endothermic. Hair/fur (insulation). Mammary glands (milk for young). Diaphragm. Three middle-ear bones. Heterodont teeth (different shapes for different functions). Mostly viviparous (live birth) except monotremes (egg-laying: platypus, echidna).

Common student misconceptions
  • "Sharks have bones." No — cartilage. Distinguishes them from bony fish.
  • "All fish are 'cold-blooded' and all mammals are 'warm-blooded'." Most fish are ectothermic, but tuna and some sharks are partially endothermic. All mammals are endothermic.
  • "Amphibians have an amniotic egg." No — that's why they need water to reproduce. Reptiles, birds, and mammals are amniotes.
  • "Birds are not reptiles." Cladistically, birds ARE reptiles — they descended from theropod dinosaurs. Modern classification often groups Reptilia + Aves together as Reptilia (or Sauropsida).
  • "All mammals give live birth." No — monotremes (platypus, echidnas) lay eggs.
See also Animal diversity Phylogenetics · vertebrates are a subgroup within phylum Chordata.

Section 04 · Diversity of Life

Phylogenetics: monophyletic, parsimony, synapomorphy.

Trees show evolutionary relationships. Clades are groups including a common ancestor + ALL descendants (monophyletic). Bad groups: paraphyletic = ancestor + SOME descendants; polyphyletic = no recent common ancestor. Synapomorphies (shared derived characters) define clades. Parsimony = fewest changes wins.

The trap

"Reptiles" as traditionally defined are PARAPHYLETIC (excludes birds, who descend from theropod dinos). True monophyletic version = "Sauropsida" or include birds. Just because two species look similar doesn't mean they're a clade — could be convergent evolution (homoplasy).

The rule

Monophyletic: ancestor + all descendants. Paraphyletic: ancestor + SOME (excludes some descendants). Polyphyletic: NO recent common ancestor included. Synapomorphy: shared derived trait (defines clade). Symplesiomorphy: shared ancestral. Homoplasy: convergent (analogous, not homologous).

The move

For tree Qs: trace from each tip back to common ancestor. If you can draw a line around them with everyone inside the circle = monophyletic. For character: derived in this group only = synapomorphy.

Clade = ancestor + all descendants.

From CoachCoach notes · tree thinking

Trees encode common ancestry. Read tips, not topology.

Tree anatomy:

  • Tip (terminal taxon): a species or group at the end of a branch.
  • Node: where a branch splits — represents a common ancestor.
  • Branch: a lineage through time.
  • Root: earliest ancestor; trees can be rooted or unrooted.
  • Sister taxa: share an immediate common ancestor.

Group classifications (relative to a tree):

  • Monophyletic (clade): includes a common ancestor AND ALL its descendants. Mammals are monophyletic.
  • Paraphyletic: ancestor + SOME descendants. "Reptiles" (excluding birds) is paraphyletic. "Fish" (excluding tetrapods) is paraphyletic.
  • Polyphyletic: grouped by feature but lacking recent common ancestor. "Warm-blooded vertebrates" (mammals + birds) is polyphyletic — they evolved endothermy independently.

Character types:

  • Synapomorphy (shared derived): trait shared by all members of a clade, inherited from their most recent common ancestor. Defines the clade. E.g., feathers in birds.
  • Symplesiomorphy (shared ancestral): trait shared but inherited from a more distant ancestor — doesn't help define the clade. E.g., having a backbone in primates.
  • Autapomorphy: derived trait unique to one species/lineage. Doesn't help group.
  • Homoplasy (analogous): similar trait independently evolved (convergence). Bird wings + bat wings + insect wings.

Parsimony: the most likely tree is the one requiring the FEWEST evolutionary changes. Used when no other info available. Maximum likelihood + Bayesian methods are statistical alternatives that consider rates of change.

Molecular clocks: assume constant mutation rate. Genetic distance ≈ time. Useful for divergence time estimates. Calibrated against fossils.

Cladistics workflow:

  • List taxa + characters.
  • Polarize characters (ancestral vs derived) using outgroup comparison.
  • Build candidate trees.
  • Pick most parsimonious (or maximum likelihood).

Reading tip: rotating a node's branches doesn't change relationships. The same tree can look different with branches flipped. Look at NODES, not left-right order of tips.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Reptiles (excluding birds) is a clade." It's PARAPHYLETIC. Birds descended from theropod dinosaurs — excluding them breaks the clade.
  • "Closer on a tree = more similar in appearance." Trees show ancestry, not similarity. Convergent evolution can make distantly related species look alike.
  • "Synapomorphy and symplesiomorphy define clades." Only synapomorphies (shared DERIVED) define clades. Symplesiomorphies are too ancestral to be useful.
  • "Most parsimonious tree is always correct." Just most likely under the assumption of fewest changes. Real evolution can take longer paths.

Section 04 · Diversity of Life

Bacterial gene transfer: conjugation, transformation, transduction.

Bacteria transfer genetic material three ways without sexual reproduction: conjugation (F factor pilus, direct cell contact), transformation (uptake of free DNA from environment), and transduction (bacteriophage carries DNA between cells). Each mechanism can spread genes — including antibiotic resistance — within a population. Binary fission (the default) produces genetically identical clones.

The trap

Generalized transduction can transfer ANY gene (random packaging). Specialized transduction transfers only genes ADJACENT to the phage integration site. Students also confuse conjugation with sexual reproduction — no fusion of cells, no meiosis, no combining of two complete genomes. Only one-way DNA transfer through the pilus.

The rule

Conjugation: F+ (or Hfr) → F- via pilus. F+ × F-: transfers F plasmid, F- becomes F+. Hfr × F-: transfers chromosomal genes (high freq), rarely transfers full F (F- stays F-). Transformation: competent cell uptakes free DNA. Transduction: phage vector. Generalized = any gene; specialized = adjacent gene only.

The move

On the DAT: identify the mechanism from the scenario. Phage involved? Transduction. Free DNA in medium? Transformation. Pilus contact? Conjugation. Check if transfer is general (any gene) or specific (adjacent to integration site).

From CoachCoach notes · horizontal gene transfer and bacterial genetics

Bacteria evolve fast because of horizontal gene transfer.

Binary fission (baseline): bacterial asexual reproduction. One cell → two identical daughter cells. No meiosis. Involves chromosome replication + septum formation. Very fast (E. coli: ~20 min doubling time). All offspring are clones (barring mutation). Binary fission is NOT gene transfer — it just copies existing genes.

Conjugation:

  • F+ × F-: F plasmid encodes pilus. F+ extends pilus to F-, forms conjugation channel. Rolling circle replication transfers one strand of F plasmid → F- becomes F+. Chromosomal genes rarely transferred.
  • Hfr × F-: F factor integrates into chromosome (Hfr = high frequency recombination strain). On conjugation, chromosome transfer begins at oriT, proceeds sequentially. Genes are transferred in time-order from the origin (earliest = closest to oriT). Full transfer takes ~100 min in E. coli; rarely completes → F- usually stays F- (F genes are at the trailing end).
  • Gene mapping: interrupt mating at specific times, test which alleles transferred. Time of entry = map distance. 1 minute ≈ 20 kb ≈ map position. Classic Jacob-Wollman "blender" experiment.

Transformation:

  • Free DNA released from lysed cells is taken up by competent cells. Competence = cell physiological state allowing DNA uptake (regulated by ComK, stress signals, quorum sensing). Griffith (1928): heat-killed smooth S pneumococcus + live rough R → live smooth = first evidence of a "transforming principle." Avery, MacLeod, McCarty (1944): identified transforming principle as DNA.
  • Can be induced artificially (electroporation, CaCl2 treatment + heat shock) — basis of all modern recombinant DNA work.

Transduction:

  • Generalized: lytic phage (e.g., P1) accidentally packages a random segment of bacterial chromosome instead of phage DNA. Any gene can be transferred. The transducing phage injects bacterial DNA into the next host; if homologous sequences recombine, the gene integrates.
  • Specialized: temperate phage (e.g., lambda) integrates at a specific attachment site (attB). Imprecise excision picks up flanking bacterial genes (e.g., gal or bio genes flanking attB). Only these adjacent genes transfer. Same host every time.

Plasmids: circular extrachromosomal DNA elements. Carry genes (antibiotic resistance, toxins, virulence factors). Replicate independently of chromosome. Conjugative plasmids (like F) self-transfer; non-conjugative plasmids need mobilization by a conjugative element. R (resistance) plasmids are major vehicles for antibiotic resistance spread in hospitals.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Conjugation = bacterial sex." It is not true sexual reproduction — no fusion of full genomes, no recombination of both parents' entire DNA. One-way DNA transfer through a pilus.
  • "Hfr × F- makes the recipient an Hfr." Rarely — because the F genes (trailing end) almost never finish transferring. F- usually remains F-.
  • "Transduction always transfers any gene." Only generalized transduction transfers random genes. Specialized transduction transfers only genes adjacent to the phage integration site.
  • "Transformation requires another cell." No — transformation only requires free DNA in the environment and a competent recipient. No donor cell is needed.
