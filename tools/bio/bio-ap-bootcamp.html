Fetal Circulation — The 3 Shunts

Umbilical vein carries OXYGENATED blood (from placenta to fetus). Umbilical arteries (×2) carry deoxygenated blood from fetus to placenta. This is reversed from the adult rule — memorize it explicitly.
⚠ Erythroblastosis Fetalis (Rh Incompatibility)
Rh– mother + Rh+ fetus: During labor, fetal Rh+ blood enters mother → mother develops anti-Rh antibodies. First pregnancy is fine. Second Rh+ pregnancy: maternal antibodies (IgG — the only Ig to cross placenta) attack fetal RBCs. Treated with Rh immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) after first delivery to prevent antibody formation.
🧠 Lymphatic System — Key Facts
Lymph = interstitial fluid + bacteria + fats + proteins not reabsorbed by venules
Moves via skeletal muscle contraction + smooth muscle peristalsis + valves (no heart pump)
Spleen: filters blood, destroys old RBCs, stores lymphocytes
Thymus: maturation site for T cells
Oncotic pressure (plasma proteins like albumin) retains fluid in capillaries — low albumin → edema

Diaphragm Mechanics

  • Active process — requires energy

  • Thoracic cavity volume decreases

🧠 "Contracts = Comes In"
Diaphragm CONTRACTS → air COMES IN (inhale). Diaphragm RELAXES → air goes OUT (exhale).
Boyle's Law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Increasing volume decreases pressure → air flows in to equalize.

Bicarbonate Buffer System

🧠 "BiC for Blood, Phos for Cell"
Bicarbonate = primary blood buffer (extracellular). Phosphate = primary intracellular buffer. Carbonic anhydrase (inside RBCs) catalyzes the CO₂ + H₂O reaction — the enzyme is inside the red blood cell, not the plasma.
▶ DAT Tip — Breathing Control
The medulla oblongata controls breathing automatically. The primary trigger is rising CO₂ (detected as falling pH) — NOT falling O₂. Peripheral chemoreceptors in carotid and aortic bodies detect O₂ changes as a secondary signal.

🧠 Bohr Effect vs. Haldane Effect
Bohr Effect: High CO₂/low pH → hemoglobin releases O₂ (delivers O₂ to active tissues)
Haldane Effect: Low O₂ → hemoglobin picks up MORE CO₂ (efficient CO₂ removal from tissues)
These effects work together at the tissue level.

CO₂ Transport & the Chloride Shift

CO₂ Transport — 3 Forms (Tissues → Lungs)
①
~70% as Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻): CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (via carbonic anhydrase in RBCs). HCO₃⁻ exits RBC; Cl⁻ enters = chloride shift. H⁺ stays in RBC → lowers pH → Bohr effect → O₂ released.
②
~23% bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin (CO₂ binds globin chains, not heme).
③
~7% dissolved in plasma as free CO₂.
CO poisoning: Carbon monoxide (CO) has ~200× higher affinity for hemoglobin than O₂ → binds irreversibly → blocks O₂ transport. Treatment: 100% O₂ or hyperbaric O₂ to competitively displace CO.

03 · The Muscular System
Sliding Filament Model & Motor Units
⏱ 20 minutes
The sliding filament model's TRUE first step — Ca²⁺ binding to troponin — is the most commonly missed question in this section. Know every step in sequence.
🧠 "Ca²⁺ Triggers My Actin, Power Arrives!"
Ca²⁺ → Troponin → Tropomyosin moves → Myosin binds Actin → Power stroke → ATP detaches myosin
The TRUE first step is Ca²⁺ binding TROPONIN (not myosin binding actin). Tropomyosin is the blocker. Without Ca²⁺, tropomyosin covers the site and the cycle cannot start.
Cross-Bridge Cycle — 6 Steps
1
Action potential arrives → Ca²⁺ released from sarcoplasmic reticulum into the cytosol.
2
Ca²⁺ binds troponin ★ → troponin shifts → pulls tropomyosin OFF the actin myosin-binding sites.
3
Myosin heads bind exposed actin sites → cross-bridge forms.
4
POWER STROKE: myosin pivots, sliding actin toward the M line; ADP + Pi released.
5
New ATP binds myosin → myosin releases actin. No ATP = RIGOR MORTIS.
6
ATP hydrolyzed (→ ADP + Pi) → myosin head re-cocks. Cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ is present.
⚠ Rigor Mortis
After death, ATP runs out. Myosin binds actin but cannot release without ATP → muscles become permanently rigid. This is rigor mortis. It is direct evidence that ATP is needed for detachment, not attachment.
▶ DAT Tip — Actin & Myosin in Cytokinesis
Actin and myosin are not exclusive to muscle cells. During cytokinesis (cell division), actin and myosin II form a contractile ring that pinches the cell in two. If actin/myosin malfunction, cytokinesis fails.

04 · Skeletal System & Skin
Bones, Connections & Epidermis Layers
⏱ 15 minutes
The skeletal and integumentary systems hand you some of the most predictable points on the DAT — but only if you don't fall for the classic traps. Lock in the connective-tissue definitions, the deep→superficial epidermis order, and the bone-cell trio, and you bank easy questions.

Connective Tissues — Ligament vs Tendon vs Cartilage

Three connective tissues hold the skeleton together, and the DAT tests them constantly. A ligament connects bone to bone (stabilizes joints). A tendon connects muscle to bone (transmits the pull of contraction). Cartilage is the smooth, avascular tissue that cushions joints and serves as the template for many bones.
🧠 Memory Hook
"Ligament Locks bones together; Tendon Tugs on bone." A ligament joins two bones at a joint; a tendon connects a muscle (the "puller") to the bone it moves. Same alliteration both times: Ligament–Lock, Tendon–Tug.
TissueConnectsJob
LigamentBone ↔ BoneStabilizes joints (e.g., the ACL — anterior cruciate ligament — links the femur to the tibia in the knee)
TendonMuscle ↔ BoneTransmits muscular force to move the bone (e.g., Achilles tendon)
CartilageCushions / models boneSmooth low-friction surface; avascular → heals slowly
⚠️ The #1 reversed-definition trap. Many practice tests (and careless answer keys) flip these two — calling a tendon "bone-to-bone" or a ligament "muscle-to-bone." They are backwards. Anchor on the correct version: Ligament = bone-to-bone, Tendon = muscle-to-bone. The ACL ends in "Ligament" precisely because it joins two bones (femur ↔ tibia).

Key Bones of the Arm & Leg

The DAT expects you to know the major limb bones and, critically, which is lateral vs medial. "Medial" means closer to the body's midline; "lateral" means farther from it (in standard anatomical position, palms forward).
Arm (Upper Limb)
Humerus (upper arm) → Radius (forearm, lateral — thumb side) + Ulna (forearm, medial — pinky side) → Carpals (wrist) → Metacarpals (palm) → Phalanges (fingers).
Leg (Lower Limb)
Femur (thigh, longest bone in the body) → Patella (kneecap) → Tibia (shin, medial — weight-bearing) + Fibula (lateral, thin) → Tarsals (ankle) → Metatarsals (foot) → Phalanges (toes).
🔑 Lateral vs Medial Anchors
Radius = lateral (thumb side) — it radiates outward; Ulna = medial (pinky side). Tibia = medial (the thick shin bone you can feel) — "Tibia is the Thick one"; Fibula = lateral and slender. Note both arm and leg end in phalanges, and both have the same -als pattern (carpals/metacarpals vs tarsals/metatarsals).

Ossification & Bone Tissue Types

Endochondral Ossification
Bone forms from a cartilage model first, which is gradually replaced by bone. Builds most bones, including all long bones (e.g., femur). "Endo-chondral = within cartilage."
Intramembranous Ossification
Bone forms directly within a fibrous membrane — no cartilage intermediate. Builds flat bones like the skull and clavicle.
Cortical (Compact) Bone
Dense outer layer. Organized into osteons (Haversian systems) — concentric rings of matrix around a central canal carrying vessels/nerves. Provides strength.
Cancellous (Spongy) Bone
Porous inner bone made of a lattice of trabeculae. Houses red marrow (hematopoiesis); lighter, found in the epiphyses.
⚠️ Cartilage Heals Slowly
Unlike bone (which is richly vascularized), cartilage is avascular — it has no blood supply. Nutrients must diffuse in slowly, so cartilage injuries (and the avascular cartilage growth plate) heal slowly. This is a frequently tested contrast.

The Integumentary System — Skin Layers

Skin has two main layers: a superficial epidermis (avascular epithelium) sitting on top of the deeper dermis (connective tissue). The epidermis is what the DAT loves to quiz with its five strata.

⚠️ Stratum lucidum trap. The lucidum layer is found in thick (glabrous) skin only — the palms and soles. Thin skin everywhere else has just four epidermal layers (no lucidum). If a question describes ordinary skin and lists all five layers, lucidum is the wrong inclusion.
⚠️ Epidermis vs Dermis
The epidermis is avascular (no blood vessels — it's epithelium fed by diffusion). All the "active" structures — blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, and sweat/oil glands — live in the deeper dermis, which is connective tissue, not epidermis. Don't assign dermal structures to the epidermis.

05 · The Renal System
Nephron, ADH & Kidney Filtration
⏱ 20 minutes
The kidney is a filtration and fine-tuning machine: it strips the blood down to a watery filtrate, then claws back almost everything worth keeping. DAT questions live and die on two things — knowing which nephron segment does which job, and knowing the two master hormones (ADH for water, aldosterone for sodium/blood pressure). Lock in the four processes, the segment-by-segment table, and the RAAS cascade and this is one of the most reliably scorable sections on the exam.

The 4 Kidney Processes — In Order

Every drop of urine is built by four sequential processes. Memorize the order with "Frequently Resting Students Excel": Filtration → Reabsorption → Secretion → Excretion.

What Each Process Does
1
Filtration — blood pressure pushes water + small solutes from the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule. Non-selective (driven by pressure, not choice).
2
Reabsorption — useful substances (glucose, amino acids, ions, water) move from the filtrate back into the blood (peritubular capillaries).
3
Secretion — wastes & excess ions (H⁺, K⁺, drugs, NH₃) move from the blood into the filtrate. Fine-tunes pH & ion balance.
4
Excretion — whatever remains leaves the body as urine.
🧠 The Master Equation
Urine = Filtration − Reabsorption + Secretion. A substance ends up in urine if it is filtered (or secreted) and not reabsorbed. Reabsorption subtracts from urine (returns to blood); secretion adds to urine.

The Nephron — Pathway of the Filtrate ★ Highest-Yield

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney. Filtrate flows: glomerulus / Bowman's capsule → PCT → descending limb → ascending limb → DCT → collecting duct. Two zones matter: the cortex (outer — holds glomeruli, PCT, DCT) and the medulla (inner — holds the loops of Henle and collecting ducts, and houses the salty osmotic gradient).

⚠️ Renal Threshold & Glucosuria
The PCT normally reabsorbs 100% of glucose — so normal urine has NO glucose. But glucose transporters have a ceiling (transport maximum, Tm). If blood glucose rises above the renal threshold (~180 mg/dL, as in untreated diabetes mellitus), transporters saturate and the excess glucose spills into the urine (glucosuria). Glucose in urine = renal threshold exceeded = think diabetes.
Descending = water out; ascending = salt out (NOT water). This is the #1 loop-of-Henle trap. The descending limb is permeable to water only (filtrate concentrates as water leaves). The ascending limb actively pumps out solutes and is impermeable to water (filtrate dilutes). The salt dumped by the ascending limb is what makes the deep medulla salty — the countercurrent gradient that lets the collecting duct concentrate urine later.
🔑 Cortex vs. Medulla (+ Vasa Recta)
Cortex (outer) = glomeruli, PCT, DCT. Medulla (inner) = loops of Henle + collecting ducts, and holds the high-salt osmotic gradient. The vasa recta are the long capillaries that dive alongside the loop of Henle; they supply the medulla without washing out the salt gradient (countercurrent exchange).

The Two Master Hormones — ADH vs. Aldosterone

ADH (Vasopressin) — WATER
Made in the hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary. Acts on the collecting duct: inserts aquaporins → reabsorbs water → small volume of concentrated urine. Released when you're dehydrated (high blood osmolarity). "ADH = Add water back."
Aldosterone — SODIUM / BP
A steroid from the adrenal cortex. Acts on the DCT / collecting duct: reabsorb Na⁺ (water follows) and secrete K⁺. Net effect: raises blood volume & blood pressure. Aldosterone moves salt; ADH moves water.
Alcohol INHIBITS ADH. Less ADH → fewer aquaporins → collecting duct can't reabsorb water → large volume of dilute urine → dehydration (the reason for the hangover). Caffeine has a similar diuretic effect. Diuretic = makes you pee = blocks/opposes ADH.

🔑 The Kidney Is More Than a Filter
Beyond making urine, the kidney: (1) secretes erythropoietin (EPO) → stimulates red-blood-cell production in bone marrow (low O₂ → more EPO); (2) regulates blood pH (reabsorbs HCO₃⁻, secretes H⁺); and (3) regulates blood pressure (via RAAS & water balance). It also activates vitamin D.

Normal urine contains NO glucose, NO protein, and NO blood cells. Glucose → think diabetes (renal threshold exceeded). Two Key Intestinal Hormones — Secretin vs. CCK ★ High-Yield

When acidic chyme enters the duodenum, the small intestine releases two hormones that coordinate the pancreas and gallbladder. The DAT tests them as a contrasting pair — match the trigger to the response.
Secretin
Trigger: acidic chyme (low pH) entering the duodenum. Response: pancreas releases bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) to neutralize the acid, protecting the intestinal lining. Secretin → bicarbonate; think "secrete base."
CCK (Cholecystokinin)
Trigger: fats & proteins in the duodenum. Response: pancreas releases digestive enzymes + gallbladder contracts to release bile; also slows gastric emptying. "Cholecysto-kinin" literally = move the gallbladder.
🧠 Memory Key — Secretin vs. CCK
Secretin responds to acid → sends bicarbonate (neutralize). CCK responds to fat/protein → sends enzymes + bile (digest). Acid = Secretin/Base; Fat = CCK/Bile.

Motility — Peristalsis vs. Segmentation

Rhythmic waves of smooth-muscle contraction that push food forward along the tract (esophagus → anus). Moves food onward.
Segmentation — MIXING
Localized back-and-forth contractions that mix chyme with enzymes and maximize contact with the absorptive wall — without net forward movement.

Large Intestine (Colon)

The large intestine secretes no digestive enzymes. Its jobs are water & electrolyte absorption (hardening waste into feces), feces storage in the rectum, and housing the gut microbiome.
🔑 Gut Bacteria & Vitamins

The large intestine itself secretes no enzymes — chemical digestion is essentially done by the time chyme arrives.

07 · Blood & Immunity
Blood Types, Immune Defense & Antibodies
⏱ 20 minutes
Blood typing and immunity are pure point-banks on the DAT: the rules are fixed, the traps are predictable, and the questions reward memorization over reasoning. Lock in the ABO antigen/antibody logic (especially the O−/AB+ universal donor & recipient nuance), the three lines of defense, the five immunoglobulins, and the MHC-I/MHC-II → CD8/CD4 split, and you will answer nearly every immune question on test day in seconds.
🎯
Why This Matters on the DAT
The DAT loves "who can donate to whom" transfusion questions and "which antibody does X" matching. The single most-missed point is forgetting the Rh factor: the true universal donor is O-negative and the true universal recipient is AB-positive. Pair that with the MHC-I → CD8 / MHC-II → CD4 split and the IgM-vs-IgG primary/secondary distinction, and you cover the bulk of immune testing.

ABO Blood Types

Red blood cells (erythrocytes) carry antigens (sugars/proteins) on their surface that mark blood as a certain type. The plasma carries antibodies against the antigens the body lacks — so your immune system will never attack your own cells, but it will clump (agglutinate) any transfused blood carrying a foreign antigen. The golden rule: your body makes antibodies against any antigen it does NOT have.

The TRUE universal donor is O-NEGATIVE and the TRUE universal recipient is AB-POSITIVE — once you count the Rh factor. "Type O" alone isn't enough: O-positive cells still carry the Rh antigen and would be flagged by an Rh− recipient. Only O− has no A, B, or Rh surface antigens, so nothing on its cells can trigger a reaction. Likewise AB+ has A, B, and Rh antigens, so it already "owns" every antigen it could receive and makes no antibodies against any of them. On the DAT, if Rh is in the answer choices, pick O− / AB+.

Rh Factor & Erythroblastosis Fetalis

The Rhesus (Rh) factor is a separate surface antigen: you either have it (Rh+) or you don't (Rh−). An Rh+ donor cannot give to an Rh− recipient, because the Rh+ cells carry an antigen the Rh− person will attack. Unlike anti-A/anti-B antibodies (which are present from birth), anti-Rh antibodies form only after an Rh− person is first exposed to Rh+ blood.
⚠ Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn (Erythroblastosis Fetalis)
Setup: an Rh− mother carrying an Rh+ fetus. The first pregnancy is usually fine — but at birth, fetal Rh+ blood mixes with maternal blood and the mother becomes sensitized, producing anti-Rh IgG. In a subsequent Rh+ pregnancy, that maternal IgG crosses the placenta (IgG is the only antibody that can) and attacks fetal RBCs → fetal hemolysis. Prevention: RhoGAM (anti-Rh immunoglobulin) given to the mother, which clears fetal Rh+ cells before she can mount her own memory response.

08 · The Endocrine System
FLAT PIG, Posterior Pituitary & Feedback Loops
⏱ 25 minutes
The endocrine system is a DAT goldmine of memorizable, high-yield facts — and a minefield of classic traps. Almost every question reduces to four things: which gland makes the hormone, what it does, whether it's lipid- or water-soluble, and how feedback shuts it off. Nail the FLAT PIG anterior-pituitary set, the posterior-pituitary "made in the hypothalamus" trap, the solubility rule, and the antagonist pairs, and you bank fast, reliable points.
🎯
Why This Matters on the DAT
Endocrine questions are pure recall — there's no reasoning shortcut, so the points go to whoever memorized the chart. The test loves to swap a source gland (ADH is "released by" vs. "made in" the posterior pituitary), flip an antagonist pair (insulin vs. glucagon), or exploit the two amine-derived exceptions (thyroid acts like a steroid; catecholamines act like peptides). Lock the tables below and these become free points.

Hormone Solubility — The "SLIP" Rule ★ High-Yield

Every hormone is either lipid-soluble or water-soluble, and that single property dictates its receptor location, mechanism, and speed. This is the most testable concept in the whole chapter.
LIPID-SOLUBLE → "SLIP through"
  • Steroids (cortisol, aldosterone, sex hormones) + Thyroid (T3/T4)
  • Cross the membrane freely → bind intracellular (cytoplasmic/nuclear) receptors
  • Hormone-receptor complex alters gene transcription
  • Slow, long-lasting effects ("direct stimulation")
  • Need a carrier protein to travel in blood
WATER-SOLUBLE → surface receptors
  • Peptide/protein hormones + Catecholamines (epi/NE)
  • Can't cross the membrane → bind cell-surface receptors (e.g. GPCRs)
  • Trigger second messengers (cAMP, IP₃/DAG, Ca²⁺)
  • Fast effects via signal amplification ("indirect")
  • Dissolve freely in plasma — no carrier needed
🧠 SLIP Mnemonic
Steroids = Lipid → IntraProtein... easier: "SLIP through the membrane." If a hormone is a Steroid (or thyroid), it's Lipid-soluble and SLIPs through the membrane to hit Intracellular receptors and change gene expression — slowly. Everything else (peptides, catecholamines) is water-soluble and works through surface receptors and second messengers — quickly.
⚠ The Two Amine Exceptions (classic trap)
Both thyroid hormones and catecholamines are amino acid–derived (from tyrosine), but they behave on OPPOSITE sides of the rule: Thyroid (T3/T4) is lipid-soluble → crosses the membrane, intracellular receptors, slow (behaves like a steroid). Catecholamines (epinephrine/norepinephrine) are water-soluble → surface receptors, second messengers, fast (behaves like a peptide). If a question calls a hormone "amine-derived," do NOT assume its solubility — check which one.

The Hypothalamus & Pituitary — Two Very Different Lobes

The hypothalamus is the master regulator linking the nervous and endocrine systems. It sits above the pituitary gland (hypophysis), which has two lobes that work in completely different ways — a favorite DAT contrast.
ANTERIOR Pituitary (adenohypophysis)
Makes its own hormones. Connected to the hypothalamus by a hypophyseal portal (blood vessel) system. Hypothalamic releasing hormones travel through the portal veins and tell it what to secrete. Releases the FLAT PIG hormones. Output is mostly tropic (targets other glands).
POSTERIOR Pituitary (neurohypophysis)
Makes NOTHING. It is a direct neuronal extension of the hypothalamus. It only stores & releases two hormones that the hypothalamus synthesized: ADH and oxytocin. Connection is a nerve track, not blood vessels.
⚡ THE #1 ENDOCRINE TRAP: ADH (vasopressin) and oxytocin are MADE in the HYPOTHALAMUS and only stored and released by the posterior pituitary. If a question asks "where is ADH/oxytocin synthesized?" the answer is the hypothalamus — NOT the posterior pituitary. The posterior pituitary is just a storage-and-release dock.

FLAT PIG — Anterior Pituitary Hormones

F L A T — the Tropic Four
  • FSH — follicle growth (♀) / sperm maturation (♂) in the gonads
  • LH — ovulation & corpus luteum (♀) / testosterone via Leydig cells (♂)
  • ACTH — drives the adrenal cortex to release glucocorticoids
  • TSH — drives the thyroid to release T3/T4
P I G — the Direct hormones
  • Prolactin — stimulates milk production (mammary glands)
  • i — just a filler vowel to make the word pronounceable
  • GH (growth hormone) — stimulates body cells to grow & divide
🔑 Accuracy: What "FLAT PiG" Actually Stands For (don't get tripped)
The canonical, most-defensible anterior-pituitary set is six hormones: FSH, LH, ACTH, TSH, Prolactin, GH (four tropic + two direct). In "FLAT PiG," the "i" is just a filler vowel — it doesn't stand for a hormone. MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone) is sometimes tacked on and the "P" occasionally read as covering it, but MSH comes from the pars intermedia (intermediate lobe, vestigial in adult humans), so treat MSH as "sometimes counted," not canonical. If an answer choice lists the six core FLAT PIG hormones, that's the safe pick; don't be thrown if MSH is included or excluded.

Adrenal Gland — Cortex vs. Medulla

Each adrenal gland has two functionally distinct regions. Both handle stress, but on completely different timelines and through different chemistry — a guaranteed compare-and-contrast question.
Adrenal CORTEX
Outer region · long-term stress · driven by ACTH. Makes steroid hormones (lipid-soluble): Cortisol (glucocorticoid — raises blood glucose, suppresses the immune system), Aldosterone (mineralocorticoid — ↑Na⁺ reabsorption → ↑BP), and a small amount of androgens (sex hormones). Mnemonic for the layers: "GFR — salt, sugar, sex."
Adrenal MEDULLA
Inner region · short-term "fight-or-flight" · driven by the sympathetic nervous system (not ACTH). Makes catecholamines (amine-derived, water-soluble): epinephrine & norepinephrine. Effects: ↑heart rate, glycogen breakdown → ↑glucose, vasoconstriction/dilation. Fast on, fast off.
🔑 Quick Discriminator
If the question says steroid, ACTH, cortisol, aldosterone, or "long-term" → cortex. If it says sympathetic, epinephrine, fight-or-flight, or "fast/short-term" → medulla. Bonus embryology trap: the medulla is ectoderm (neural-crest), the cortex is mesoderm.

Thyroid & Parathyroid

The thyroid (largest endocrine gland, in front of the trachea) makes T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 (thyroxine) in response to TSH — these raise metabolic rate and are lipid-soluble. T4 is more abundant and stable but is converted to the more potent T3 in cells. The thyroid's C cells (parafollicular) also make calcitonin (lowers blood Ca²⁺). The separate parathyroid glands make PTH (raises blood Ca²⁺).

(1) The TSH inverse: in hypothyroidism, T3/T4 are LOW but TSH is HIGH — the pituitary keeps shouting "make more!" via negative feedback. So low thyroid hormone ≠ low TSH. (2) Goiter from both: an enlarged thyroid (goiter) occurs in BOTH hyper- and hypothyroidism, and iodine deficiency is the most common cause (iodine is needed to build T3/T4, so the gland enlarges trying to compensate). (3) Solubility: T3/T4 are amine-derived but act like steroids — lipid-soluble, intracellular receptors.

Feedback Loops — Negative vs. Positive

NEGATIVE Feedback (the default)
The output inhibits its own production → promotes stability/homeostasis. This governs most hormones. Example: high testosterone inhibits GnRH → less LH/FSH → less testosterone. Also: high T3/T4 shut off TRH/TSH; high blood glucose → insulin → glucose drops → insulin stops.
POSITIVE Feedback (rare, explosive)
The output amplifies itself → a self-intensifying spiral toward a discrete event. Examples: oxytocin during labor (contractions → cervical stretch → more oxytocin → stronger contractions); blood clotting cascade; the pre-ovulatory estrogen → LH surge.
🧠 How to Tell Them Apart
If the response shuts the stimulus off and keeps a value steady → negative feedback (think thermostat). If the response feeds the stimulus and snowballs toward a one-time endpoint (birth, a clot, ovulation) → positive feedback. The DAT default assumption is negative; positive feedback is the flagged exception.
🔑 Don't Forget the Pineal
The pineal gland secretes melatonin, which sets your circadian rhythm (sleep-wake cycle). It's a one-liner the DAT occasionally tucks into a list of "match the gland to its hormone."

Brain Regions & Their Functions

The brain breaks into the cerebrum (four lobes), the cerebellum, the brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla), and the limbic system (thalamus, hypothalamus, hippocampus, amygdala). The single most testable "life-or-death" fact: damage to the medulla oblongata is fatal because it runs your vital autonomic functions.

The Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)

The BBB is a highly selective filter that protects the brain. It's formed by tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells, reinforced by astrocyte foot processes wrapping the capillaries. The rule for what gets through is simple and high-yield: lipid-soluble / nonpolar molecules cross freely; polar / water-soluble molecules are blocked.
✓ CAN cross (lipid-soluble / nonpolar)
O₂ and CO₂ (gases), alcohol, caffeine, anesthetics, nicotine, steroid hormones. Small, fat-soluble, uncharged molecules slip through the membranes.
✗ CANNOT cross (polar / water-soluble)
Most proteins, most antibiotics, pathogens, ions, large/charged molecules. They're blocked by the tight junctions (no leaky gaps to squeeze through).
🧠 "Lipids Leap, Polars Pause"
If a molecule is nonpolar / lipid-soluble, it leaps across the BBB (that's why caffeine and alcohol affect your brain so fast). If it's polar / water-soluble, it pauses — blocked at the gate. Two builders: tight junctions (endothelial cells) + astrocyte foot processes.

The Action Potential ★ Highest-Yield

A neuron sits at a resting potential of −70 mV, maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase, which pumps 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in (against their gradients, using ATP). An action potential is all-or-none: once threshold is reached it fires at full amplitude every time, or not at all.
Steps of an Action Potential
1
Resting (−70 mV). Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase (3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in) keeps the inside negative; voltage-gated channels closed.
2
Threshold (−55 mV). A stimulus depolarizes the membrane to ~−55 mV → voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open. (Below threshold = no AP.)
3
Depolarization. Na⁺ rushes IN, driving the membrane up toward +30 mV (inside becomes positive).
4
Repolarization. Na⁺ channels inactivate; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open → K⁺ flows OUT, dropping the potential back down.
5
Hyperpolarization (undershoot). K⁺ channels close slowly, so the membrane briefly dips below −70 mV → sets up the relative refractory period.
6
Return to rest. The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase + leak channels restore the −70 mV resting potential.
Absolute refractory period = Na⁺ channels are INACTIVATED. During this window no action potential can fire no matter how strong the stimulus — because the voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are physically inactivated (not just closed). This is what enforces one-way propagation. The relative refractory period (during hyperpolarization) can fire, but only with a stronger-than-normal stimulus.
⚠ Pump vs. Channels — Don't Mix Them
The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump sets up and restores the resting gradient (active, uses ATP, 3 out / 2 in). The voltage-gated channels create the actual spike (passive, ions flow down their gradient). The DAT often asks which one is responsible for resting potential (pump) vs. the upswing/downswing (channels).

Myelination & Saltatory Conduction

Myelin is the insulating fatty sheath around axons that speeds up conduction; myelinated axons make up the brain's white matter. Between myelin segments are bare gaps called Nodes of Ranvier — the action potential "jumps" node to node in saltatory conduction ("saltare" = to leap), which is much faster than continuous conduction.
🧠 Who makes myelin? "COPS"
CNS = Oligodendrocytes · PNS = Schwann cells. One oligodendrocyte myelinates many axons in the CNS; one Schwann cell wraps a single segment of one axon in the PNS. Both are types of glial cells (neuroglia), not neurons.

Synaptic Transmission

Acetylcholinesterase degrades acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft (via hydrolysis), terminating the signal so the muscle/neuron can reset. Many nerve agents and insecticides work by inhibiting this enzyme → ACh builds up → continuous firing.

10 · The Reproductive System
Gametogenesis, the Menstrual Cycle & Pregnancy
⏱ 20 minutes
The menstrual cycle is one of the single most heavily tested topics on the DAT — and almost every question hinges on which hormone does what and the feedback switches that drive it. Lock in the hormone curves, the estrogen positive-feedback LH surge, and the corpus-luteum / hCG handoff and you bank easy points.

Male Anatomy & Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis is the production of four haploid spermatozoa from one diploid spermatogonium. A spermatogonium becomes a primary spermatocyte, undergoes meiosis I → two secondary spermatocytes, then meiosis II → four spermatids. Spermiogenesis is the final maturation of round spermatids into motile, flagellated sperm — no cell division and no change in DNA content occurs at this last step.
Sperm Pathway — "SEVEN UP"
S
Seminiferous tubules (of the testes) — site of spermatogenesis. Contain Sertoli & Leydig cells.
E
Epididymis — sperm storage & maturation (gain motility). Moved by peristalsis.
V
Vas (ductus) deferens — paired muscular tubes that transport sperm.
E
Ejaculatory duct — propels sperm forward; receives seminal vesicle secretions.
U
Urethra — shared with the urinary tract; exits via the Penis.
🔑 Accessory Glands
Semen = sperm + accessory gland secretions. Seminal vesicles (fructose for energy, alkaline fluid), prostate gland (milky alkaline fluid), and bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands (pre-ejaculate that lubricates and neutralizes acidic urine in the urethra). These nourish sperm and enhance motility — they are not the site of sperm production.
Sertoli (Sustentacular) Cells
Driven by FSH. "Nurse" cells — nourish and support developing sperm; form the blood–testis barrier (immune privilege). Secrete inhibin, which selectively inhibits FSH via negative feedback. FSH → Sertoli (both have "S").
Leydig (Interstitial) Cells
Driven by LH. Sit in the interstitium between tubules and produce testosterone, which drives spermatogenesis and male secondary sex characteristics. LH → Leydig (both have "L").
🧠 Memory Key
FSH → Sertoli (S/S) for Sperm support; LH → Leydig (L/L) for testosterone. Sperm anatomy: Head (nucleus + acrosome enzymes), Midpiece (mitochondria → ATP), Tail (flagellum of microtubules for propulsion).

Female Anatomy & Oogenesis

Ovary produces ova + estrogen/progesterone. The egg travels through the oviduct (fallopian tube) — the site of fertilization — to the uterus, where a blastocyst implants in the endometrium. The cervix connects the uterus to the vagina.
In oogenesis, oogonia form primary oocytes before birth that arrest in prophase I. Each month after puberty, one primary oocyte completes meiosis I → a large secondary oocyte + a small polar body. Ovulation releases the secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II. Meiosis II only completes if fertilization occurs, yielding one viable ovum + a second polar body. The unequal divisions (cytoplasm hoarded by the ovum, dumped from polar bodies) give the zygote nutrient reserves.

The Menstrual / Ovarian Cycle ★ Highest-Yield

Hormone Curves Over a ~28-Day Cycle
Ovarian Cycle — Step by Step
1
Follicular phase (days 1–13). Hypothalamus → GnRH → anterior pituitary releases FSH (+ LH). FSH drives follicle growth; the growing follicle secretes estrogen, which thickens the endometrium. (Uterine: menses → proliferative phase.)
2
LH surge → Ovulation (~day 14). Estrogen climbs to a high threshold and flips to positive feedback, triggering a sharp LH surge (with a smaller FSH bump). The LH surge ruptures the dominant follicle → the secondary oocyte (metaphase II) is released into the oviduct.
3
Luteal phase (days 15–28). The empty follicle becomes the corpus luteum (maintained by LH), which secretes progesterone + estrogen to maintain/secrete the endometrium. (Uterine: secretory phase.) High progesterone/estrogen now exert negative feedback, suppressing FSH/LH.
4
No fertilization → menstruation. The corpus luteum degenerates into the corpus albicans → progesterone & estrogen fall → the endometrium can't be maintained and sheds (menses). Falling hormones release the brakes on FSH → the cycle restarts.

Estrogen is usually NEGATIVE feedback — but turns POSITIVE at high levels. For most of the cycle estrogen/progesterone inhibit GnRH/FSH/LH. The exception is the pre-ovulatory spike: sustained high estrogen flips to positive feedback and triggers the LH surge that causes ovulation. This switch is the single most common menstrual-cycle trap on the DAT.
The corpus luteum makes PROGESTERONE — not the follicle. The pre-ovulation follicle's main product is estrogen; the post-ovulation corpus luteum is the progesterone source. If progesterone drops, it's because the corpus luteum degenerated. Progesterone "pro-gestation" = maintains the endometrium for pregnancy.

Pregnancy & the hCG Handoff

Fertilization occurs in the oviduct (fallopian tube), forming a diploid zygote. If implantation occurs, the embryo's trophoblast/chorion secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin), which rescues the corpus luteum so it keeps making progesterone + estrogen — preventing menstruation and keeping the endometrium intact. hCG is the molecule detected by pregnancy tests. After the first trimester, the placenta takes over progesterone/estrogen production and the corpus luteum regresses.
🧠 Why You Don't Ovulate While Pregnant (= How "The Pill" Works)
Sustained high estrogen + progesterone exert strong negative feedback on the hypothalamus/pituitary → low FSH & LH → no follicle maturation and no LH surge → no ovulation. Combined oral contraceptives mimic this hormonal state to suppress ovulation.
Oxytocin
Posterior pituitary. Drives uterine contractions in labor via positive feedback (contractions → stretch cervix → more oxytocin → stronger contractions). Also causes milk let-down (ejection) reflex.
Prolactin
Anterior pituitary. Stimulates milk production (synthesis) in the mammary glands. Suckling → more prolactin (positive feedback). Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin moves it.
Prolactin vs. oxytocin for lactation. Prolactin = milk production/synthesis. Oxytocin = milk let-down/ejection. Don't swap them. Both involve positive feedback driven by infant suckling.

Circulatory

  • Pulmonary veins=O₂-rich; pulmonary arteries=O₂-poor (only ones reversed)
  • Path: body→vena cava→RA→RV→pulmonary artery→lungs→pulmonary vein→LA→LV→aorta→body
  • Arteries away from heart; veins toward; capillaries = gas/nutrient exchange
  • Pacemaker = SA node→AV node→Bundle of His→Purkinje fibers
  • Valves: tricuspid (R AV), bicuspid/mitral (L AV), pulmonary & aortic semilunar
  • "Lub-dub": lub=AV valves close (systole); dub=semilunar close (diastole)
  • Left ventricle = thickest wall (pumps to whole body)
  • Fetal shunts: foramen ovale, ductus arteriosus, ductus venosus

Respiratory

  • Path: nose→pharynx→larynx→trachea→bronchi→bronchioles→alveoli
  • Alveoli = site of gas exchange (simple squamous, huge surface area)
  • Diaphragm contracts→flattens→inhalation (active); relaxes→exhalation (passive)
  • CO₂ (not low O₂) is the primary drive to breathe; sensed via blood pH
  • CO₂ carried mostly as bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) in plasma
  • Surfactant reduces surface tension, keeps alveoli from collapsing
  • Hemoglobin: 4 O₂ per molecule; cooperative binding (sigmoidal curve)

Muscular

  • 3 types: skeletal (striated, voluntary, multinucleate), cardiac (striated, involuntary, intercalated discs), smooth (no striations, involuntary)
  • Sliding filament: actin (thin) slides over myosin (thick); sarcomere shortens
  • Trigger: action potential→Ca²⁺ released from sarcoplasmic reticulum→binds troponin→exposes actin
  • ATP required for myosin power stroke AND to detach (rigor mortis = no ATP)
  • Sarcomere = Z-line to Z-line; I-band & H-zone shrink, A-band constant
  • Slow-twitch (I)=red, oxidative, fatigue-resistant; fast-twitch (II)=white, glycolytic, powerful
  • Neuromuscular junction NT = acetylcholine

Skeletal / Skin

  • Bone = connective tissue; osteoblasts build, osteoclasts break down, osteocytes maintain
  • Compact bone unit = osteon (Haversian system); cells in lacunae
  • Red marrow = hematopoiesis (blood cells); yellow marrow = fat
  • Cartilage is avascular, heals slowly; hyaline most common
  • Skin layers: epidermis (stratified squamous), dermis, hypodermis (subcutaneous fat)
  • Epidermis is avascular; keratinocytes; melanocytes make melanin (UV protection)
  • Skin = largest organ; thermoregulation, barrier, vitamin D synthesis

Renal / Urinary

  • Nephron = functional unit; ~1 million per kidney
  • Flow: glomerulus→Bowman's capsule→PCT→loop of Henle→DCT→collecting duct
  • Glomerulus filters by pressure; PCT reabsorbs most (glucose, ions, water)
  • Loop of Henle = countercurrent multiplier concentrates urine
  • ADH (vasopressin)→inserts aquaporins→water reabsorbed→concentrated urine
  • Aldosterone→Na⁺ reabsorption (water follows)→raises BP
  • Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system raises blood pressure
  • Order of processes: Filtration, Reabsorption, Secretion, Excretion

Digestive

  • Path: mouth→esophagus→stomach→small intestine→large intestine→rectum
  • Mouth: amylase (starch) + mechanical; stomach: pepsin + HCl (protein), low pH
  • Most chemical digestion & absorption in small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum)
  • Pancreas: amylase, lipase, trypsin + bicarbonate (neutralizes acid)
  • Liver makes bile (emulsifies fat); gallbladder stores bile
  • Villi/microvilli increase surface area; absorption into capillaries/lacteals
  • Large intestine = water & electrolyte reabsorption, houses gut bacteria
  • Peristalsis (smooth muscle) moves food; chemical churning

Blood / Immunity

  • Plasma (55%) + formed elements; RBCs (no nucleus, hemoglobin), WBCs, platelets
  • Innate=fast, nonspecific (skin, phagocytes, inflammation); adaptive=slow, specific, memory
  • B cells→antibodies (humoral); T cells (cell-mediated): helper (CD4), cytotoxic (CD8)
  • Macrophages & dendritic cells = antigen-presenting cells (MHC)
  • Memory cells = faster secondary response (basis of vaccines)
  • Blood types: A,B,AB,O; O⁻ universal donor, AB⁺ universal recipient
  • Clotting: platelets→thrombin→fibrinogen to fibrin mesh

Endocrine

  • Hypothalamus controls pituitary (master gland)
  • Anterior pituitary (FLAT PIG): FSH, LH, ACTH, TSH, Prolactin, Ignore(endorphins), GH
  • Posterior pituitary releases (not makes) ADH & oxytocin
  • Insulin lowers, glucagon raises blood glucose (pancreas islets)
  • Thyroid: T3/T4 raise metabolism; calcitonin lowers blood Ca²⁺
  • Parathyroid (PTH) raises blood Ca²⁺ (opposes calcitonin)
  • Adrenal medulla=epinephrine (fight/flight); cortex=cortisol, aldosterone
  • Steroid/lipid hormones→intracellular receptors; peptide→surface + 2nd messenger (cAMP)

Nervous

  • Resting potential ≈ −70 mV; Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in)
  • Action potential: depolarize (Na⁺ in)→repolarize (K⁺ out)→hyperpolarize; all-or-none
  • Myelin (saltatory conduction) speeds signal; nodes of Ranvier
  • CNS=oligodendrocytes, PNS=Schwann cells make myelin
  • Synapse: AP→Ca²⁺ in→neurotransmitter release→postsynaptic receptors
  • Reflex arc: sensory(afferent)→interneuron→motor(efferent), bypasses brain
  • Autonomic: sympathetic (fight/flight), parasympathetic (rest/digest)
  • Neuron parts: dendrites receive, axon sends, soma = cell body

Reproductive

  • Spermatogenesis: continuous, 4 sperm/meiosis, in seminiferous tubules
  • Oogenesis: 1 egg + polar bodies/meiosis; arrested at metaphase II until fertilization
  • Male hormones: FSH→sperm, LH→testosterone (Leydig cells)
  • Menstrual cycle: FSH→follicle; LH surge→ovulation; corpus luteum→progesterone
  • Estrogen=builds endometrium; progesterone=maintains it
  • Fertilization in fallopian tube; implantation in uterus
  • hCG maintains corpus luteum in early pregnancy (basis of pregnancy tests)
