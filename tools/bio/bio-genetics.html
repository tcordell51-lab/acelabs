Tier 2 · Genetics foundations

Probability rules: the AND rule and the OR rule.

Every Punnett, pedigree, and Hardy-Weinberg calculation reduces to two rules. AND (multiply): for INDEPENDENT events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). OR (add): for MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Get these two right and the whole genetics chapter unlocks.

The trap

Students multiply when they should add (or vice versa). They forget to check whether events are independent (different gametes, different traits) or mutually exclusive (genotypes that cannot co-occur).

The rule

AND = × when events are independent (e.g., one trait does not affect the other; chance the first child AND the second child are both affected). OR = + when events are mutually exclusive (e.g., the offspring is AA OR Aa OR aa — can only be one). For dihybrid: P(AB) = P(A) × P(B).

The move

Read the question for "and" / "both" / "all" → multiply. For "either" / "or" / "one of" → add. For dihybrid ratios, use the AND rule on each gene's monohybrid odds.

And = multiply (independent). Or = add (mutually exclusive).

From CoachCoach notes · the two rules + when each applies

Master these two rules and 95% of genetics math becomes mechanical.

AND rule (product / multiplication rule): for INDEPENDENT events — events whose outcomes do not affect each other.

  • Two children from the same parents. Each child's genotype is independent of the others (each meiosis is its own event). P(both affected) = P(child 1 affected) × P(child 2 affected).
  • Two traits in a dihybrid cross. P(tall AND yellow) = P(tall) × P(yellow), if the genes assort independently.
  • Two alleles at one locus during gametogenesis. P(gamete carries A from mother AND B from father) = P(A in maternal gamete) × P(B in paternal gamete).

OR rule (sum / addition rule): for MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE events — events that cannot happen at the same time.

  • "Affected" phenotype from Aa × Aa cross. Affected = aa. Unaffected = AA or Aa. P(unaffected) = P(AA) + P(Aa) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4. Genotypes are mutually exclusive (a single offspring is exactly one genotype).
  • Sex of a child. P(boy) + P(girl) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Boy and girl are mutually exclusive for one birth.

Combining the two: most DAT problems chain AND and OR. "What is the probability that exactly one of three children is affected?" Each child is independent (AND across the three), but "exactly one" can mean child 1 only OR child 2 only OR child 3 only (OR across the three orderings). P = 3 × (1/4) × (3/4) × (3/4) = 27/64.

Common student misconceptions
  • "All three children carry the same probability of affected, so just use 1/4." No — each child is its own meiosis. P(all 3 affected) = (1/4)³ = 1/64.
  • "P(Aa) is added because there are two ways to make Aa (A from mom + a from dad, OR a from mom + A from dad)." Yes — P(Aa) = (1/2)(1/2) + (1/2)(1/2) = 1/2. The AND rule applied to each path, then OR rule across the two paths.
  • "Independent assortment means events are mutually exclusive." No — independent assortment of two genes means one gene's outcome does not affect the other. They co-occur happily (use AND).
See also Mendelian Pedigrees Hardy-Weinberg · every downstream genetics topic uses these two rules.

Tier 2 · Node 21 of 40

Mendelian genetics: probability, not magic.

Three laws explain ~95% of single-gene inheritance: dominance, segregation, independent assortment. The trick isn't the math — it's reading the cross correctly. Build the Punnett, count the cells, predict the offspring.

The trap

Students confuse genotype (Aa) with phenotype (the visible trait). They forget that for codominance and incomplete dominance, the phenotypic ratio is NOT 3:1.

The rule

Each gamete carries one allele per gene (segregation). For Aa × Aa: genotype 1:2:1 (AA:Aa:aa), phenotype 3:1 if A is dominant. Dihybrid AaBb × AaBb: 9:3:3:1.

The move

Identify parental genotypes. Build the Punnett. Count cells matching each phenotype. Reduce to ratio. Test cross = breed unknown with homozygous recessive.

Aa × Aa: genotype 1:2:1. Phenotype 3:1 if dominant.

If two heterozygous parents (Aa × Aa) cross, what is the phenotypic ratio of the offspring?
From Coach Coach notes · the inheritance patterns the DAT actually tests

Genotype is what they have. Phenotype is what they show.

Mendel's three laws are the framework: (1) Dominance — one allele masks the other. (2) Segregation — the two alleles for a gene separate during gamete formation; each gamete carries one. (3) Independent assortment — alleles for different genes assort independently (true if genes are on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome). Linked genes violate this and inherit together more often than chance — recombination frequency measures the violation.

Beyond simple dominance, the DAT tests three deviation patterns:

  • Incomplete dominance: heterozygote is intermediate. Red × White snapdragons → Pink. RR : RW : WW = 1:2:1 phenotype matches genotype.
  • Codominance: both alleles fully expressed in heterozygote. Type AB blood: A and B both visible. Roan cattle: red and white hairs both visible.
  • Sex-linked (X-linked) traits: recessive X-linked traits show up more in males because they only have one X. Hemophilia, color blindness. Affected fathers can't pass to sons (Y comes from dad).

The test cross is your tool for figuring out an unknown genotype: cross with a homozygous recessive (aa). If unknown is AA, all offspring show dominant. If unknown is Aa, half show recessive. Ratio reveals the truth.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Aa × Aa gives 4 different offspring genotypes." No — AA, Aa, Aa, aa. The two Aa cells are the same genotype; just different gamete origins. Genotype count = 3.
  • "Phenotype ratio is always 3:1 for monohybrid." Only for complete dominance. Incomplete dominance and codominance give 1:2:1.
  • "Sex-linked traits ignore Mendel." They don't — they obey segregation. They just inherit on the X chromosome, so males (XY) show recessive X traits more often.
  • "A dihybrid 9:3:3:1 means the genes are linked." Opposite — 9:3:3:1 means the genes ARE assorting independently. Linked genes deviate from this.
See also Mitosis · segregation happens in meiosis I, but the cell cycle context (mitosis vs meiosis) is foundational here.

Tier 1 · Node 12 of 40

Meiosis: two divisions, four unique haploid gametes.

Meiosis is sexual reproduction's division mechanism. One 2n parent → four genetically unique n daughters via two sequential divisions. Meiosis I separates homologous pairs; meiosis II separates sister chromatids (like mitosis). Two events generate variation: crossing-over in prophase I and independent assortment in metaphase I.

The trap

Students treat meiosis I and meiosis II as identical. They're not. Meiosis I separates HOMOLOGS. Meiosis II separates SISTERS. Different reductions, different chromosome counts.

The rule

Meiosis I = reductional (2n → n). Homologous pairs separate. Meiosis II = equational (n → n). Sister chromatids separate. Two events generate variation: crossing-over (prophase I) + independent assortment (metaphase I).

The move

Track ploidy at each step. Identify which separation happens at each anaphase. Note where genetic variation enters: prophase I (crossing-over) and metaphase I (independent assortment).

Meiosis I separates homologs. Meiosis II separates sisters. Four unique gametes.

From Coach Coach notes · the variation engines + the ploidy tracker

The two divisions do different things. Memorize that, everything else falls out.

Track ploidy at each step:

  • Before S phase: 2n, each chromosome is one chromatid.
  • After S phase (still 2n): each chromosome is now two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. Cell is 2n with 4n DNA content.
  • End of meiosis I: n. Homologs separated. Each daughter has one chromosome from each homologous pair, BUT each chromosome is still two sisters.
  • End of meiosis II: n. Sisters separated. Each gamete has one of each chromosome, single chromatid each. Four gametes total.

Two sources of genetic variation (high-yield on DAT):

  • Crossing-over: in prophase I, homologs pair up (synapsis, forming a tetrad/bivalent), then exchange chromosome segments at chiasmata. Recombines maternal + paternal alleles.
  • Independent assortment: in metaphase I, each homologous pair aligns independently of others. Maternal/paternal can go to either pole — 2n possible combinations (223 = ~8 million for humans).
Common student misconceptions
  • "Meiosis II is just like mitosis." Mechanically yes (sisters separate), but the cell entering meiosis II is haploid (n, with each chromosome as 2 sisters). Mitosis happens in diploid cells.
  • "Crossing-over happens in metaphase I." No — crossing-over is in prophase I. Independent assortment is what happens in metaphase I.
  • "Meiosis produces 2 gametes." No — 4. Two divisions × 2 daughters per division = 4 gametes.
  • "All gametes are genetically identical." Opposite. Crossing-over + independent assortment + random fertilization produce ~1014 unique zygotes for humans.
See also Mitosis Mendelian genetics · segregation and independent assortment happen in meiosis I; Mendel's laws are observed effects of meiotic mechanics.

Section 02 · Genetics

Linkage and crossing over: recombination frequency = map distance.

Two genes on the same chromosome are linked — they tend to be inherited together, violating Mendel's independent assortment. Crossing over (homologous recombination at chiasmata in prophase I) breaks linkage. The recombination frequency (RF = recombinants / total) measures how often crossing over separates two alleles. 1% RF = 1 centimorgan. Three-point crosses determine gene order. Chi-squared tests whether observed ratios fit independent assortment or hint at linkage.

The trap

Maximum RF is 50%, not 100%. At 50% the genes look unlinked — either truly on different chromosomes OR so far apart on the same chromosome that double crossovers scramble the result. So "RF = 0.5" can't distinguish unlinked from very-distant. Students also forget that parental phenotypes are most common; recombinants are minority. The DAT loves the order: parentals (high) → singles → doubles (rarest).

The rule

RF = recombinants / total × 100%. Closer genes → smaller RF. 1% RF ≡ 1 cM (centimorgan, the genetic map unit). Three-point cross: the rarest two phenotypic classes are double crossovers; the gene that has flipped relative to parentals is the middle gene. Chi-squared compares observed to expected (1:1:1:1 testcross or 9:3:3:1 dihybrid). High X2 → reject independent assortment → suspect linkage.

The move

For RF Qs: count recombinants, divide by total, multiply by 100. For map order Qs: identify parentals (most common), doubles (least common), figure out which gene flipped. For chi-squared Qs: compute expected from ratio, sum (O−E)2/E, compare to critical value at df = classes − 1.

From Coach Coach notes · testcrosses, three-point crosses, and the chi-squared sanity check

Linkage breaks independent assortment. Recombination frequency tells us how badly.

The setup: two genes on the same chromosome are linked. In a heterozygous individual, the alleles can be in cis (AB / ab — "coupling") or trans (Ab / aB — "repulsion"). Without recombination, only the parental combinations would appear in gametes.

Why crossing over rescues variation: in prophase I, homologs synapse and exchange chromosome arms at chiasmata. Each chiasma swaps everything distal to that point. Genes farther apart are more likely to have a chiasma between them → more recombinants.

Recombination frequency formula:

  • RF = (number of recombinant offspring) / (total offspring) × 100%
  • Range: 0% (genes are at the same locus, never recombine) to 50% (independent assortment — behaves as if unlinked).
  • 1% RF = 1 centimorgan (cM) = 1 map unit (m.u.). The centimorgan is named after Thomas Hunt Morgan.

Worked example (testcross): AaBb (cis: AB/ab) × aabb. If genes were unlinked, expect 1:1:1:1 (50 of each in 200 offspring). Observed: 80 AB, 80 ab, 20 Ab, 20 aB. Parentals (AB + ab) = 160. Recombinants (Ab + aB) = 40. RF = 40/200 = 20% → 20 cM apart.

Three-point cross: with three linked genes (A, B, C), eight phenotypic classes appear. Logic:

  • Most frequent two classes = parental combinations.
  • Least frequent two classes = double crossovers (a crossover happened in BOTH intervals).
  • To find the middle gene: compare a double crossover to a parental. The single gene that has switched its phase is the middle gene.
  • Map distances: for each interval, distance = (single crossovers in that interval + double crossovers) / total × 100%. Double crossovers count for BOTH intervals because two crossovers happened.

Chi-squared test for linkage:

  • Compute expected counts under the null hypothesis (independent assortment, e.g., 1:1:1:1 for testcross).
  • X2 = Σ (O − E)2 / E, summed over all phenotypic classes.
  • Degrees of freedom = (number of phenotypic classes) − 1.
  • Compare X2 to the critical value (e.g., 7.815 at df=3, α=0.05). If X2 > critical value, reject the null → observed ratio differs significantly from independent assortment → suspect linkage (or selection, viability differences, etc.).

Interference and coefficient of coincidence: double crossovers are usually rarer than expected from multiplying single-crossover frequencies. Coefficient of coincidence = observed doubles / expected doubles. Interference = 1 − COC. Positive interference (most common in eukaryotes) means one crossover suppresses nearby ones.

Common student misconceptions
  • "RF can be greater than 50%." No — the maximum is 50% (effectively unlinked). Two genes far apart on the same chromosome show RF capped at ~50% because of multiple crossovers.
  • "The middle gene in a three-point cross is the one in the parental class." No — the middle gene is identified by comparing double crossovers to parentals: the gene that flipped in the doubles is the middle one.
  • "Chi-squared proves linkage." It can only reject independent assortment. Other causes (selection, viability) could also produce an unexpected ratio.
  • "Crossing over and independent assortment are the same thing." They aren't. Crossing over is in prophase I (recombines linked alleles); independent assortment is in metaphase I (random alignment of homolog pairs from different chromosomes).
See also Meiosis · crossing over occurs in prophase I; the math of RF assumes meiotic mechanics from the meiosis node.

Pedigrees: five inheritance patterns to identify.

Pedigrees are family trees marking affected individuals. The DAT shows you a pedigree and asks: which inheritance mode? Autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked dominant, X-linked recessive, mitochondrial. Each has tells.

The trap

Students confuse X-linked recessive (mostly males affected, no male-to-male transmission) with autosomal recessive. They forget mitochondrial inheritance is exclusively from mother.

The rule

AD: every gen, ~50% from affected parent. AR: skips gens, two unaffected → affected child. XR: mostly males, no M→M. XD: affected dads pass to ALL daughters, NO sons. Mito: affected mom → ALL kids; affected dad → none.

The move

Look at: which sex affected? Skipped generations? Male-to-male transmission? Affected female with unaffected father? Each filter eliminates patterns.

No male-to-male = X-linked. Skips gens = recessive. Mom only = mitochondrial.

Section 02 · Genetics

Hardy-Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1.

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium describes a non-evolving population. Five conditions: no mutation, no migration, no selection, random mating, infinite population. Violate any → evolution. The DAT loves "given q², find carrier frequency."

The trap

Students confuse q (allele freq), q² (homozygous-recessive freq = affected freq), and 2pq (heterozygote freq = carrier freq). Memorize which is which.

The rule

p = freq dominant allele; q = freq recessive allele; p+q=1. Genotypes: p² = AA; 2pq = Aa (carriers); q² = aa (affected). Always sum to 1.

The move

Given affected freq (q²), take √q² to get q. p = 1 − q. Carrier freq = 2pq. The trick: 2pq is HETEROZYGOTE freq, NOT q.

Affected = q². Carriers = 2pq. Allele freq = q.
PriorityHigh
DifficultyMedium
TierGenetics · High-yield
Typical question wording
1 in 10,000 individuals is affected by a recessive disorder. What fraction of the population are carriers?
From CoachCoach notes · p² + 2pq + q² and the classic carrier problem

Memorize: q² is affected, 2pq is carrier.

Step-by-step for the classic problem: "1 in 10,000 affected" → q² = 0.0001 → q = √0.0001 = 0.01. p = 1 − 0.01 = 0.99. Carrier freq = 2pq = 2(0.99)(0.01) ≈ 0.0198 ≈ 2% (1 in 50).

Five HW conditions for NO evolution: no mutation, no migration (gene flow), no selection, random mating, infinite population (no genetic drift). Real populations rarely meet all 5 → most populations evolve.

Common scenarios tested: CF affects ~1/2500 in some pops → q ≈ 0.02, carriers ≈ 4%. Sickle-cell allele in West African pops elevated by balancing selection (HbAS resists malaria).

How HW gets violated — population genetics specifics:

  • Mutation: introduces new alleles. Slow on its own, but it's the source of all variation natural selection acts on.
  • Migration (gene flow): alleles enter or leave the population. Mixes gene pools, reduces genetic differences between populations.
  • Non-random mating: assortative mating (similar phenotypes pair up), inbreeding (related individuals pair). Inbreeding does NOT change allele frequencies but increases homozygosity (q2 goes up, 2pq goes down). Inbreeding depression: more homozygous recessive deleterious genotypes get expressed.
  • Selection: non-random survival or reproductive success. Directly changes allele frequencies. The most common HW violator in real populations.
  • Genetic drift: random sampling effects in finite populations. Strongest in small populations. Two named subtypes:
    • Bottleneck effect: a sharp population reduction (disaster, hunting, habitat loss). The survivors carry only a sample of the original gene pool — allele frequencies shift randomly, often losing rare alleles. Classic example: the cheetah (passed through a bottleneck ~10,000 years ago; today extremely low genetic diversity, susceptible to disease). Northern elephant seals (~20 individuals in 1890s) and European bison are other textbook examples.
    • Founder effect: a small group leaves the main population to colonize a new area. The founders carry a non-representative slice of the original allele pool. The new population can have very different allele frequencies even without selection. Classic examples: Old Order Amish in Pennsylvania (high frequency of Ellis-van Creveld syndrome traced to one founder couple), Afrikaner population in South Africa (elevated frequency of porphyria variegata and Huntington disease from a small founding group), island populations.

Drift vs selection in tiny populations: when N is small, drift can overwhelm selection. A mildly advantageous allele can disappear by chance; a mildly deleterious allele can become fixed. Conservation biologists worry about this even without immediate threats — drift erodes diversity in small populations.

Common student misconceptions
  • "q² is the carrier frequency." No — q² is the AFFECTED frequency (homozygous recessive). 2pq is the carrier frequency (heterozygous).
  • "Hardy-Weinberg means evolution is impossible." No — HW describes a non-evolving population. Violations → evolution. HW is a null hypothesis.
  • "p² always equals 0.5." No — p² depends on p. If p=0.7, p²=0.49.

Non-Mendelian: codominance, incomplete, X-linked, polygenic, epistasis, pleiotropy.

Beyond simple dominant/recessive. Codominance: both alleles fully expressed (AB blood). Incomplete dominance: heterozygote = blend (pink snapdragons). Multiple alleles: 3+ versions (I^A, I^B, i). X-linked recessive: skips a generation, more common in males. Polygenic: many genes → continuous traits (height, skin). Epistasis: one gene masks another (Bombay, Lab coat). Pleiotropy: one gene → many traits (sickle, Marfan).

The trap

Codominance vs incomplete dominance — students blend them. Codominance = BOTH alleles visible separately (AB blood = both A and B antigens). Incomplete = literal mix (pink). Also: X-linked recessive trait passed through carrier mothers, expressed mostly in sons.

The rule

Codominance ≠ incomplete. Codom: both expressed at once. Incomp: blended phenotype. X-linked recessive: affected fathers cannot pass to sons (sons get Y), but ALL daughters are carriers. Epistasis: gene at locus B masks/modifies gene at locus A. Pleiotropy: ONE gene, MANY phenotypes.

The move

See AB blood → codominance. Pink F1 → incomplete. Skipped generation in males → X-linked recessive. 9:3:4 / 13:3 / 12:3:1 ratios → epistasis (modified 9:3:3:1).

AB blood = codominance. Pink snapdragon = incomplete. Bombay = recessive epistasis.

From CoachCoach notes · six non-Mendelian patterns

Codominance: both alleles fully expressed simultaneously. Classic = ABO blood. I^A I^B = type AB blood (BOTH antigens, not blended). Roan cattle (red + white hairs both visible). Don't confuse with incomplete.

Incomplete dominance: heterozygote shows intermediate phenotype. Red (RR) × white (rr) snapdragons → all pink (Rr) F1. F2 = 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. The 1:2:1 phenotypic ratio = giveaway.

Multiple alleles: 3+ versions of one gene at the population level (any individual still has just 2). ABO has I^A, I^B, i. I^A and I^B are codominant; both dominant over i.

X-linked recessive: color blindness, hemophilia, DMD. Females need 2 copies (X^aX^a) to express; males need 1 (X^aY). Carrier mom × normal dad → 50% sons affected, 50% daughters carriers. Affected dad → 0% sons (Y from him), 100% daughters carriers.

Polygenic inheritance: many genes contribute additively → continuous bell-shaped distribution. Height, skin color, IQ. Each "dose" of dominant allele adds increment. Hard to map to single locus.

Epistasis: one gene masks another. Recessive epistasis (Bombay phenotype, Labrador coat color: ee genotype masks B/b → 9:3:4). Dominant epistasis (squash color, 12:3:1). The 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio gets modified.

Pleiotropy: one gene → many phenotypes. Sickle-cell allele → anemia + malaria resistance + pain crises + organ damage. Marfan → tall + heart + lens + aorta. Don't confuse with polygenic (opposite direction).

Common student misconceptions
  • "AB blood is incomplete dominance." No — codominance. Both A and B antigens are fully present, not blended.
  • "Pleiotropy = polygenic." Opposite directions. Pleiotropy: 1 gene → many traits. Polygenic: many genes → 1 trait.
  • "X-linked recessive father passes to sons." Father gives Y to sons, X^a only to daughters (who become carriers).
  • "9:3:3:1 always means standard dihybrid." Modified ratios (9:3:4, 12:3:1, 9:7) suggest epistasis is operating.

Section 02 · Genetics

Gene regulation: lac, trp, eukaryotic.

Cells turn genes on/off without changing DNA sequence. lac operon (inducible, ON only when lactose is present and glucose is absent). trp operon (repressible, OFF when tryptophan is plentiful). Eukaryotic regulation: enhancers + transcription factors + chromatin remodeling + miRNA + alternative splicing.

The trap

lac operon ON conditions: glucose LOW + lactose HIGH. CAP-cAMP boosts transcription only when glucose is low (cAMP is high). Students forget catabolite repression.

The rule

Inducible (lac): normally OFF, induced ON. Repressible (trp): normally ON, repressed OFF. lac fully ON = lactose binds repressor (frees operator) AND glucose absent (cAMP-CAP binds activator site). trp ON = trp absent. Eukaryotes: many layers — chromatin → TFs → enhancers → splicing → miRNA → translation control.

The move

Identify operon type by what happens when nutrient added. Lactose added → lac ON. Tryptophan added → trp OFF. Always check both glucose and lactose for lac.

lac ON = lactose high + glucose low. trp ON = tryptophan low.

DifficultyHard
TierMolecular · High-yield
Typical question wording

From CoachCoach notes · lac, trp, and eukaryotic control

Same DNA, different expression. Regulation = phenotype.

lac operon (Jacob-Monod, 1961): three structural genes (lacZ β-galactosidase, lacY permease, lacA transacetylase). One promoter, one operator, one repressor binding site. INDUCIBLE: default OFF. Lactose isomerizes to allolactose, which binds the lac repressor → repressor falls off operator → RNA pol can transcribe.

Catabolite repression: only matters at the lac operon. High glucose → low cAMP → CAP (catabolite activator protein) doesn't bind → weak transcription even if lactose present. Low glucose → high cAMP → cAMP-CAP binds CAP site → strong transcription. Cell prefers glucose; lactose is plan B.

Four scenarios for lac: (1) glu+ lac− → OFF (no need). (2) glu+ lac+ → barely ON (CAP off). (3) glu− lac− → OFF (no inducer). (4) glu− lac+ → MAX ON (CAP on, repressor off).

trp operon: REPRESSIBLE. Default ON because cell needs tryptophan. When trp accumulates, trp binds the trp repressor (which was inactive) → activates it → binds operator → blocks transcription. Plus attenuation: ribosome stalling on leader sequence forms a terminator hairpin when trp is plentiful.

Eukaryotic regulation is multilayered:

  • Chromatin remodeling: heterochromatin = silenced; euchromatin = active. Histone acetylation (HAT) = open. Methylation = often silenced. CpG islands.
  • Transcription factors: general (TFIID, etc., RNA pol II machinery) + specific (bind enhancers/silencers).
  • Enhancers: can be 1000s of bp away, looped to promoter via mediator complex.
  • RNA processing: alternative splicing (one gene → many proteins), 5' cap, poly-A tail, RNA editing.
  • Post-transcriptional: miRNA + siRNA degrade mRNA / block translation.
  • Post-translational: ubiquitination → proteasome degradation. Phosphorylation switches.
Common student misconceptions
  • "lac operon turns on whenever lactose is added." Only fully ON when glucose is also low (cAMP-CAP needed for high transcription).
  • "trp operon and lac operon work the same way." Opposite logic: lac is inducible (default OFF), trp is repressible (default ON).
  • "Allolactose is the substrate of β-galactosidase." Allolactose is the inducer (an isomer of lactose). Lactose IS the substrate. β-gal makes both glucose+galactose AND allolactose from lactose.
  • "Eukaryotes use operons." Eukaryotes mostly do NOT use operons (each gene has its own promoter). Operons are typical of prokaryotes.

Section 02 · Genetics

Mutations: point, frameshift, chromosomal; repair: MMR, NER, HR, NHEJ.

Mutations occur at every level. Point mutations: silent (synonymous), missense (amino acid swap), nonsense (premature stop). Frameshift: insertion or deletion of non-multiple-of-3 → all downstream codons shifted, usually catastrophic. Chromosomal: deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation. Repair: MMR (mismatch), NER (UV thymine dimers), HR (high fidelity), NHEJ (fast but error-prone).

The trap

Silent mutation = same amino acid (degeneracy of code). Missense = different amino acid (may or may not matter). Nonsense = early STOP → truncated protein → usually nonfunctional. A 1-bp insertion is far worse than a 3-bp insertion.

The rule

Point: 1 base swap. Frameshift: ±1 or ±2 bases (NOT multiple of 3). Silent: codon changes, AA same. Missense: AA changes. Nonsense: STOP introduced. Repair pathways: MMR for replication errors, NER for bulky lesions (UV), HR/NHEJ for double-strand breaks.

The move

Look at sequence change. Same AA = silent. Different AA = missense. STOP = nonsense. Indel = check ÷3 (frameshift if not).

Insertion of 1 base = frameshift. Insertion of 3 bases = in-frame (single AA add).

DifficultyMedium
TierMolecular · High-yield
Typical question wording
CGA → CGG. The codon change is best described as:
From CoachCoach notes · mutation types and the four repair pathways

Mutations cluster by scale. Repair clusters by damage type.

Point mutations (1 bp):

  • Silent (synonymous): codon changes but encodes same amino acid (genetic code degeneracy). E.g., GCA → GCG, both Ala. Usually neutral.
  • Missense: codon → different amino acid. May be conservative (similar AA, e.g., Leu→Ile) or non-conservative. Sickle-cell = GAG→GTG = Glu→Val.
  • Nonsense: codon → STOP (UAA/UAG/UGA). Truncated protein, usually nonfunctional. NMD often degrades the transcript.

Frameshift: insertion or deletion of bases NOT divisible by 3. Shifts the reading frame downstream → ALL subsequent codons garbled, usually a premature STOP. Far more damaging than missense in most cases. CFTR ΔF508 deletion (3 bp = NOT frameshift, but still loses 1 AA).

Chromosomal mutations — large-scale rearrangements:

  • Deletion: a chunk of chromosome is lost. Cri-du-chat syndrome (5p deletion) is a classic example. Loss of dosage of the affected genes.
  • Duplication: a chunk is copied and inserted again. Gene dosage rises. Duplications can drive evolution by providing extra gene copies that diverge freely without losing the original function.
  • Inversion: a chunk is flipped 180° in place. Two flavors:
    • Paracentric inversion: inversion does NOT include the centromere (both breakpoints on the same arm). Often produces acentric and dicentric products at meiosis → gametes inviable.
    • Pericentric inversion: inversion DOES include the centromere (one breakpoint per arm). Produces unbalanced gametes (duplications/deletions) when crossover happens within the inversion loop.
  • Translocation: a chunk moves to a non-homologous chromosome.
    • Reciprocal translocation: mutual exchange of segments between two non-homologous chromosomes. Carriers are often phenotypically normal but produce unbalanced gametes.
    • Robertsonian translocation: two acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) fuse near the centromere; small arms lost. Carriers have 45 chromosomes but are usually normal. Robertsonian (14;21) is a major non-disjunction-independent cause of Down syndrome — a translocation parent has elevated risk of producing a trisomy 21 child.
    • Philadelphia chromosome: reciprocal t(9;22) — classic example of a translocation that creates a fusion gene. Mechanism: two non-homologous chromosomes exchange segments, placing gene segments in new regulatory context.
  • Aneuploidy — wrong chromosome number (caused by nondisjunction in meiosis):
    • Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome): 47,XX,+21 or 47,XY,+21. Most common viable autosomal trisomy. Risk increases with maternal age.
    • Trisomy 13 (Patau), Trisomy 18 (Edwards): usually fatal in infancy.
    • Monosomy X (Turner, 45,X): only viable monosomy. Female phenotype, short stature, infertility.
    • Klinefelter (47,XXY): male phenotype with reduced fertility, sometimes mild learning differences.
    • XYY, XXX: usually mild or asymptomatic.
    See the Bonus topics · Aneuploidies + nondisjunction node for the full nondisjunction mechanism (meiosis I vs II error patterns).
  • Polyploidy: entire extra sets of chromosomes (3n, 4n). Common in plants, very rare in animals (usually lethal in mammals; some fish and amphibians tolerate it). Allopolyploidy + autopolyploidy can produce instant speciation in plants.

Repair pathways:

  • Mismatch repair (MMR): catches replication errors that escaped proofreading. Scans for mismatches; cuts the new (unmethylated) strand. Keeps the replication error rate low.
  • Nucleotide excision repair (NER): removes bulky distortions like UV-induced thymine dimers. Cuts out a ~12-30 nt patch and resynthesizes. DAT trigger: UV damage.
  • Base excision repair (BER): single damaged base (oxidation, deamination). Glycosylase removes the base; AP endonuclease cuts backbone; polymerase fills.
  • Homologous recombination (HR): high-fidelity double-strand break repair using sister chromatid as template. Requires an intact homolog. Accurate but slow.
  • NHEJ (non-homologous end joining): fast but error-prone double-strand break repair; ligates ends directly. Predominant in G1 when no sister chromatid is available.
Common student misconceptions
  • "A silent mutation has no effect." Often true at protein level, but can affect splicing, mRNA stability, codon usage / translation efficiency.
  • "A 3-bp deletion = frameshift." No — multiples of 3 stay in frame. Just lose 1 amino acid (e.g., CFTR ΔF508).
  • "NER fixes mismatches." NER fixes BULKY lesions (e.g., UV dimers). MMR fixes mismatches.

Chromosomal-level mutations

Structural mutations affect large chromosome segments — visible at the karyotype level. Five types to know cold.

Deletion

A segment of the chromosome is removed. Gene dosage drops. Canonical DAT example: cri-du-chat syndrome (5p deletion). Heterozygous deletion = hemizygous for that region.

Duplication

A segment is repeated (tandemly or elsewhere). Gene dosage increases. Can evolve into novel genes via divergence — extra copies are free to mutate without losing the original function.

Translocation

Linkage and crossing over: recombination frequency = map distance.

Genes on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together. Crossing over (homologous recombination during prophase I) can separate them. The farther apart two genes are, the more often a crossover occurs between them. RF = recombinants ÷ total × 100%. 1% RF = 1 centimorgan (cM) = 1 map unit. Three-point test crosses reveal gene ORDER (low-frequency double crossovers identify the middle gene).

The trap

Maximum RF is 50% (if genes are far enough apart, they assort independently even if on the same chromosome). That makes them APPEAR unlinked. Students also forget: double crossovers REDUCE apparent RF because both crossovers cancel out. To find the middle gene: identify the double-crossover class (lowest frequency), compare to parentals — the gene that flipped position is in the middle.

The rule

RF = (recombinant offspring) ÷ (total offspring) × 100%. 1% RF = 1 cM. Parental classes = most frequent (linked genes stay together). Recombinant classes = less frequent (crossover separated them). Three-point cross: double crossovers = rarest class. Middle gene = flipped relative to parentals in double crossover class.

The move

For linkage Qs: start with the testcross data, identify parental (most frequent) vs recombinant (less frequent) phenotypes, calculate RF. For gene-order Qs: find the rarest class and compare to parental class — which gene position changed? That's the middle one.

RF = recombinants ÷ total. 1% RF = 1 centimorgan. Middle gene = flipped in double-crossover class.

From CoachCoach notes · linkage, mapping, chi-squared

Recombination frequency is a physical map in probability units.

Linkage fundamentals: genes on the same chromosome tend to be co-inherited. The frequency of this co-inheritance depends on chromosomal distance. The unit: 1 centimorgan (cM) = 1% recombination frequency between two loci = roughly 1 Mb in humans (varies). Thomas Hunt Morgan discovered linkage in Drosophila (1910s). Alfred Sturtevant (Morgan's student) built the first genetic map at age 20.

Coupling vs repulsion:

  • Coupling (cis): dominant alleles on the same chromosome (AB/ab). Parental types = AB and ab. Recombinants = Ab and aB.
  • Repulsion (trans): dominant alleles on different chromosomes (Ab/aB). Parental types = Ab and aB. Recombinants = AB and ab.
  • You can tell which by identifying the most frequent classes in a testcross — those are the parental arrangements.

Three-point cross logic (step-by-step):

  • Order the 8 phenotypic classes from most to least frequent.
  • Two most frequent = parental types (no crossover).
  • Two least frequent = double crossover types.
  • Compare double-crossover classes to parental classes: the gene that switched position (relative to the other two) is the MIDDLE gene.
  • Calculate map distances: (single crossovers for interval AB + double crossovers) ÷ total = distance AB in cM; same for BC.
  • Coefficient of coincidence (COC) = observed doubles ÷ expected doubles. Interference = 1 − COC. (Positive interference = fewer double crossovers than expected.)

Chi-squared test for linkage: null hypothesis = independent assortment (1:1:1:1 for testcross). Calculate expected counts from equal ratios. X² = ∑(O-E)²/E. Degrees of freedom = number of classes − 1 (for testcross, df = 3). If X² > critical value at p=0.05 (7.815 for df=3), reject H0 → evidence of linkage.

Common student misconceptions
  • "If two genes are on the same chromosome, they're always inherited together." Only if they're very close (low RF). With RF = 50%, they appear completely unlinked despite being on the same chromosome.
  • "Double crossovers between two loci increase the apparent map distance." Double crossovers DECREASE apparent RF between the flanking genes (they look like no crossover). For three-point crosses, you must add back the double-crossover contributions to each interval.
  • "1 cM = 1 Mb everywhere." The physical distance per cM varies dramatically across the genome and between sexes (female meiosis generally has more recombination in certain regions).
