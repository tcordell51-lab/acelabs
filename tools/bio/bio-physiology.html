Tier 3 · Node 25 of 40

Action potential: an all-or-nothing voltage spike.

A neuron at rest sits at -70 mV. When depolarized past threshold (-55 mV), voltage-gated Na+ channels snap open, the membrane potential spikes to +30 mV, then K+ channels open and bring it back. Refractory period prevents immediate re-firing. This is how nerves transmit signals over long distances.

The trap

Students think action potentials vary in size with stimulus strength. They don't — AP is all-or-nothing. They also confuse the resting-potential mechanism (Na/K pump + leak channels) with the AP firing mechanism (voltage-gated channels).

The rule

Resting -70 mV. Threshold at -55 mV. Above threshold, voltage-gated Na+ channels open (depolarization). At peak +30 mV, Na+ inactivates and K+ opens (repolarization). K+ overshoots (hyperpolarization). Pump resets.

The move

For any voltage state, identify which channels are open / closed / inactivated. For any channel state, predict the voltage trend. Memorize: Na+ in for up. K+ out for down.

Na+ in for up. K+ out for down. Pump resets the gradient.

From Coach Coach notes · the resting potential vs the spike, and saltatory conduction

Resting potential is maintained. Action potentials are events.

The resting potential (-70 mV) is held constant by two things: K+ leak channels (always open, K+ tends to flow out down its gradient) and the Na+/K+ ATPase pump (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per ATP — so the cell stays slightly more negative inside). The pump doesn't generate the AP — it just keeps the gradient ready so when voltage-gated channels DO open, ions rush in/out fast.

The all-or-nothing principle: above threshold (-55 mV), voltage-gated Na+ channels open in a positive feedback loop — depolarization opens more channels, which depolarizes more, which opens more. Once it starts, it always reaches +30 mV. Sub-threshold stimuli cause graded potentials but no AP. Stimulus strength is encoded by AP frequency, not amplitude.

The refractory period has two parts:

  • Absolute refractory: Na+ channels are inactivated (a third state, distinct from closed). Cannot fire another AP. Lasts ~1-2 ms after the spike.
  • Relative refractory: Na+ channels are recovering, K+ still open (hyperpolarized). Can fire but needs a stronger-than-normal stimulus. Lasts a few more ms.

Saltatory conduction: in myelinated axons, the AP "jumps" between Nodes of Ranvier (gaps in myelin). Far faster than continuous conduction. Multiple sclerosis demyelinates axons — signals slow and degrade.

Common student misconceptions
  • "AP gets stronger with stronger stimulus." No — AP is fixed in size (all-or-nothing). Stimulus strength is encoded by the frequency of APs, not their amplitude.
  • "Na+/K+ pump generates the AP." No — the pump maintains the resting gradient. APs are driven by voltage-gated channels, which use the gradient the pump set up.
  • "K+ flowing out depolarizes the cell." Opposite. K+ out HYPER-polarizes (it's positive leaving, making inside more negative). K+ efflux is what brings the cell back down after the spike.
  • "AP travels in both directions along the axon." Refractory period prevents backward propagation — channels behind the wave are inactivated.

Section 03 · Physiology

Synaptic transmission: vesicle → cleft → receptor.

When an action potential reaches an axon terminal, Ca2+ rushes in and triggers synaptic vesicle fusion via SNARE proteins. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synapse and bind postsynaptic receptors: ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels, fast) or metabotropic (GPCRs, slow). EPSPs and IPSPs are algebraically summed at the axon hillock. Precise termination of signaling requires reuptake transporters and enzymatic breakdown.

The trap

GABA is inhibitory (opens Cl- channels, hyperpolarizes). Glutamate is excitatory (main). Acetylcholine can be EITHER: excitatory at the NMJ (ionotropic nicotinic), inhibitory at the heart (muscarinic, GPCR). Dopamine and serotonin are mostly metabotropic — slower, modulatory. Students mix up ionotropic vs metabotropic speed and mechanism.

The rule

AP → Ca2+ in → SNARE → vesicle fuses → NT into cleft. Ionotropic = ion channel opens directly (fast, ms). Metabotropic = GPCR → 2nd messenger (slow, seconds). EPSP: Na+/Ca2+ in, depolarizing. IPSP: Cl- in or K+ out, hyperpolarizing. AChE breaks down ACh in cleft. Reuptake transporters recycle most other NTs.

The move

For NT identity Qs: check receptor type (nicotinic vs muscarinic for ACh). For receptor type: ionotropic = faster, directly gated. For summation: temporal = same synapse rapid-fire; spatial = multiple synapses simultaneously.

Ca2+ in → SNARE fusion → NT release. GABA = inhibitory. Glutamate = excitatory.

From CoachCoach notes · synaptic events and neurotransmitters

The synapse converts an electrical signal into a chemical signal and back.

Pre-synaptic sequence: AP arrives at axon terminal → voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open → Ca2+ flows in → binds synaptotagmin → triggers SNARE complex (synaptobrevin/VAMP + syntaxin + SNAP-25) → vesicle-membrane fusion → exocytosis. Botulinum toxin cleaves SNARE proteins → flaccid paralysis.

Neurotransmitters:

  • Acetylcholine (ACh): NMJ = always excitatory (nicotinic nAChR, ionotropic). Cardiac = inhibitory (muscarinic M2, GPCR, K+ opens). AChE in cleft breaks it down.
  • Dopamine: reward, motor control. Metabotropic (D1-D5). Parkinson = dopamine loss in substantia nigra.
  • Serotonin (5-HT): mood, sleep, appetite. Mostly metabotropic. SSRIs block SERT reuptake.
  • GABA: main CNS inhibitory. GABA-A = ionotropic Cl- channel (fast). GABA-B = metabotropic. Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A.
  • Glutamate: main CNS excitatory. AMPA = fast Na+/K+; NMDA = Ca2+ permeable, key in LTP.
  • Glycine: inhibitory in spinal cord (Cl- channel).
  • Norepinephrine: fight-or-flight. Alpha/beta adrenergic receptors (GPCRs).

Post-synaptic integration: EPSP = Na+/Ca2+ in, partial depolarization. IPSP = Cl- in or K+ out, hyperpolarization. Temporal summation = one synapse fires rapidly. Spatial summation = multiple synapses simultaneously. Net sum at axon hillock → if > threshold, AP fires.

Signal termination: reuptake transporters (DAT for dopamine, SERT for serotonin, NET for NE). AChE hydrolyzes ACh. MAO degrades catecholamines. Diffusion away from cleft.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Acetylcholine is always excitatory." At NMJ yes. At cardiac muscle it is inhibitory via muscarinic receptor.
  • "Ionotropic receptors are slower." Opposite — ionotropic = direct ion channel = fast (milliseconds). Metabotropic = GPCR = slower (seconds).
  • "GABA depolarizes the cell." GABA opens Cl- channels — Cl- flows IN — hyperpolarizes. GABA is inhibitory.
  • "Ca2+ flows during the AP to cause it." Neuronal AP = Na+/K+. Ca2+ enters at the terminal via separate voltage-gated Ca2+ channels to trigger vesicle fusion.
See also Physiology · Action potential · the AP arriving at the terminal is what triggers synaptic transmission. These two are sequential events.

Section 03 · Physiology · Nervous

Reflex arc: a spinal-cord shortcut — brain not required.

A reflex bypasses the brain so the response is faster than a voluntary movement. The five components are: receptor → afferent (sensory) neuron → integration center (spinal cord) → efferent (motor) neuron → effector. The simplest reflex (knee-jerk) is monosynaptic — just sensory and motor neurons, one synapse. Withdrawal reflexes are polysynaptic — an interneuron sits in between. The brain is informed in parallel but does not gate the response.

The trap

Students think every reflex involves the brain. They don't — the integration center is the spinal cord. Brain involvement makes a movement voluntary, not reflexive.

The rule

Five components every time. Monosynaptic (1 synapse, 2 neurons) → stretch reflex (knee-jerk). Polysynaptic (≥2 synapses, includes interneuron) → withdrawal, crossed-extensor, most others.

The move

Count the synapses to classify the reflex. Identify the receptor (e.g., muscle spindle for stretch, nociceptor for withdrawal) and the effector (skeletal muscle, gland, smooth muscle). DAVE: Dorsal Afferent, Ventral Efferent.

Stretch reflex: monosynaptic. Withdrawal: polysynaptic. Brain is told in parallel, not in line.

From Coach Coach notes · why monosynaptic is fastest, and the dorsal/ventral split

Reflexes are local circuits. The brain learns about them after the fact.

The patellar (knee-jerk) reflex is the textbook monosynaptic stretch reflex. Tap the patellar tendon → quadriceps muscle is stretched → muscle spindle (Ia sensory ending) fires → afferent neuron travels through the dorsal root into the spinal cord → synapses directly onto an alpha motor neuron in the ventral horn → efferent fibre exits via the ventral root → quadriceps contracts. Two neurons. One synapse. Time from tap to contraction is <50 ms.

The withdrawal reflex (touch hot stove) adds an interneuron. Sensory afferent from skin nociceptor → spinal cord → interneuron (in dorsal/intermediate gray) → motor neuron → effector. The interneuron lets the spinal cord do basic processing (e.g., excite flexors, inhibit extensors via reciprocal inhibition; trigger crossed-extensor on the contralateral side to stabilize posture).

Spinal cord anatomy is on the test. Gray matter is central (H-shape, cell bodies). White matter is peripheral (myelinated axons / tracts). Dorsal roots = sensory in; ventral roots = motor out. Mnemonic: SAME — Sensory Afferent, Motor Efferent.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Reflexes go through the brain." No — the brain is notified in parallel via ascending tracts but the reflex itself is a spinal circuit. That's why a patient with a high spinal cord injury can still have intact reflexes below the lesion.
  • "Monosynaptic means one neuron." Means one synapse — still two neurons (sensory + motor).
  • "Reflexes are conscious." No — you're aware of the result, not the circuit. The action precedes any cortical processing.
  • "All sensory enters the dorsal horn." Yes — dorsal columns / dorsal roots. Motor exits ventrally. Confusing this is a classic DAT trap.
See also Action potential · reflexes ride on the same AP machinery; speed is set by myelination + axon diameter (largest = fastest, e.g., Ia spindle afferents).

Section 03 · Physiology · Nervous

Brain regions: function follows location.

Major regions and what they do: cerebrum (cortex — cognition, voluntary movement, sensation, language), cerebellum (motor coordination, balance, motor learning), brainstem (autonomic vitals: HR, breathing, BP), thalamus (sensory relay for everything except smell), hypothalamus (homeostasis, hormones, hunger/thirst/temperature), hippocampus (memory consolidation), amygdala (emotion, fear, fight-or-flight). Spinal cord: gray matter (cell bodies) inside, white matter (tracts) outside; dorsal in, ventral out.

The trap

Students confuse cerebellum (coordination) with cerebrum (cognition), and conflate thalamus (sensory relay) with hypothalamus (homeostasis + endocrine link). They also forget that smell bypasses the thalamus — goes directly from olfactory bulb to cortex.

The rule

Big lobes (frontal/parietal/temporal/occipital) on the cerebrum. Walnut-shaped cerebellum at the back coordinates movement. Stem at the bottom keeps you alive. Limbic system = emotion + memory (hippocampus + amygdala). Hypothalamus controls pituitary — one-line link between nervous and endocrine systems.

The move

Map any function or symptom to a region. Damage clues: ataxia → cerebellum; memory loss → hippocampus; fear/aggression changes → amygdala; temperature/thirst dysregulation → hypothalamus; respiratory failure → medulla.

Cerebrum = cognition. Cerebellum = coordination. Brainstem = vitals. Thalamus = relay. Hypothalamus = homeostasis. Hippocampus = memory. Amygdala = emotion.

Memorize the map. The DAT will quiz region ↔ function mappings.

Cerebrum lobes (cortex):

  • Frontal: voluntary movement (motor cortex), planning, personality, executive function, Broca's area (speech production).
  • Parietal: somatosensory cortex (touch, proprioception), spatial reasoning.
  • Temporal: auditory cortex, Wernicke's area (language comprehension), face recognition.
  • Occipital: visual cortex.

Brainstem (midbrain → pons → medulla, top to bottom): contains the cardiac, respiratory, and vasomotor centers. Damage to the medulla = death. The reticular activating system here controls arousal/sleep.

Limbic system (deep brain): hippocampus writes new long-term memories (damage = anterograde amnesia, e.g., patient H.M.); amygdala processes fear/aggression and tags emotionally significant memories.

Spinal cord: gray matter is central (H-shape, cell bodies). White matter is peripheral (myelinated tracts — ascending sensory and descending motor). Dorsal columns carry fine touch + proprioception; spinothalamic tract carries pain + temperature; corticospinal tract is the descending motor highway.

Common student misconceptions
  • "All sensation goes through the thalamus." Smell (olfaction) bypasses the thalamus — olfactory bulb projects directly to the olfactory cortex. The DAT loves this exception.
  • "Hypothalamus and pituitary are the same thing." Hypothalamus is the brain region; pituitary is the endocrine gland it controls. Hypothalamus releases hormones that act on the pituitary (anterior) or sends axons directly to it (posterior, releasing ADH and oxytocin).
  • "Cerebellum controls fine motor strength." Cerebellum coordinates and times movement; strength is set by motor cortex + cord output. Cerebellar damage = ataxia (uncoordinated movement), not weakness.

Section 03 · Physiology · Nervous

Special senses: five receptors, five transduction stories.

Vision: light → cornea → lens → retina → rods (B&W, dim) + cones (color, bright, R/G/B) → bipolar → ganglion → optic nerve. Hearing: sound → tympanic membrane → ossicles (malleus → incus → stapes) → oval window → cochlea → hair cells on the basilar membrane (high freq at base, low freq at apex). Vestibular: semicircular canals (rotation), utricle/saccule (linear). Taste: 5 modalities (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami) on tongue papillae. Smell: olfactory epithelium → olfactory bulb → cortex (only sense that bypasses the thalamus).

The trap

Students mix up rods vs cones (rods = dim/B&W, cones = bright/color), confuse the cochlea (hearing) with the semicircular canals (rotational balance), and forget that olfaction is the lone sense that bypasses the thalamus.

The rule

Photoreceptors hyperpolarize in light (less glutamate release — counterintuitive). Lens accommodation: ciliary muscle contracts → suspensory ligaments slack → lens rounds → near vision. Relaxation → lens flattens → far vision. Cochlear hair cells: stereocilia bend → tip-link channels open → K+/Ca2+ influx → depolarization.

The move

From CoachCoach notes · the cardiac action potential

The cardiac AP has a plateau. The neuronal AP does not.

Cardiac myocytes use a 5-phase action potential. The signature feature is the plateau (Phase 2) — a long stretch where the membrane stays depolarized while Ca²⁺ flows IN and K⁺ flows OUT in near-balance. This plateau is why each heartbeat lasts hundreds of milliseconds instead of the millisecond-scale neuronal spike, and it is what allows ventricular contraction to fully eject blood before the next beat fires.

5 phases

Phase 4 (resting): ~ −90 mV, held by K⁺ leak channels.
Phase 0 (upstroke): fast voltage-gated Na⁺ in → rapid depolarization toward +30 mV.
Phase 1 (early repol): Na⁺ channels close, transient K⁺ out → small dip.
Phase 2 (plateau): slow L-type Ca²⁺ IN balances K⁺ OUT → membrane held near 0 mV for ~150–300 ms.
Phase 3 (repol): Ca²⁺ channels close, K⁺ efflux dominates → back to −90 mV.

The why

The plateau exists for one functional reason: to prevent tetanus. Skeletal muscle can sum twitches into a sustained contraction because its refractory period is short. Cardiac muscle cannot — the plateau extends the absolute refractory period through nearly all of contraction, so a second stimulus arrives only after the heart has finished pumping. This is what protects the heart from fatal fused contractions.

Neuronal vs cardiac

Both: Phase 0 is fast Na⁺ in; Phase 3 is K⁺ out. Difference: the neuronal AP repolarizes immediately after the spike (~1 ms total). The cardiac AP pauses at the top with Ca²⁺ in, K⁺ out for ~200 ms before repolarizing. Pacemaker cells (SA / AV nodes) are a third pattern: no fast Na⁺ channels at all — they use slow Ca²⁺ for both Phase 4 drift and Phase 0 upstroke. That is why beta-blockers slow the rate (they target the Ca²⁺-driven funny current) without flattening the QRS.

Cardiac plateau = Ca²⁺ in balanced by K⁺ out. Function = prevent tetanus, allow full ejection.
Common student misconceptions
  • "The cardiac AP is just a slower neuronal AP." No — it has an extra phase (the plateau) driven by a different ion (Ca²⁺) for a specific functional reason (refractory extension).
  • "Calcium is just for muscle contraction inside the cell." Ca²⁺ also drives the plateau electrically AND triggers contraction via calcium-induced calcium release from the SR. Both jobs at once.
  • "All heart cells use the same AP." Contractile myocytes (ventricle/atrium) use the 5-phase Na⁺/Ca²⁺/K⁺ pattern above. Pacemaker cells (SA/AV) skip fast Na⁺ entirely — Ca²⁺ does both phases.

From CoachCoach notes · the RAAS cascade

RAAS = the body's blood-pressure thermostat. Renin → angiotensin → aldosterone.

When blood pressure drops (e.g., dehydration, hemorrhage), the kidney's juxtaglomerular cells detect low renal blood flow and release RENIN. Renin kicks off a cascade that restores blood pressure within minutes-to-hours.

The cascade

1. Low BP detected by kidney → JG cells release RENIN.
2. Renin cleaves circulating ANGIOTENSINOGEN (made by liver) → ANGIOTENSIN I (inactive).
3. ANGIOTENSIN CONVERTING ENZYME (ACE), in lung capillaries, cleaves ANG I → ANGIOTENSIN II (active).
4. Ang II does two things: (a) potent VASOCONSTRICTOR — raises peripheral resistance, raises BP. (b) Triggers adrenal cortex to release ALDOSTERONE.
5. Aldosterone tells the kidney's late DCT + collecting duct: retain Na+, lose K+. Water follows Na+ → blood volume rises → BP rises.
6. Negative feedback: now-normal BP suppresses renin release → cascade winds down.

The trap

Students conflate ALDOSTERONE with ADH. They're different: ALDOSTERONE acts at late DCT, controls Na+ (and water follows osmotically) + K+ exchange — slower, longer-lasting BP control. ADH (vasopressin) acts at collecting duct, inserts AQUAPORIN-2 to directly absorb water — faster, controls plasma osmolarity. RAAS responds to BP; ADH responds to osmolarity (and BP if severe).

DAT integration

RAAS is THE classic cardio + renal + endocrine integration story. The DAT loves to test: (1) where each step happens (kidney for renin, liver for substrate, lung for ACE, adrenal cortex for aldosterone, kidney DCT for aldosterone action); (2) what Ang II does (vasoconstrict + trigger aldosterone); (3) the contrast with ADH (Na+ vs water control). ACE inhibitors (lisinopril, enalapril) block step 3 — used for hypertension, heart failure.

Low BP → kidney renin → liver substrate → lung ACE → adrenal aldosterone → kidney retains Na+ + water → BP up.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Aldosterone controls water reabsorption directly." Aldosterone controls Na+ reabsorption (and K+ secretion); water follows Na+ osmotically. ADH is the direct water controller.
  • "RAAS is just one hormone." RAAS is a multi-step cascade with multiple organs (kidney + liver + lung + adrenal). Each step is a separate testable concept.
  • "ACE is in the kidney." ACE is on the endothelium of LUNG capillaries (highest concentration), although it's elsewhere too. Pulmonary localization explains why ACE inhibitors can cause cough (substance P accumulation in lung).

Each segment has one job. Memorize them.

Glomerulus + Bowman's capsule: pressure-driven filtration. Water + small solutes (ions, glucose, AAs, urea) pushed into Bowman's space. Proteins + cells stay in blood (size barrier).

Section 03 · Structure & Function

Respiratory: gas exchange, O2 dissociation, Bohr.

Lungs move air; alveoli exchange gas. Hemoglobin O2 dissociation curve is sigmoidal (cooperative). Right shift (release O2 in tissues): ↑CO2, ↑H+ (low pH), ↑temp, ↑BPG. Left shift (hold O2 in lungs): opposite, plus fetal Hb. Control by medulla via central + peripheral chemoreceptors (mostly CO2-driven).

The trap

Breathing is driven primarily by CO2, NOT O2. Hyperventilating blows off CO2 → respiratory alkalosis → can pass out. Bohr effect = MORE acidic = MORE O2 release (right shift).

The rule

O2-Hb cooperative binding (sigmoidal). Right shift = LOWER affinity = MORE O2 unloaded (working tissues). Left shift = HIGHER affinity = O2 stays bound (lungs, fetal Hb). Drive = arterial pCO2 (central chemoreceptors in medulla detect CSF H+).

The move

Read shift direction by context. Exercise/tissue → right (deliver O2). Lungs/fetal → left. CO2 leaves blood as bicarbonate; carbonic anhydrase critical.

From CoachCoach notes · gas exchange and the Bohr effect

You don't breathe to get O2 in. You breathe to get CO2 out.

Anatomy: nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveoli. Surfactant (Type II pneumocytes) reduces alveolar surface tension (Laplace).

Mechanics: diaphragm contracts (down) + external intercostals lift ribs → thoracic volume increases → pressure drops → air rushes in (Boyle). Expiration is passive at rest (elastic recoil).

Hemoglobin: 4 subunits, each binds 1 O2 → cooperative binding → sigmoidal curve. P50 (~26 mmHg in adult) = pO2 at 50% saturation.

Bohr effect (right shift = release O2):

  • ↑ CO2 (working muscles produce it)
  • ↑ H+ (low pH, lactic acid)
  • ↑ Temperature (hot working muscle)
  • ↑ 2,3-BPG (in chronic hypoxia, high altitude)

Left shift (hold O2): opposite of above, PLUS fetal Hb (HbF) has higher O2 affinity (γ chains don't bind BPG well) → pulls O2 from maternal blood across placenta. CO has 200× Hb affinity (also left shift).

CO2 transport: ~70% as bicarbonate (CA in RBC: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 → HCO3⁻ + H⁺), ~23% bound to Hb (carbamino), ~7% dissolved.

Control of breathing: medulla (DRG, VRG) sets rhythm. Pons modulates. Central chemoreceptors in medulla detect CSF H+ (proxy for arterial CO2). Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid, aortic bodies) detect pO2, pCO2, pH. CO2 is the dominant driver UNLESS pO2 drops below ~60 mmHg (then hypoxic drive kicks in — careful in COPD patients).

Common student misconceptions
  • "Breathing rate is set by O2 levels." Mostly CO2 (via H+ in CSF). O2 only takes over below ~60 mmHg (peripheral chemoreceptors).
  • "Right shift = stronger O2 binding." Opposite — right shift = LOWER affinity = MORE unloading. Useful in tissues.
  • "CO2 travels in blood mostly bound to Hb." ~70% as bicarbonate, only ~23% on Hb (carbamino).
  • "Surfactant increases surface tension." It REDUCES surface tension, prevents alveolar collapse (atelectasis).

From CoachCoach notes · lung volumes and capacities

Five volumes, four capacities. Capacities are SUMS of volumes.

The DAT tests lung volumes via spirometry graphs — students must read off tidal volume, vital capacity, residual volume, etc. Build the mental picture: a normal breath in/out (tidal), plus what you can force in (IRV) and out (ERV), plus what stays in even after maximal exhalation (RV, can't be measured by spirometry).

Five VOLUMES

TV = Tidal Volume (~500 mL): the amount you move in/out of a normal resting breath.
IRV = Inspiratory Reserve Volume (~3000 mL): extra you can force IN beyond TV.
ERV = Expiratory Reserve Volume (~1100 mL): extra you can force OUT beyond TV.
RV = Residual Volume (~1200 mL): air STUCK in the lungs after maximal exhalation — can't be measured by spirometry (you can't blow it out).
Total: ~5800 mL for a typical adult male.

Four CAPACITIES (= sums)

IC = Inspiratory Capacity = TV + IRV (everything you can inhale from resting end-expiration).
FRC = Functional Residual Capacity = ERV + RV (what's left in lungs at end of normal exhalation).
VC = Vital Capacity = TV + IRV + ERV (full max-in-to-max-out swing — what spirometry measures).
TLC = Total Lung Capacity = VC + RV = everything (~5800 mL).

The traps

(1) RV cannot be measured by spirometry (residual = stuck). You measure it by gas dilution (helium) or body plethysmography.
(2) COPD/emphysema → AIR TRAPPING → RV goes UP, VC goes DOWN, TLC may stay similar (just shifted).
(3) Restrictive disease (fibrosis) → ALL volumes go DOWN.
(4) Vital capacity (max in to max out) ≠ Tidal volume (one normal breath). Read the question.

Five volumes (TV, IRV, ERV, RV — but RV is unmeasurable). Capacities are sums: IC, FRC, VC, TLC.
From CoachCoach notes · Frank-Starling law

The heart pumps out what comes in. More stretch → more force, within limits.

The FRANK-STARLING LAW: cardiac output is regulated by venous return. The more blood that fills the ventricle (PRELOAD), the more the cardiac muscle stretches, and the harder it contracts on the next beat. Within physiological limits, this is automatic and intrinsic — no nervous-system control needed.

The mechanism

Stretched cardiac muscle fibers achieve a more OPTIMAL actin-myosin overlap, giving more cross-bridge cycling per contraction. Increased preload → more stretch → more force → more stroke volume. The relationship plots as an upward curve that eventually plateaus (over-stretching loses overlap).

Why it matters

The two ventricles can match output without conscious control: if the RV pumps 10% more blood through the lungs, the LV automatically receives 10% more preload and pumps 10% more out. This intrinsic matching is what keeps pulmonary and systemic circuits in balance. Failure of Frank-Starling (e.g., severe heart failure) → blood backs up.

DAT trap

Don't confuse PRELOAD (volume of blood stretching the ventricle before contraction — what Frank-Starling responds to) with AFTERLOAD (the pressure the ventricle must pump against, like aortic pressure — increased afterload REDUCES stroke volume). Different concepts, opposite effects on stroke volume.

More preload (stretch) → more force → more stroke volume. Automatic, intrinsic, no nervous control needed.

Endocrine: steroid vs peptide, axes, feedback.

Hormones are chemical messengers from glands to distant targets. Steroid (cortisol, aldosterone, sex hormones, vitamin D): lipid-soluble, cross membrane, bind intracellular receptors, alter transcription, slow/long-lasting. The rule

The move

Immune: innate vs adaptive, MHC I vs II, B vs T cells.

Innate: born-with, fast, non-specific (skin, neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells, complement, inflammation). Adaptive: learned, specific, slower first response, FAST on second exposure (memory). T cells (cell-mediated, MHC-restricted) and B cells (humoral, antibodies). MHC I on ALL nucleated cells presents to CD8 (kill if infected). MHC II on APCs (dendritic, macrophages, B cells) presents to CD4 (helper, coordinator).

The trap

CD4 = MHC II (the 4×2 = 8 mnemonic — they multiply to 8). CD8 = MHC I. B cells make antibodies AND act as APCs. Antibodies don't kill directly — they neutralize, opsonize, activate complement, or trigger ADCC.

The rule

Innate = pattern recognition (PAMPs by TLRs), non-specific, no memory. Adaptive = specific receptors (TCR, BCR), clonal selection, MEMORY. MHC I = endogenous (intracellular) antigens → CD8 cytotoxic T → kills cell. MHC II = exogenous (engulfed) antigens → CD4 helper T → coordinates response.

The move

For "first exposure vs second": same path is faster + bigger 2nd time = adaptive memory. For "infected cell killed": CD8 + MHC I. For "antibody class switch": helper T + B cell + cytokines.

CD4 helps; CD8 kills. CD4 × MHC II = 8. CD8 × MHC I = 8.

From Coach Coach notes · the synapse from action potential to postsynaptic decision

Action potentials are electrical. Synapses are chemical. Synaptic transmission is the conversion.

The presynaptic side:

  • 1. AP arrives at the axon terminal. The depolarization spreads to the membrane around the active zone.
  • 2. Voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open. Ca2+ is highly concentrated outside the cell; it floods in down its gradient.
  • 3. Ca2+ binds synaptotagmin, the calcium sensor on docked vesicles.
  • 4. SNARE proteins (synaptobrevin/VAMP on vesicle — v-SNARE; syntaxin + SNAP-25 on plasma membrane — t-SNARE) zip together, forcing the vesicle and membrane to fuse.
  • 5. Neurotransmitter dumps into the synaptic cleft (~20-40 nm wide).
  • Tetanus toxin cleaves synaptobrevin in inhibitory neurons → loss of inhibition → muscle spasm. Botulinum toxin cleaves SNAREs at NMJ → flaccid paralysis.

The neurotransmitters (DAT-relevant set):

  • Acetylcholine (ACh): at the NMJ (always excites skeletal muscle via nicotinic receptor — ionotropic). In autonomic system, parasympathetic + preganglionic sympathetic. Muscarinic receptors in viscera = metabotropic.
  • Glutamate: principal excitatory NT in the brain. Receptors: AMPA + NMDA + kainate (ionotropic), mGluR (metabotropic). NMDA is voltage- AND ligand-gated (Mg2+ block) — key to learning.
  • GABA: principal inhibitory NT in the brain. GABA-A = ionotropic Cl- channel (benzodiazepines, alcohol potentiate). GABA-B = metabotropic.
  • Glycine: principal inhibitory NT in the spinal cord. Opens Cl- channels.
  • Dopamine: reward, motivation, motor control (substantia nigra, VTA). Metabotropic (D1-D5).
  • Serotonin (5-HT): mood, sleep, GI motility. Mostly metabotropic.
  • Norepinephrine (NE): sympathetic postganglionic + locus coeruleus arousal.

Postsynaptic receptors:

  • Ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels): the NT binding site IS the channel. Fast (ms). Ions flow directly.
    • Cation channel (Na+ in) → depolarize → EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential).
    • Anion channel (Cl- in) → hyperpolarize → IPSP (inhibitory postsynaptic potential).
  • Metabotropic (GPCR): NT binds GPCR → G-protein activates 2nd messenger (cAMP, IP3/DAG, Ca2+) → modifies ion channels indirectly. Slower (100s of ms to seconds), longer-lasting.

The neuromuscular junction (NMJ): motor neuron → ACh → nicotinic ACh receptor on muscle fiber (ionotropic, Na+/K+ permeable) → end-plate potential → muscle AP → excitation-contraction coupling. Always excitatory (no inhibitory NMJ in vertebrates). AChE in the cleft chops ACh fast (~25,000/sec per enzyme) so each AP gets exactly one twitch.

Termination of signal (three modes):

  • Reuptake by transporters on the presynaptic terminal. Most monoamines (dopamine: DAT; serotonin: SERT; norepinephrine: NET). SSRIs block SERT → more serotonin in cleft.
  • Enzymatic degradation in the cleft. ACh → AChE breaks it to acetate + choline; choline reuptaken for resynthesis.
  • Diffusion away from the cleft (especially for peptide NTs).

Summation at the axon hillock: the postsynaptic neuron receives thousands of EPSPs and IPSPs simultaneously. They algebraically sum.

  • Temporal summation: one input fires repeatedly in rapid succession; EPSPs add before previous fades.
  • Spatial summation: multiple inputs fire simultaneously at different synapses; their EPSPs add.
  • If sum at the axon hillock pushes membrane past threshold (~−55 mV), AP fires. Otherwise, nothing.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Sodium triggers vesicle fusion." No — calcium does. The AP brings Na+ into the terminal, but the actual fusion trigger is Ca2+ binding synaptotagmin.
  • "Every EPSP fires an AP." No. A single EPSP usually doesn't reach threshold. Summation is required.
  • "All ACh is excitatory." ACh is excitatory at the NMJ (nicotinic) but inhibitory in the heart via muscarinic-2 receptors (slows heart rate).
  • "Glutamate and GABA are different molecules with separate origins." Actually GABA is synthesized FROM glutamate by GAD (glutamate decarboxylase). Switching one enzyme changes the cell from excitatory to inhibitory.

Sliding filament: cross-bridge cycle, sarcomere bands.

Skeletal muscle contracts when actin and myosin slide past each other. Sarcomere = Z-line to Z-line. I-band (actin only, light) shrinks. A-band (myosin overlap, dark) stays the SAME width. H-zone (myosin only) shrinks. M-line in middle. ATP is needed for myosin DETACHMENT (rigor mortis = no ATP). Ca2+ from SR via T-tubules unblocks tropomyosin via troponin C.

The trap

A-band width does NOT change during contraction. Only I-band and H-zone shrink. Rigor mortis happens because ATP is needed to RELEASE myosin from actin, not to bind. Calcium is the trigger; ATP is the cycle fuel.

The rule

Sarcomere shrinks; filaments don't. Cross-bridge cycle: (1) ATP binds → myosin releases actin. (2) ATP hydrolyzed → myosin "cocks" (high-energy). (3) Ca2+ moves tropomyosin → myosin binds actin. (4) Power stroke + Pi release → ADP released → myosin pulls. Repeat.

The move

For band Qs: only I and H shrink. For ATP/Ca2+: ATP detaches, Ca2+ enables binding. For rigor: no ATP → stuck attached.

A-band constant. I-band + H-zone shrink. ATP for release.

  • 1. ATP binds myosin head → conformational change → myosin RELEASES actin.
  • 2. ATP → ADP + Pi (still bound to head) → head "cocks" to high-energy primed position.
  • 3. Ca2+ binds troponin C → troponin pulls tropomyosin off actin's myosin-binding sites → myosin head BINDS actin (cross-bridge formed).
  • 4. Pi released → power stroke (myosin head pivots ~45°, pulls actin toward M-line). ADP released.
  • 5. Repeat: next ATP binds → release. Without ATP, head stays attached → rigor.

Excitation-contraction coupling: AP travels down sarcolemma → into T-tubule → activates DHPR (voltage sensor) → mechanically/chemically opens RyR (ryanodine receptor) on SR → Ca2+ floods cytosol → binds troponin C → contraction.

Relaxation: SERCA pump (ATP-dependent) pulls Ca2+ back into SR. Tropomyosin re-blocks. Cross-bridges stop forming.

Rigor mortis: after death, ATP runs out. Myosin can't detach. Muscles lock until proteolysis breaks bonds (~24-48 hr).

Smooth muscle differences: no troponin (uses calmodulin → MLCK → phosphorylates myosin light chain). No sarcomeres. Slower, sustained contraction.

Common student misconceptions
  • "A-band shrinks during contraction." A-band = myosin length, which doesn't change. Only I-band and H-zone shrink.
  • "ATP is needed for myosin to BIND actin." Backwards — ATP is needed for RELEASE. Binding happens when Ca2+ unblocks the site.
  • "Filaments shorten during contraction." They don't shorten — they SLIDE past each other. The sarcomere shrinks, the filaments don't.
  • "Smooth muscle uses troponin." Smooth muscle uses calmodulin + MLCK pathway. Troponin is striated muscle (skeletal + cardiac).
