Same geographic area. Driven by polyploidy (common in plants), habitat differentiation, sexual selection.

Adjacent populations with a hybrid zone. Some gene flow continues but environmental gradient drives divergence.

PREZYGOTIC ISOLATION
Prevents fertilization
  • Habitat: different niches (snakes — water vs land)
  • Temporal: different seasons (Rana frogs)

Population growth

Exponential: unlimited resources → J-curve. Logistic: carrying capacity K → S-curve. r-selected: many small offspring, no parental care, early maturity (insects, weeds, mice). K-selected: few large offspring, parental care, late maturity (elephants, humans).

Survivorship curves

Type I: high early survival, late mortality (K-selected). Type II: constant rate of mortality (some birds). Type III: high juvenile mortality, survivors live long (r-selected).

Trophic energy flow (10% rule)

~10% energy transfers up each trophic level (rest = heat, metabolism). Energy pyramid always upright; biomass + numbers can invert (parasites, plankton).

Nitrogen cycle

N₂ fixation (Rhizobium, cyanobacteria) → NH₃ / NH₄⁺ → Nitrification (Nitrosomonas → NO₂⁻; Nitrobacter → NO₃⁻) → assimilation by plants → ammonification (decomposers → NH₄⁺) → denitrification back to N₂.

Phosphorus cycle has NO atmospheric phase (rock weathering only). Carbon: photosynthesis ↔ respiration. Water: evaporation, transpiration, precipitation.

Ecology
Quick reference

Competitive exclusion (Gause): two species with identical niches can't coexist.

Fundamental niche: potential. Realized niche: actual (after competition). Connell's barnacles.

Keystone species: disproportionately large effect (Pisaster sea star).

Primary succession: no soil (lichens pioneer). Secondary: soil intact (post-fire).

Mimicry: Batesian (harmless mimics harmful) vs Müllerian (two harmful converge).

Decreased response to repeated harmless stimulus. The simplest form of learning.

EX: Aplysia gill withdrawal reflex weakens with repeated touch

Pavlov. Pair an unconditioned stimulus (UCS) with a conditioned stimulus (CS) → CS alone evokes the response (CR).

EX: bell + food → dog salivates to bell alone
Operant ConditioningASSOC

Skinner. Behavior modified by consequences. Positive reinforcement (add reward) or negative (remove aversive) → increase behavior. Punishment → decrease. Negative reinforcement ≠ punishment (gotcha!).

Novel problem-solving without trial and error. Suggests cognitive map / understanding.

EX: vampire bat blood-sharing (Trivers)

Visual, auditory, chemical (pheromones), tactile, electric. Bee waggle dance codes distance + direction (von Frisch).

Section 06 · Evolution

Natural selection: five evolutionary mechanisms.

Selection is one of five mechanisms that change allele frequencies. Three SHIFT-the-mean variants (directional, stabilizing, disruptive) plus balancing (maintains polymorphism) plus genetic drift (random sampling, no selection). Plus mutation + gene flow.

The trap

Students confuse "fitness" with "physical strength" or "survival." Fitness = reproductive success. They miss that balancing selection MAINTAINS variation while directional REDUCES it.

The rule

Directional: one extreme favored, mean shifts. Stabilizing: intermediates favored, variance shrinks. Disruptive: extremes favored, mean splits. Balancing: heterozygote advantage / freq-dependent → polymorphism kept. Drift: random, stronger in small populations.

The move

Identify mode by what happens to the population's curve: shift = directional; narrow = stabilizing; split = disruptive; stable poly = balancing; random = drift.

Fitness = reproduction, not survival or strength.

DifficultyMedium
TierEvolution foundation · High-yield

Sickle-cell allele frequency stays high in malaria-endemic regions because:
From CoachCoach notes · the five mechanisms

Selection is one mechanism. Five total change allele frequencies.

Hardy-Weinberg's 5 conditions for NO evolution: no mutation, no migration, no selection, random mating, infinite population. Violate any → allele frequencies change.

Selection types (variation in fitness):

  • Directional: one extreme favored. Mean shifts over generations. Reduces variation only at disfavored end. Antibiotic resistance, peppered moths.
  • Stabilizing: intermediate favored, both extremes selected against. Reduces variance, mean unchanged. Human birth weight (~7 lbs optimal).
  • Disruptive (diversifying): both extremes favored, intermediates selected against. Bimodal distribution → can drive sympatric speciation. African seedcrackers, cichlid mouth morphs.
  • Balancing: maintains polymorphism. Heterozygote advantage (HbAS resists malaria) or frequency-dependent selection (rare morph favored). Both alleles persist long-term.

Genetic drift (random): Stronger in SMALL populations. Bottleneck = catastrophic reduction (cheetahs). Founder effect = small group colonizes new area (Amish polydactyly). Both reduce diversity, not via fitness.

Mutation: ultimate source of variation. Rare, but feeds the rest. Gene flow: migration between populations homogenizes them, opposes divergence.

Common student misconceptions
  • "Fitness = strength." Fitness = lifetime reproductive contribution. A weak organism with many fertile offspring has higher fitness than a strong one with none.
  • "Convergent evolution → homologous structures." Backwards. Convergent → analogous (independent origins, same function). Divergent → homologous (same origin, possibly different function).
  • "Stabilizing selection shifts the mean." No — it reduces variance. Directional shifts the mean.
  • "Founder effect = bottleneck." Different. Founder = colonization of new area by small group. Bottleneck = catastrophic kill.

Section 06 · Evolution

Speciation: allopatric, sympatric, peripatric, parapatric.

New species form via reproductive isolation. Allopatric: geographic barrier separates. Sympatric: same area (polyploidy in plants, habitat shift). Peripatric: small founder pop. Parapatric: hybrid zone. Plus prezygotic vs postzygotic isolation mechanisms.

The trap

Students mix up sympatric and parapatric. They forget mules = postzygotic (hybrid sterility), not prezygotic. Different breeding seasons = temporal (prezygotic).

The rule

Allopatric = geographic. Sympatric = same area, often via polyploidy. Peripatric = small peripheral pop, drift-heavy. Parapatric = adjacent + hybrid zone. Prezygotic isolation: habitat, temporal, behavioral, mechanical, gametic. Postzygotic: hybrid inviability, sterility, breakdown.

The move

Identify mode by geography (separate? same?). Identify isolation by timing (before fertilization? after?).

Mule = postzygotic. Different seasons = temporal (prezygotic).
PriorityHigh
DifficultyMedium
TierEvolution · High-yield
Typical question wording
Which type of reproductive isolation does the sterility of mules represent?
From CoachCoach notes · speciation modes + isolation types

Allopatric is most common. Sympatric in plants via polyploidy.

Allopatric speciation: geographic barrier (river, mountain range, ocean) separates one population. Each accumulates different mutations + selection pressures → diverge → reproductively incompatible. Examples: Grand Canyon squirrels (Kaibab N rim, Abert S rim), Galápagos finches.

Sympatric speciation: new species in SAME area. Common in plants via polyploidy (autopolyploidy = same species genome doubled; allopolyploidy = hybrid + chromosome doubling — instant speciation). In animals: habitat shift (apple maggot fly Rhagoletis), sexual selection (Lake Victoria cichlids).

Peripatric: tiny peripheral founder population. Drift-heavy. Special case of allopatric. Hawaiian Drosophila exploded into 800+ species this way.

Parapatric: adjacent populations with hybrid zone. Some gene flow continues; environmental gradient drives divergence (mine-tolerant grass at boundaries).

Prezygotic isolation: habitat, temporal (different breeding seasons), behavioral (mating signals), mechanical (genital incompatibility), gametic (sperm-egg protein mismatch).

Postzygotic isolation: hybrid inviability, hybrid sterility (mule, liger), hybrid breakdown (F2 weak).

Common student misconceptions
  • "Mules represent prezygotic isolation." Mules form (zygote viable) but are sterile. That's POSTzygotic — hybrid sterility.
  • "Sympatric speciation requires geographic separation." Opposite — sympatric = SAME geography. Allopatric requires separation.
  • "Polyploidy is rare." Common in plants. ~50% of plant species are polyploid. Drives sympatric speciation.

Section 06 · Evolution & Ecology

Ecology: population growth, r vs K, succession, energy flow.

Levels: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere. Exponential growth (dN/dt = rN) is unconstrained. Logistic growth (dN/dt = rN(K−N)/K) levels off at carrying capacity K. r-strategists: many offspring, little parental care, opportunistic. K-strategists: few offspring, lots of care, near-K. Energy flows up trophic levels at ~10% efficiency.

The trap

Carrying capacity (K) isn't fixed — it depends on resources. Density-dependent factors (disease, competition) regulate near K; density-independent (weather, fire) hit regardless of population size. Primary succession starts on bare rock (no soil); secondary succession on disturbed but soiled ground.

The rule

Exponential = unlimited resources (early growth, invasive species). Logistic = resource-limited, plateaus at K. r-strategy: small body, fast maturation, many young, low survival per young (insects, weeds). K-strategy: long-lived, late mature, few young, high investment (elephants, humans).

The move

For growth Qs: read the curve. S-shape = logistic. J-shape = exponential. For succession: bare rock = primary; cleared field = secondary.

Energy transfer ≈ 10% per trophic level.

From CoachCoach notes · population, community, ecosystem

Patterns at scale, from individuals to biomes.

Population growth equations:

  • Exponential: dN/dt = rN. Doubles per generation. Fits early colonization, lab cultures, invasive species without predators.
  • Logistic: dN/dt = rN((K−N)/K). When N is small, behaves like exponential. As N → K, growth slows. At N = K, growth = 0.

Carrying capacity K: max population a habitat can sustain given resources. Not fixed — changes with conditions.

r-selected vs K-selected:

  • r-strategists: high r (fast intrinsic growth). Many offspring, low survival each, little parental care, often unstable habitats. Insects, dandelions, bacteria, mice.
  • K-strategists: populations near K. Few offspring, high survival, long lives, parental investment. Whales, elephants, humans, oaks.

Density-dependent vs independent factors:

  • Density-dependent: intensity scales with N — disease, competition, predation (often). Drives population to K.
  • Density-independent: weather, drought, fire, hurricanes. Hit regardless of N.

Community ecology:

  • Competition (−,−): two species using same resource. Competitive exclusion principle: two species can't occupy identical niches indefinitely. Resource partitioning resolves it.
  • Predation (+,−): hare-lynx oscillation.
  • Mutualism (+,+): mycorrhizae, lichens, gut microbes.
  • Commensalism (+,0): epiphytes on trees.
  • Parasitism (+,−): tapeworms, ticks.
  • Keystone species: disproportionate effect on community (sea otters, wolves at Yellowstone).

Succession:

  • Primary succession: starts on lifeless substrate (bare rock, lava flow). Pioneer species = lichens (break rock → soil), then mosses, then plants.
  • Secondary succession: after disturbance that left soil (fire, plowed field, clearcut). Faster — soil + seed bank already there.
  • Climax community: stable end state (in classical view; modern ecology sees cycles + disturbance dynamics).

Energy flow:

  • Producers (autotrophs): capture solar/chemical energy. Plants, algae, chemoautotrophs.
  • Primary consumers: herbivores.
  • Secondary consumers: carnivores eating herbivores.
  • Tertiary consumers: top predators.
  • ~10% rule: energy passes up at ~10% efficiency. Why food chains rarely exceed 4-5 levels.
  • Decomposers: bacteria + fungi recycle nutrients.

Biogeochemical cycles:

  • Carbon cycle: CO2 ↔ photosynthesis ↔ biomass ↔ respiration / combustion. Long-term storage in fossil fuels and limestone.
  • Nitrogen cycle (DAT-tested step names + organisms):
    • Nitrogen fixation: N2 (atmospheric, ~78%) → NH3/NH4+. Done by Rhizobium (legume root nodules — the textbook symbiotic fixer), free-living Azotobacter, cyanobacteria, plus abiotic via lightning. Catalyzed by the nitrogenase enzyme — oxygen-sensitive (root nodules use leghemoglobin to scavenge O2).
    • Nitrification (two steps, two genera): NH4+ → NO2- by Nitrosomonas (and Nitrosococcus); then NO2- → NO3- by Nitrobacter. Both are chemoautotrophic soil bacteria. Plants prefer nitrate (NO3-) for uptake.
    • Assimilation: plants take up NO3- (or NH4+) and incorporate N into amino acids, nucleotides, chlorophyll. Animals get N by eating plants or other animals.
    • Ammonification: decomposers (saprophytic bacteria, fungi) break down dead organic matter and waste → release NH3/NH4+ back to the soil.
    • Denitrification: NO3- → N2 (back to atmosphere) by Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and other anaerobic soil bacteria. Closes the cycle.
    Mnemonic for soil order: Fix → Nitrify (Nitrosomonas, then Nitrobacter) → Assimilate → Ammonify → Denitrify.
  • Water cycle: evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation.
  • Phosphorus cycle: no atmospheric phase; weathering of rock → soil → plants → animals → decomposers. Often the limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Carrying capacity is constant." K depends on resources, weather, etc. It changes.
  • "r-strategists are smarter." Different strategy, not better. Fits unstable habitats.
  • "Primary and secondary succession differ in plants used." They differ in STARTING substrate. Primary = no soil (bare rock). Secondary = soil intact (after disturbance).
  • "~10% energy transfer means 90% is "lost"." 90% used for the consumer's own metabolism, heat (2nd law), undigested. Not literally lost from the universe.

Section 06 · Ecology

Community interactions: competition, predation, symbiosis.

Two organisms in the same community can affect each other's fitness in five basic ways. Three are symbiotic (close, long-term association: mutualism +/+, commensalism +/0, parasitism +/−). Two are non-symbiotic: competition (−/−) and predation/herbivory (+/− but not embedded). Add the niche concept (fundamental vs realized) and competitive exclusion, and you have most of the DAT's community ecology.

The trap

Students confuse commensalism (one benefits, the other is unaffected: +/0) with mutualism (both benefit: +/+). They forget competitive exclusion: two species occupying the SAME niche cannot stably coexist — one is excluded.

The rule

Use the +/−/0 sign rule for the SECOND organism. Mutualism: +/+. Commensalism: +/0. Parasitism: +/− (parasite benefits, host harmed but usually not killed). Predation: +/− (predator benefits, prey killed). Competition: −/− (both lose). Niche: the role + resources a species uses. Fundamental = could occupy in absence of competitors. Realized = actually occupies given competition.

The move

For any pair, ask "+/− for each organism." Then assign the term. For "two species cannot stably coexist on identical resources" → competitive exclusion principle. For "fundamental vs realized" → absence vs presence of competitors.

Mutualism +/+. Commensalism +/0. Parasitism +/−. Predation +/−. Competition −/−.

From CoachCoach notes · the five interactions + niche concepts

Use the +/−/0 grid. Memorize one example per cell.

Mutualism (+/+). Both species benefit. Examples: bees and flowering plants (pollination + nectar); mycorrhizae (fungi and plant roots: fungi get sugars, plants get phosphorus); gut bacteria and humans (digestion aid + warm habitat); cleaner fish and large fish (food + parasite removal); lichens (fungus + algae or cyanobacterium).

Commensalism (+/0). One benefits, the other is unaffected (or barely affected). Examples: barnacles on whales (transport, no harm to whale); epiphytes on tropical trees (sunlight access, no harm to tree); cattle egrets following grazing herds (insects flushed up, no harm to cattle).

Parasitism (+/−). Parasite benefits, host harmed but typically NOT killed (because killing the host hurts the parasite). Examples: tapeworms in vertebrate guts; ticks on mammals; mistletoe on trees; many viruses.

Predation (+/−). Predator benefits, prey killed and eaten. Drives population cycles (Lotka-Volterra-style oscillations: when prey rises, predators rise, prey crashes, predators crash, repeat). Drives evolutionary arms races (camouflage, mimicry, toxins, speed).

Herbivory (+/−). Sometimes considered a form of predation; herbivore benefits, plant harmed but often not killed (browsing vs grazing).

Competition (−/−). Both species are harmed when they share limiting resources (food, water, light, mates, space). Interspecific = between species. Intraspecific = within a species (often most intense).

Niche concepts. A species' niche is its full role — what it eats, where it lives, when it is active, how it reproduces, who eats it.

  • Fundamental niche: the niche a species could occupy in the ABSENCE of competitors and other limiting interactions. Defined by physiological tolerance.
  • Realized niche: the niche it actually occupies given the presence of competitors, predators, and other species. Usually narrower than the fundamental niche.
  • Competitive exclusion principle (Gause): two species cannot stably coexist on the same limiting resources. One excludes the other (locally), or one shifts its niche — resource partitioning.
  • Resource partitioning: closely related species coexist by using different parts of a shared resource (different prey sizes, different feeding times, different parts of a tree).

Keystone species. A species whose impact on the community is disproportionately large relative to its abundance. Removing a keystone species drastically reorganizes the community. Classic examples: sea otters (control sea urchins, which eat kelp), wolves (top-down control on ungulate populations).

Common student misconceptions
  • "Commensalism = mutualism." No — in commensalism only one party benefits; the other is unaffected.
  • "Predators always reduce prey populations to extinction." Usually no — coevolution stabilizes predator-prey dynamics; populations cycle but persist.
  • "Two species can occupy the same niche if they are different sizes." Same niche means same resources at the same time. Different sizes is one form of resource partitioning — meaning the niches are NOT actually identical.
  • "A keystone species is just any abundant species." No — keystone species have outsized effects relative to their abundance, often top predators.

Section 06 · Evolution & Ecology

Behavior: innate vs learned, kin selection, communication.

Innate: genetic, fixed action patterns (FAPs), reflexes — performed correctly on first try. Learned: habituation, classical/operant conditioning, imprinting, insight. Altruism often explained by kin selection (Hamilton's rule: rB > C). Reciprocal altruism is a secondary explanation.

The trap

Imprinting (Konrad Lorenz's ducklings) is a SPECIAL form of learning that happens during a critical period. Hamilton's rule rB > C: a gene for altruism spreads when relatedness × benefit to recipient > cost to actor. r(siblings) = 0.5, r(cousins) = 0.125. "I would die for 2 brothers or 8 cousins" (Haldane).

The rule

Innate = FAP, reflex, genetic. Learned = changes with experience. Habituation = decreased response to repeated harmless stimulus (snail recoil). Classical conditioning = associate two stimuli (Pavlov). Operant = behavior shaped by consequences (Skinner). Imprinting = critical period bonding. Hamilton: rB > C.

The move

For behavior type Qs: untrained → innate. Practice changes outcome → learned. For altruism: compute rB and compare to C.

From CoachCoach notes · the two questions the DAT actually asks about behavior

The DAT asks one behavior question. Two patterns dominate.

Riya's note: behavior is a single-question topic on the DAT. Don't drown in the ethology textbook. Master two things: (1) innate vs learned, and (2) kin selection (Hamilton's rule). Everything else is bonus context.

Innate vs learned:

  • Innate = genetically programmed; performed correctly on first try, no experience needed. Reflexes (knee jerk), fixed action patterns (FAPs — stereotyped sequence triggered by sign stimulus, like goose egg-rolling), migration, circadian rhythms.
  • Learned = changes with experience. Three DAT-relevant types: habituation (decreased response to repeated harmless stimulus), classical conditioning (Pavlov — pair neutral + meaningful stimulus), operant conditioning (Skinner — behavior shaped by reinforcement/punishment).
  • Imprinting (Lorenz's ducklings) is the one named hybrid — learning that locks in during a brief critical period and is largely irreversible.

Kin selection · Hamilton's rule: a gene for altruism spreads when rB > C:

  • r = coefficient of relatedness. Full sibling = 0.5, half-sib = 0.25, first cousin = 0.125, identical twin = 1.
  • B = benefit to recipient (offspring units). C = cost to actor.
  • Haldane's quip captures it: "I would lay down my life for two brothers (2 × 0.5 = 1) or eight cousins (8 × 0.125 = 1)."
  • Worker-bee self-sacrifice for the hive is the canonical DAT example. Inclusive fitness = direct offspring + relatives' offspring weighted by r.
Common student misconceptions
  • "Imprinting is the same as classical conditioning." Imprinting has a critical period and is largely irreversible. Classical conditioning stays flexible.
  • "Altruism contradicts evolution." Inclusive fitness: helping close kin propagates shared genes. rB > C explains it.
  • "Habituation = forgetting." Habituation is an active dampening of response to a specific stimulus, not memory loss.
