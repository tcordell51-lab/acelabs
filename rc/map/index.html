Light in the Deep

1Below about 200 meters, almost no sunlight reaches the ocean, and below 1,000 meters the water is dark to every eye. Yet most animals that live in the deep water column make light of their own, and biologists have spent a century asking how they do it and what the light is for. The answers turn out to differ from one animal to the next.

2The light itself comes from a chemical reaction: an enzyme called luciferase speeds the oxidation of a small molecule called luciferin, and the energy released leaves as light rather than heat. Some animals make both molecules; others house luminous bacteria in special organs and borrow theirs. Most of this light is blue, because blue wavelengths travel farthest through seawater, while red light is absorbed within a few meters.

3One of the most common uses of this light is camouflage. A hatchetfish seen from below would show as a dark shape against the faint light filtering down from above, so it glows from rows of organs along its belly, matching that light and erasing its silhouette. Biologists call this counterillumination, and certain squid do the same with light produced by bacteria they host.

4A few predators break the blue rule. The stoplight loosejaw, a dragonfish, produces red light, and unlike most deep-sea animals it can also see red, so it can light up its prey without being noticed. How many other animals use such private channels is still unknown, because red light is as hard for human instruments to detect at depth as it is for most of the animals living there.
