# Active Reading Techniques: Evidence Base and Transfer to Timed Standardized-Test Reading

Compiled 2026-05-07. All citations include URL and access date. Effect sizes are from the original meta-analysis; "transfer to timed test" is judged separately because most learning-strategy research measures delayed retention, not 60-minute scan-and-answer performance.

---

## Critical framing: studying-for-retention vs. timed-test reading are different tasks

Almost the entire canonical literature (Dunlosky, Pashler/IES, Hattie, Bisra) measures **learning gains** — typically delayed free-recall or comprehension tests taken hours to weeks after study, with no time pressure during reading. The DAT Reading Comprehension section is a different task: ~3.3 minutes per passage to read, ~30 seconds per question, **with the passage available to re-scan**. Encoding for week-out recall is not the goal; building a usable mental map plus a search index, fast, is.

Implication: techniques rated "high utility" by Dunlosky (practice testing, distributed practice) are about *how you train across weeks*, not what you do during the 60-minute section. Techniques rated "low utility" for study (highlighting, rereading) can still be useful as **navigation aids during a timed test** — different mechanism, different evaluation.

Key honest caveats:
- "Effect size for reading comprehension" in Hattie often pools K-12 instructional interventions, not adult timed standardized testing. Transfer is plausible but not directly evidenced.
- No high-quality RCT (that I could find) directly compares active-reading techniques head-to-head on DAT/MCAT/GRE under timed conditions. DAT prep advice is practitioner-driven, not peer-reviewed.

## Technique-by-technique evidence

### 1. Annotation / highlighting / underlining

**Dunlosky et al. (2013):** rated **LOW utility**. "Highlighting has been shown to have failed to help students... may impair inference-making by fragmenting conceptual connections." Provides psychological comfort but no learning advantage; worse for comprehension-transfer tasks. Source: <https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Hattie Visible Learning:** Highlighting/underlining d = 0.50 (rank 82) — at hinge point, modest. Source: <https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Mason et al. (2024), J. Computer Assisted Learning:** Learner-generated highlighting improves memory but **not comprehension**; instructor-provided highlighting improves both. Source: <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12903> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Cognitive load angle:** Marking the text creates split-attention cost during the marking action itself; only pays back if marks become a useful index later. Source: <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X97909516> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: small / inconsistent.
- Time cost during timed test: low if minimal, high if students over-highlight.
- Transfer to timed RC: **plausibly useful as a search index** (locate keywords/dates faster) but not as a comprehension tool. Booster and Bootcamp both warn against over-highlighting.
- Works for science prose: yes for proper nouns, numbers, comparison/contrast markers; poor for inference-question support.

### 2. Paragraph summarization (one-line summary per paragraph)

**Dunlosky:** rated **moderate-to-low utility** — works only after extensive training (90 min instruction + five 50-min coaching sessions in study samples). Once trained, gains are robust. Source: <https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Hattie:** Summarization d = 0.79 (rank 18); Outlining/transforming d = 0.66. Both well above 0.40 hinge. Source: <https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2021), text-structure meta-analysis:** Structure strategies (which include paragraph mapping for expository text) produce significant near- and mid-transfer gains on main-idea identification but only weak far-transfer to general RC. Source: <https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rrq.311> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**DAT-specific:** Bootcamp's "Vanilla" and "Mapping" methods both endorse a few-word summary per paragraph plus paragraph number. Ray's 30-RC breakdown used exactly this: 9-11 min reading-with-map, then 9-10 min answering, using map as index. Source: <https://bootcamp.com/success-stories/rays-detailed-breakdown-for-a-30-in-reading-comprehension> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: large (d ~0.66-0.79) once trained.
- Time cost during timed test: meaningful (1-2 min/passage) but pays back during question phase.
- Transfer to timed RC: **strong** — the only technique that directly produces a search index and a textbase summary simultaneously.
- Works for science prose: yes; expository structure (cause-effect, compare-contrast) maps cleanly to one-line summaries.

### 3. Mental mapping (visualize structure as tree/outline, no writing)

**Hattie:** Concept mapping d = 0.64; Outlining/transforming d = 0.66. Source: <https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Kintsch situation-model literature:** Building a mental representation of "what the passage is about" (situation model) predicts comprehension and search speed; readers who succeed at situation-model construction read faster and retrieve faster. Source: <https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/Cognitive/Readings/Zwaan_Radvansky_1998.pdf> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: moderate-to-large for externalized maps; weaker evidence for purely mental.
- Time cost during timed test: essentially zero (it's what you're doing anyway).
- Transfer to timed RC: **strong** for capable readers; the risk is that without any external anchor, students think they have a map but don't, and have to reread. Externalized (written) maps de-risk this.
- Works for science prose: yes — science RC passages have predictable structures (definition, mechanism, evidence, exception).

### 4. Predictive reading (pause and predict what's next)

**Hattie:** Planning and prediction d = 0.76 (rank 22). Source: <https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Reciprocal teaching (Palincsar & Brown):** Prediction is one of four core strategies; combined intervention raises RC scores from ~30% to ~70% after 15-20 days of training. Aggregate reciprocal-teaching d = 0.74. Source: <https://www.readingrockets.org/classroom/classroom-strategies/reciprocal-teaching> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: large in reciprocal-teaching package; isolated effect of prediction alone is smaller and less well-isolated.
- Time cost during timed test: low if internalized; medium if students pause explicitly.
- Transfer to timed RC: **moderate**. Prediction is most useful for narrative/argumentative text. DAT science passages are descriptive — predicting the next sentence in a paragraph about lateral inhibition is hard and not particularly useful. Better suited for ACT/SAT/LSAT than DAT.
- Works for science prose: weaker than for argumentative prose.

### 5. Question generation while reading (self-questioning)

**Hattie:** Self-verbalization/self-questioning d = 0.55 (rank 63). Source: <https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Question-generation meta-analyses:** Generation effects are statistically significant for factual and near-transfer comprehension, but **far-transfer is weak**. Source: <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ339568> and <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acp.3639> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: moderate.
- Time cost during timed test: high — generating good questions is metacognitively expensive.
- Transfer to timed RC: **low-to-moderate**. Risk: students generate questions that aren't the ones the test asks. Better as a training technique between sessions than during the test.
- Works for science prose: yes for trainees, but inefficient under time pressure.

### 6. Elaborative interrogation ("why is this true?")

**Dunlosky:** rated **moderate utility** but conditional on prior knowledge; can backfire if the learner has no factual base. Source: <https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Hattie:** Elaborative interrogation d = 0.42 (rank 112) — just above hinge point. Source: <https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Original studies (Pressley et al. 1987 and follow-ups):** Strongest for factual recall; effects on comprehension and transfer measured in only ~3 studies, mixed. Source: <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02702711.2025.2482627> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: small-to-moderate.
- Time cost during timed test: **very high** — asking "why" of every claim in a 500-word science passage in 3 minutes is impossible.
- Transfer to timed RC: **low**. This is a study-for-retention tool, not a timed-test tool.
- Works for science prose: best in a knowledge-rich learner (biology major reading bio passages); poor otherwise.

### 7. Self-explanation (explain in own words)

**Bisra et al. (2018) meta-analysis:** 69 effect sizes from 64 studies, n = 5917 learners. Overall weighted g = **0.55**. 41/64 comparisons came from math/science domains — high relevance to DAT science RC. Self-generated explanations outperform provided explanations. Source: <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Dunlosky:** rated **moderate**, with strongest evidence for transfer to novel problems (cited 90% vs 30% gain on reasoning tasks in classic studies). Source: <https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: moderate-to-large (g = 0.55), best transfer evidence of any technique here.
- Time cost during timed test: high if done aloud/in full; can be compressed to a one-sentence "what is this paragraph really saying" — at which point it converges with paragraph summarization.
- Transfer to timed RC: **moderate-to-strong** in compressed form; **low** if applied in its full study-for-retention form.
- Works for science prose: yes — the Bisra meta-analysis is dominated by STEM contexts.

### 8. SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review)

**Robinson (1941); reviewed in multiple sources:** Studies show SQ3R-trained groups beat untreated controls (e.g., mean 67 vs 44 in one quasi-experiment). Source: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQ3R> and <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED080939> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Critical limitation:** "Although intuitively recognized as superior... the modicum of research which compares SQ3R to other methods has provided **inconclusive empirical evidence**." Source: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQ3R> (accessed 2026-05-07).

**Pashler/IES (2007):** SQ3R is not endorsed as one of the seven evidence-based recommendations; the guide emphasizes deep questions, worked examples, spacing, and interleaving. Source: <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED498555> (accessed 2026-05-07).

- Effect on study retention: positive vs. no-treatment controls, no strong evidence vs. other active methods.
- Time cost during timed test: **prohibitive** — 5 steps designed for textbook study, not 3-minute passages.
- Transfer to timed RC: **near zero in its packaged form.** Individual components (survey-then-read, recite/summarize) overlap with paragraph summarization and mental mapping and are useful as components.
- Works for science prose: designed for textbooks; the "Recite" and "Review" steps assume hours-later recall, not 30-seconds-later question answering.

## DAT-specific practitioner consensus (not peer-reviewed)

- **Bootcamp Vanilla Method:** Read full passage 7-9 min, answer 10-12 min, light back-referencing, minimal mapping. Source: <https://bootcamp.com/blog/vanilla-method-for-dat-reading-comprehension> (accessed 2026-05-07).
- **Bootcamp Mapping (Ray's 30):** Read 9-11 min with one-line paragraph summaries + paragraph numbers; answer 9-10 min using map as search index. Source: <https://bootcamp.com/success-stories/rays-detailed-breakdown-for-a-30-in-reading-comprehension> (accessed 2026-05-07).
- **Bootcamp Search-and-Destroy:** Skip the passage; question-driven keyword hunt. Sacrifices inference/tone questions for speed. Author personally found it counterproductive. Source: <https://bootcamp.com/blog/search-destroy-guide-for-dat-reading-comprehension> (accessed 2026-05-07).
- **DAT Booster:** Four-strategy menu (Classic, Search-and-Destroy, Mapping, Hybrid); recommends 6-8 min read + 10-12 min answer; explicitly says no single strategy works universally. Source: <https://boosterprep.com/dat/study-guide/top-strategies-to-master-dat-reading-comprehension> (accessed 2026-05-07).

The practitioner consensus converges on **light paragraph mapping with selective annotation of proper nouns/numbers** as the modal high-scorer approach, with Search-and-Destroy as a fallback for slow readers.

## Speed-accuracy tradeoff (the hard constraint)

Rayner et al. (2016) and Balota et al.: doubling reading rate via "speed reading" reduces comprehension; eye movements are only ~10% of reading time, so the bottleneck is lexical/semantic integration. Any technique that *adds* time during reading must produce more than that cost back during answering. Sources: <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100615623267> and <http://psychnet.wustl.edu/coglab/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/Balota-Speed.pdf> (accessed 2026-05-07).

This is why paragraph summarization is the strongest candidate: the ~60-90 sec it costs during reading is paid back 2-3x during the answer phase by faster passage navigation.
