# RC Stamina, Working Memory Under Stress, and Anxiety on the DAT

Research compiled 2026-05-07 for AceTheDAT. All URLs accessed 2026-05-07. Peer-reviewed sources prioritized; prep-community claims flagged and filtered.

The DAT is a ~4.5-hour, computer-administered exam in this order: Survey of Natural Sciences (90 min), PAT (60 min), optional 30-min break, then Reading Comprehension (60 min, 50 questions over 3 passages), then Quantitative Reasoning (45 min). RC sits at roughly the **3.5- to 4.5-hour mark** of a sustained cognitive task — the worst possible position for a section that taxes working memory and sustained attention. This document gathers what the literature actually shows.

---

## 1. Cognitive fatigue across multi-hour tests

### 1.1 The headline number (Sievertsen, Gino & Pisano, 2016, PNAS)

The largest natural-experiment study of test-time fatigue uses administrative data on all Danish public-school children, 2009/10–2012/13 (~2 million test instances). Because computer availability — not student choice — determined when each child sat a test, the design isolates time-of-day effects cleanly.

- **Performance falls 0.9% of a standard deviation for every hour later in the testing day** (95% CI: 0.7–1.0% SD). [PNAS abstract](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1516947113) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- **A 20–30 minute break recovers 1.7% SD** (95% CI: 1.2–2.2). [Sievertsen et al., PNAS 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26884183/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- Extrapolating linearly to the DAT: RC begins ~3.5 h in, so a student is operating at roughly **3% SD below their hour-1 baseline** going into RC. The break between PAT and RC partially offsets this. Across a full 4.5 h test, raw decline could exceed 4% SD without breaks — a meaningful but not catastrophic effect (~3–4 raw-scale points on tests scored similarly to standardized exams).

### 1.2 The Boksem & Tops mental-fatigue framework (Brain Research Reviews, 2008)

Boksem & Tops re-cast mental fatigue as a **cost/benefit decision** rather than pure resource depletion: nucleus accumbens, orbitofrontal cortex, amygdala, insula, and anterior cingulate cortex jointly evaluate expected reward against energetic cost. Fatigued subjects:

- Have difficulty focusing attention, planning, and adaptively changing strategies after negative outcomes.
- **Correct their mistakes less often**, with impaired post-error performance adjustment.
- Disengage from continued effort unless the anticipated reward is high enough to justify it. [Boksem & Tops, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18652844/) (accessed 2026-05-07)

The clinical implication for RC: fatigued students don't lose IQ points — they lose **error-monitoring** and **strategy-switching**. They commit to a wrong answer and don't re-evaluate.

### 1.3 Sustained-attention vulnerability (Lim & Dinges, 2010)

Lim & Dinges' meta-analysis of 70 studies, 147 cognitive tests under short-term (<48 h) sleep deprivation produced effect sizes (Hedges' ḡ):

- Simple attention/vigilance lapses: ḡ = −0.762 (large)
- Working memory accuracy: ḡ = −0.555 (moderate-large)
- Working memory speed: ḡ = −0.515
- Complex attention accuracy: ḡ = −0.479
- Processing speed accuracy: ḡ = −0.245 (n.s.)
- Reasoning accuracy: ḡ = −0.125 (n.s.) [Lim & Dinges, 2010, PMC3290659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3290659/) (accessed 2026-05-07)

The pattern is clear: **vigilance/sustained attention degrades first and worst**; pure reasoning is most preserved. RC is heavy on sustained attention (3 long passages, 50 questions).

### 1.4 Schmeichel — depletion of executive control

Schmeichel (2007) showed that controlling attention at the start of a study **reduced ability to divide attention** during a subsequent working-memory test. Performing divided-attention tasks impaired subsequent emotion suppression. [Hofmann, Schmeichel & Baddeley, 2012, Trends in Cognitive Sciences](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22336729/) (accessed 2026-05-07)

For the DAT: 90 min of NSS plus 60 min of PAT plus accumulated test anxiety all draw from the same executive-control pool that RC then demands.

## 2. Working memory and reading comprehension

### 2.1 The foundational result (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980)

Daneman & Carpenter's reading-span task — read sentences aloud, recall their final words in order — produced spans of 2 to 5 in 20 college students. **Span correlated strongly with three reading-comprehension measures, including verbal SAT and fact-retrieval/pronominal-reference tasks.** Word span alone (storage) did not. [Daneman & Carpenter, 1980, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022537180903126) | [ERIC summary](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ230538) (accessed 2026-05-07)

### 2.2 Daneman & Merikle meta-analysis (1996) and Peng et al. (2013/2014)

- Daneman & Merikle (1996): combined processing-and-storage measures (reading/listening span) predict comprehension better than storage-only measures (word/digit span). [Daneman & Merikle, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8920099/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- Peng et al. meta-analysis, 77 studies, 6,179 participants: confirmed Daneman & Carpenter's claim that combined-capacity measures outperform storage-only measures in predicting comprehension. [Working memory and language comprehension meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03214546) (accessed 2026-05-07)

### 2.3 2015+ updates: working-memory updating drives inference

Recent work converges on a sharper claim: **the "updating" subcomponent of working memory — replacing stale content with new — predicts inference-based comprehension specifically**.

- Working-memory updating is engaged "especially when deeper comprehension is necessary, as in the case of inferences." [Updating and reading comprehension, PMC10110648](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10110648/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- Working-memory capacity (not prior knowledge) predicts which readers maintain attention and comprehension on dense text. [Frontiers in Education, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.00026/full) (accessed 2026-05-07)

### 2.4 Implication for strategy: re-read or memorize?

This is the central practical question. The literature points to a specific answer:

- RC questions on standardized tests require **holding the passage's structure** (claim/evidence/counter-argument) while **comparing it to each answer choice**. That comparison loads working memory.
- Trying to **memorize** passage content in detail loads working memory during reading and crowds out structural understanding. This is the failure mode of high-test-anxiety students per Beilock & Carr (2005) — they exhaust WMC on the storage task and can't perform the comparison task.
- Trying to **re-read** the full passage per question loads working memory with surface text again, costing time and rebuilding the same comparison pressure repeatedly.
- The working-memory-friendly approach: **build a mental skeleton during reading** (paragraph-level claims and how they relate), then **search-and-verify** for detail questions, **re-derive** for inference questions from the skeleton. This off-loads to the page (which is right there) what would otherwise be held in WM.

This matches Daneman & Carpenter's original interpretation: it is the **processing capacity** (operating on and structuring text), not the **storage capacity** (memorizing words), that predicts comprehension. Students should train for the skeleton, not the detail.

## 3. Test anxiety effects specifically on RC

### 3.1 Eysenck's Attentional Control Theory (Eysenck et al., 2007)

ACT updates Eysenck's earlier processing-efficiency theory. Anxiety:

- Impairs the **goal-directed attentional system** and amplifies the **stimulus-driven attentional system** — students get pulled to whatever is salient (the clock, a hard word, body sensations).
- Specifically targets the **central executive's inhibition and shifting functions**.
- Hurts **efficiency** (resources used) more than **effectiveness** (final accuracy), because anxious students compensate by working harder — but at a cost. [Eysenck, Derakshan, Santos & Calvo, 2007, Emotion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516812/) | [PDF mirror, TU Dresden](https://tu-dresden.de/die_tu_dresden/fakultaeten/fakultaet_mathematik_und_naturwissenschaften/fachrichtung_psychologie/i1/allgpsy/lehre/pruefungsliteratur_KN_2013/Eysenck-2007.pdf) (accessed 2026-05-07)

For RC: the compensation tax is real on a timed test. Anxious students may reach the same accuracy on Passage 1 — but ran out of time on Passage 3.

### 3.2 Beilock & Carr (2005, 2007) — paradoxical high-WM choking

- High-WM-capacity individuals **choke disproportionately** under pressure on math problems demanding WM. [Beilock & Carr, 2005, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2005.00789.x)
- Pressure consumes the very WMC that high performers normally use. The mechanism: task-irrelevant worry occupies WM. [Beilock & Carr, 2007, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17484426/) (accessed 2026-05-07)

The DAT is high-stakes for dental-school applicants. Strong students with high WM are paradoxically at risk on RC under pressure.

### 3.3 Detail vs. inference questions

The empirical picture is mixed but leans toward inference being more vulnerable:

- Calvo & Carreiras (2003) and follow-ups: cognitive test anxiety affects both recall and inferential reasoning, with **content from expository text "not fully available" to high-anxiety students**. Inference questions appear more impaired than literal-retrieval questions where the answer can be matched directly to the text. [Cognitive test anxiety and text comprehension, Ball State PDF](https://espace.bsu.edu/aarc/wp-content/uploads/sites/142/2016/09/CTA-nonevaluativepressure.pdf) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- A behavioral-genetics study of 426 twin pairs (grades 3–7) on the FCAT found r = −.28 to −.29 between test-anxiety dimensions (worry, autonomic) and reading scores, but did not separate question types. [Test anxiety and high-stakes reading comprehension, PMC5487000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5487000/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- A 2022 high-powered study (Theobald, Breitwieser & Brod) cautions that **test anxiety does not predict exam performance once knowledge is controlled for**, challenging the strong interference hypothesis. [Theobald et al., Psychological Science 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976221119391) (accessed 2026-05-07)

The honest read: anxiety probably matters most on questions that require **holding structure and inferring relationships** (working-memory updating); detail-locate questions that can be solved by ctrl-F-style search are more robust to anxiety, because they offload to the page.

## 4. Stamina-building protocols (LSAT/MCAT community wisdom, evidence-filtered)

The prep-community literature converges on a small set of practices. Filtered for evidence-backed claims (i.e., things consistent with the fatigue/WM science above, not "speed-reading guru" claims):

### 4.1 Full-length mocks at original pacing and start time

- **Start your mocks at the same time the real DAT starts.** Circadian alignment matters; Sievertsen's PNAS result is fundamentally a time-of-day effect. [MedLife Mastery — Building MCAT stamina](https://medlifemastery.com/mcat/preparation/mindset/build-mcat-stamina/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- **Weekly full-length is the upper bound, not a baseline.** 2–3 practice tests per week is generally not recommended on the LSAT either. [PowerScore Forum, LSAT stamina](https://forum.powerscore.com/viewtopic.php?t=6778) | [Blueprint Prep, LSAT speed/endurance](https://blog.blueprintprep.com/lsat/how-to-improve-your-lsat-speed-and-endurance/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- **Build with timed sub-blocks on non-mock days.** A 70-min section, 10-min break, 60–70-min next block trains the same sustained-attention muscle. [Leah4Sci — MCAT endurance](https://leah4sci.com/how-to-how-to-build-endurance-for-the-7-5-hour-mcat/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- **Phasing:** start with one section/day untimed if accuracy is unstable, work up to timed sections, then half-tests, then full-lengths. Full-length cadence rises in the last 4–6 weeks before test day. Most students see measurable RC improvement in 4–6 weeks of focused work. [Impetus LSAT — Build stamina](https://www.impetuslsat.com/post/build-lsat-stamina) | [Orgoman, DAT RC strategies 2026](https://orgoman.com/blogs/dental-admissions-test-and-road-to-dental-school-acceptance/dat-reading-comprehension) (accessed 2026-05-07)

### 4.2 What the science actually backs in that list

- Practice-at-real-start-time: backed by chronotype / time-of-day effects (Sievertsen).
- Sub-blocks mirroring section length: backed by Schmeichel (executive control is depletable, must be trained), Boksem (energetic-cost evaluation adapts to expectation), and basic specificity-of-practice principles in motor/cognitive learning.
- Two full-lengths per week is the ceiling: backed by chronic-fatigue/recovery principles in Banks & Dinges; doing too many full-lengths cuts into sleep and recovery.

## 5. Mid-test recovery techniques (during the 60-min RC block, between sections)

### 5.1 Diaphragmatic / paced breathing

- Slow diaphragmatic breathing stimulates the vagus nerve and engages the parasympathetic system. [Diaphragmatic breathing meta-analysis, ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0965229925001931) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- A meta-analysis of 40 RCTs on respiratory interventions for anxiety found significantly lower anxiety symptoms vs. control. [Breathwork meta-analysis, Scientific Reports 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-27247-y) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- Caveat: device-guided studies show **large effect size for anxiety reduction but not always statistical significance in small pilots**. [Device-guided breathing for test anxiety, Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.678098/full) (accessed 2026-05-07)

Practical: 4-7-8 breath or 4-4-4-4 box breath for 30–60 seconds between passages. Cheap, no downside.

### 5.2 Brief breaks and micro-recovery

- Sievertsen's 1.7% SD recovery from a 20–30 minute break is the closest empirical benchmark. The DAT has one optional break between PAT and RC — **take it**. [Sievertsen 2016, PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26884183/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- The 20-20-20 rule (look 20 ft away for 20 seconds every 20 min) is widely promoted but **has limited direct peer-reviewed validation**. [20-20-20 evidence review, Optometry Times](https://modernod.com/topics/myopia/myth-busting-the-202020-rule/38651/) | [Talens-Estarelles et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35963776/) (accessed 2026-05-07) It does relieve dry-eye symptoms; cognitive benefit is plausible-but-unproven.
- Pomodoro-style micro-breaks improve sustained attention in study contexts (consistent with break literature). Between passages on RC, a 10-second eye-rest plus a slow exhale is the realistic ceiling.

### 5.3 Posture reset and arousal reappraisal

- Stress-arousal reappraisal (reframe physical symptoms as "my body is helping me perform"): Jamieson et al. (2016, 2024 meta-analysis) found d = 0.23 overall improvement in task performance from reappraisal interventions; d = 0.45 for mixed interventions. [Jamieson et al., 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1948550616644656) | [Reappraisal meta-analysis, Scientific Reports 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-58408-w) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- This is one of the better-supported single-instruction interventions. Teach students to reframe heart racing on RC as "fuel" not "panic."

## 6. Nutrition and hydration during testing

### 6.1 Glucose and attention

- Glucose alone improves attention, processing speed, and working memory. [Glucose effects on continuous performance test, PubMed 12798283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12798283/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- **Caffeine + glucose combination outperforms either alone** on multi-tasking, attention, and verbal memory. [Caffeine+glucose multi-tasking study, PMC4265209](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4265209/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- Very large glucose doses do not further help attention and can impair impulse control. Moderate doses win.

### 6.2 Caffeine timing

- Peak plasma caffeine occurs **30–60 minutes after ingestion**, max around 45 minutes. [Caffeine cognitive effects review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763416300690) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- Low-to-moderate doses (40–200 mg, roughly 0.5–4 mg/kg) reliably enhance attention, vigilance, and reaction time. [Caffeine + cognition review, MindLab Pro summary](https://www.mindlabpro.com/blogs/nootropics/effects-of-caffeine-on-cognitive-tasks) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- 100 mg shows sustained accuracy benefit at 30 min; 200 mg can cause **decline by 60 min** for some — lower steady doses outperform a single big slug. [Caffeine dose-timing study, MDPI 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/4/1703) (accessed 2026-05-07)

Practical DAT protocol: a moderate caffeine dose (100–150 mg) 30–45 min before test start; a small additional dose (50–100 mg) at the mid-test break, paired with simple carbs — but only if the student is a habitual coffee drinker, since caffeine-naive students risk jitters and bathroom interruptions.

### 6.3 Hydration

Mild dehydration (~1–2% body water loss) measurably degrades attention and mood. The test center does not allow drinking at the desk, so steady hydration before and at the break is the lever. No need to belabor this; it's settled.

## 7. Sleep deprivation effects on RC

### 7.1 Banks & Dinges (2007) — chronic restriction thresholds

- **7 hours/night is the lower bound** below which deficits accumulate. [Banks & Dinges 2007, PMC1978335](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1978335/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- 6 hours/night for 14 days → lapses comparable to 1–2 nights of total sleep deprivation.
- 4 hours/night for 14 days → lapses comparable to 2–3 nights of total sleep deprivation.
- Critically: **subjective sleepiness ratings did not match objective deficit.** Students who feel "fine" on 5 hours are not fine.

### 7.2 RC-specific evidence

- Walker (2009) and Lim & Dinges (2010): sleep deprivation hits **sustained attention** and **working memory** hardest — the two pillars of RC performance. Pure reasoning is largely preserved. [Walker, 2009, NYAS lab PDF](https://walkerlab.berkeley.edu/reprints/Walker_NYAS_2009.pdf) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- An older study found six subjects sleep-deprived overnight showed "little evidence of reduced reading rate or comprehension" but **only three of six maintained non-deprived performance** — a small sample with large individual variability. [Sleep deprivation and reading comprehension, PubMed 3741938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3741938/) (accessed 2026-05-07)
- The relationship between sleep quality and reading comprehension in college students is positive and significant. [Self-reported sleep quality and reading comprehension, PMC4608899](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4608899/) (accessed 2026-05-07)

### 7.3 Practical baseline

- **Test-week target: 7.5–8.5 hours/night for at least 5 consecutive nights pre-test.** Going below 7 even for 2–3 nights moves a student into measurable WM/attention deficit.
- The night before is overrated. Banks & Dinges show **chronic restriction is what accumulates**; a single normal night cannot reverse a week of 5-hour nights.
- Conversely, don't try to "sleep-load" — extending well beyond habitual sleep duration on the night before can fragment sleep and increase next-morning grogginess.

## Bottom-line synthesis

### Documented magnitude of decline by hour 4

- **~0.9% SD per hour, ~3–4% SD by hour 4** of a sustained testing session, based on Sievertsen et al.'s natural experiment. A 20–30 minute mid-test break recovers ~1.7% SD.
- Mechanism is concentrated on **sustained attention** (Lim & Dinges: ḡ = −0.76 for vigilance lapses under stress) and **error monitoring** (Boksem & Tops: post-error adjustment is what fails first).
- Pure reasoning is most preserved (ḡ = −0.125, n.s.). What erodes is the ability to **stay on the page and check your work**, not the ability to think.

### WM-load implication: re-read or memorize?

- **Neither, mostly.** The working-memory cost of full memorization (Beilock; Daneman & Carpenter) and the time cost of full re-reads both consume the central executive that RC demands.
- The WM-friendly approach is **structural encoding during reading** — build a paragraph-level skeleton of claims and how they relate — then **search-and-verify on detail questions** (offload to the page) and **re-derive from skeleton on inference questions** (operate on a small, compressed mental model rather than the surface text).
- Per Peng et al. and the updating literature, **inference questions specifically tax working-memory updating**; this is where anxious or fatigued students lose the most points.

### Three evidence-backed pre-RC mental-prep techniques

1. **Stress-arousal reappraisal** (Jamieson et al.): a 1-minute instruction to reframe racing heart and tight chest as "fuel" rather than "panic." Meta-analytic d ≈ 0.23 on performance; up to d ≈ 0.45 when combined with other framing.
2. **Slow diaphragmatic breathing**, 30–60 seconds before opening Passage 1: vagal-tone activation, parasympathetic shift. Strong RCT evidence for anxiety reduction; suggestive evidence for performance improvement.
3. **Brief expressive writing** if the student is high-test-anxiety: 5–10 min before the test, write out the worries. Original Ramirez & Beilock effect (2011) was substantial for anxious students; **note: replication failures by Camerer et al. (2018) — present this as low-cost rather than guaranteed.**

### Minimum sleep + nutrition baseline that matters most for test day

- **Sleep: ≥7 hours/night for the 5 nights before the test, with 7.5–8.5 the target.** This is the single largest controllable variable for RC. Banks & Dinges show one good night cannot reverse a week of restriction; the night-before is overrated, the week-before is everything.
- **Nutrition: a moderate, mixed-macro breakfast 60–90 min before test start** (complex carb + protein, not a sugar slug); a small simple-carb snack at the mid-test break to keep glucose steady before RC.
- **Caffeine: 100–150 mg, 30–45 min before test start, only if habitually consumed.** A second small dose (50–100 mg) at the mid-test break is reasonable; avoid stepping up to 200+ mg at once (accuracy can decline by 60 min in some individuals).
- **Hydration: steady through the morning and at the break.** Mild dehydration measurably degrades attention; the test center does not let you drink at the desk.
