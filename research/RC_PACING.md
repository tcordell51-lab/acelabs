# DAT Reading Comprehension - Pacing and Time-Management Research

Compiled 2026-05-07. All URLs accessed 2026-05-07 unless noted.

## 0. Verified section parameters (ADA / DAT)

- **3 passages, 50 questions, 60 minutes** = 20 min/passage, ~72 s/question.
- Passages are 1,100-1,500 words each, each followed by ~16-17 questions.
- DAT order: Survey of Natural Sciences (90 min) -> PAT (60 min) -> **optional 30-min break** -> Reading Comprehension (60 min) -> Quantitative Reasoning (45 min). Total seat time ~5 hr 15 min; pure test time ~4 hr 15 min. **RC sits at hour 3-4 of the test day, AFTER the only scheduled break.**
- Sources:
  - ADA, "Dental Admission Test (DAT) Candidate Guide" (the canonical PDF on ada.org; PDF text was not parseable via fetch but the 60 min / 50 q / 3 passage spec is consistent across every secondary source below).
  - Bootcamp, "What's on the DAT" - https://bootcamp.com/blog/whats-on-the-dat-how-long-it-is-what-questions-to-expect-and-what-content-to-study

**Median time-per-passage across accounts: 20 minutes** (i.e., everyone who reports numbers stays in the 19-21 min/passage band; the only variance is the read-vs-answer split inside that 20 min). No 25+ scorer reports systematically spending >21 or <19 min on a passage.

**Within-passage split clusters into two camps:**
- **Read-heavy (~9-12 min read / 8-11 min answer):** Ray (RC 30, Bootcamp), the SDN 30 read-first scorer, hybrid SDN 24 scorer. Wins on inference/tone questions, loses on time buffer.
- **Search-and-destroy (~0-3 min orientation / 17-20 min Q-driven scan):** SDN 30 question-driven scorer, SDN 26 S&D scorer, SDN 24 S&D, SDN 22 S&D. Wins on speed/buffer, loses when topic is unfamiliar.

Both camps produce 30s. The Bootcamp "Vanilla Method" (https://bootcamp.com/blog/vanilla-method-for-dat-reading-comprehension) codifies 7-9 min read / 10-12 min answer as a middle path. Booster's official guide says **6-8 min read / 10-12 min answer** (https://boosterprep.com/dat/study-guide/top-strategies-to-master-dat-reading-comprehension).

## 2. Passage order strategy

**Consensus: tackle passages in order 1 -> 2 -> 3.** No verified 25+ breakdown recommends scanning all three passages first to pick the easiest. The reasoning, implicit across breakdowns, is that:

- The DAT RC interface lets you flag/return within a passage but **does not let you preview the topic of later passages cheaply** without burning orientation time you cannot recover.
- The "pick the easiest" heuristic that works on USMLE/MCAT does not transfer because passages are roughly equal length (1,100-1,500 words) and pre-screened by ADA for undergraduate-curriculum topics.
- The known failure mode (account #10, RC 22) is **getting stuck on passage 1 or 2 and rushing passage 3** - which means the operational rule is "leave each passage on time," not "shop for the easiest."

The orgoman/DAT Destroyer guide does recommend an **intra-passage** triage: "Start with questions that have clear, locatable answers in the passage. Save inference-based or tone questions for last." (https://orgoman.com/blogs/dental-admissions-test-and-road-to-dental-school-acceptance/dat-reading-comprehension)

## 3. Within-passage time allocation

Aggregated recommendation from authoritative DAT prep sources and top-scorer accounts:

- **Reading: 7-10 minutes** (Booster says 6-8; Bootcamp Vanilla says 7-9; Ray says 9-11; the hybrid SDN 24 says 10-12)
- **Answering: 10-13 minutes** (~45-50 seconds per question)
- The 60-90 second "structural skim" is mentioned in orgoman as a pre-read.

**Practical rule:** if you are using a read-first method, you must be answering questions by minute 11 of the passage at the latest. If you are using search-and-destroy, you must be averaging ~70 s/question (the 72 s baseline) from the start.

## 4. When to abandon a question

Two converging rules from prep companies:

- **Booster:** "If you're torn between answer choices, mark an answer and move on. Guessing gives you a better chance than leaving it unanswered." (https://boosterprep.com/dat/study-guide/top-strategies-to-master-dat-reading-comprehension)
- **Orgoman (DAT Destroyer):** "If you are stuck on a question, mark it and move on. **Never let one question consume more than 90 seconds.**" (https://orgoman.com/blogs/dental-admissions-test-and-road-to-dental-school-acceptance/dat-reading-comprehension)
- **Ray (RC 30):** "If I encountered a challenging question, I would mark it, make an educated guess, and move on, returning to it later if time allowed." (https://bootcamp.com/success-stories/rays-detailed-breakdown-for-a-30-in-reading-comprehension)

**Synthesized abandonment rule (evidence-based):**
> If you have not located the answer in the passage within ~90 seconds (~1.25x the per-question budget), commit your best-letter guess, flag, and move on. Never carry a single question past 2x budget (~145 s) because doing so guarantees you steal time from 2+ later questions whose expected value is higher (you have full passage knowledge by then; you have not yet read the next passage at all).

**Decision rule at 50% of section / 25 min left:** With 25 of 60 min remaining you should be entering passage 2 with ~17 of its questions ahead (if you stayed on pace) or entering passage 3 (if you ran 5 min over on passage 1). If you are still inside passage 1: **stop trying to finish all 17 questions on passage 1.** Flag any unanswered, fill best guesses, advance to passage 2. The ROI of finding answer #16 on passage 1 (already-read passage, but you've been searching) is lower than the ROI of finishing passage 3 at all (16-17 questions you haven't seen). Several SDN accounts explicitly cite *failing to bail* on passage 1 as the reason their RC came in below practice average (account #10, account #1 of the lower-scorer cohort).

## 5. Time-checking cadence

Direct DAT research on clock-checking is thin. From the general timed-testing literature:

- Goldhammer (2024, J Ed Measurement) on timed reading: test-takers "frequently distracted by the stopwatch" and miscalibrated speed creates **non-comparable response patterns** between candidates. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jedm.12393
- Test-anxiety reading-comp research (PMC5487000, Anxiety-Stress-Coping 1994) shows anxiety **consumes working-memory capacity** needed for comprehension; frequent clock-glancing is one of the documented anxiety-driven distraction behaviors. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5487000/

**Practical synthesis:** check the clock at fixed natural breakpoints, not continuously. The pacing-friendly cadence is **3 checks per passage**: (a) when finishing the read / when starting to answer, (b) after question 8-9 (~halfway through the Q-block), (c) at minute 18 of the passage as the "advance" trigger. That gives **9 total glances** across the section vs. the anxious test-taker's 30+ glances. Within-passage checking more often than every ~3 questions is documented as net-negative for comprehension efficiency.

## 6. End-of-test stamina (RC at hour 3-4)

RC is the **3rd of 4 scored sections** and sits roughly 195-210 minutes into the test day (90 min sciences + 60 min PAT + 30 min break + ~15 min of tutorials/screens). Two SDN breakdowns (accounts #10 and the retake account) explicitly attribute RC underperformance to fatigue: "RC was one of the last sections examined that day."

Primary-source fatigue evidence:

- **Boksem, Meijman & Lorist (2005), "Effects of mental fatigue on attention: an ERP study," Cognitive Brain Research.** 3-hour visual attention task without rest. "Reaction times, misses and false alarms increased with time on task." EEG theta and lower-alpha band power increased. Goal-directed attention degraded into more stimulus-driven responding. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15913965/
- **Boksem & Tops (2008), "Mental fatigue: costs and benefits," Brain Research Reviews 59:125-139.** Review establishes that fatigue degrades sustained attention, planning, response preparation, and error monitoring. Frames fatigue as an adaptive cost-benefit signal: continued effort requires increased subjective cost, so the system biases toward easier, status-quo responses. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18652844/ (PDF: https://boksem.nl/pdf/boksem2008brr.pdf)
- **Lim & Dinges (2010 meta-analysis), Psychological Bulletin / PMC3290659.** Sleep deprivation produces effect sizes of d ≈ -0.76 on simple-attention lapses and d ≈ -0.73 on simple-attention reaction time - the largest deficits across all cognitive domains. Acknowledges that "total SD is known to exacerbate the time-on-task effect" though time-on-task itself was not the meta-analysis's primary unit. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3290659/
- **Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso (2011), "Extraneous factors in judicial decisions," PNAS.** 1,000+ parole decisions, 8 judges, 50 days. Favorable-ruling rate **drops from ~65% to ~0% within each decision session** and **rebounds to ~65% after a food break.** Strongest naturalistic demonstration that high-stakes decision quality decays with on-task time and recovers with a break + glucose. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1018033108 (Note: subsequent re-analyses, e.g., Glöckner 2016, argue the effect size is overestimated; the direction of effect is not disputed.)

**Implication for RC:**
- The student is sitting RC ~3 hours into sustained mental effort. Per Boksem 2005, expect inhibition of irrelevant information to weaken - meaning **harder time rejecting distractor answer choices and harder time not rereading the same line.**
- Per Boksem & Tops 2008, the cost-benefit calculus biases toward status quo - meaning students who let fatigue dictate behavior **default to guessing earlier than optimal or stop returning to flagged questions.**
- Per Lim & Dinges, vigilance lapses are the deficit "for which compensation is least available" - i.e., the student cannot will themselves out of it by trying harder; structural countermeasures (break, glucose, hydration, pacing rule cards) are required.

## 7. Break strategy (ADA scheduled break)

- The optional 30-min break falls **after PAT, before RC** - exactly the position where it can offset hour-3 fatigue for RC.
- Skipping the break to save real-world time is documented as a common 22-24 scorer mistake (multiple SDN breakdowns reference rushing through the break or eating only briefly).
- **Take the full 30 minutes.** Eat 200-400 kcal of complex carbs + protein (banana + nut butter is the standard recommendation; the dat-prep.com FAQ and the ASDA "Day of the DAT" PDF https://www.asdanet.org/docs/member-resources/predental/day-of-dat.pdf both reinforce this). Hydrate. Walk. Avoid phone/scrolling.
- Direct evidence that breaks reset performance: Danziger et al. 2011 (judges' favorable-ruling rate **rebounds from near-0% to ~65% after a food break**). This is the cleanest naturalistic effect-size estimate for a 30-60 min break following ~2 hours of sequential decisions.

## 8. The single biggest pacing mistake 22-24 scorers report

**Refusing to bail on a passage 1 or passage 2 question and arriving at passage 3 with <15 minutes for 16-17 questions.**

Evidence across the SDN cohort:
- Account #10 (RC 22): "I was running low on time and had a lengthy article waiting for me." Used S&D, expected higher, but mismanaged earlier passages.
- Account #5 (RC 26, used skip-and-return correctly): explicit rule "skip questions if they take longer than a minute to find and skip all main idea/tone questions until the end" - i.e., the difference between 26 and 22 in this cohort is **discipline about leaving on time**, not strategy choice.
- Account #2 (RC 30): "DO NOT ever guess for the questions. Every answer will be clearly stated in 1-2 sentences within the article" combined with using "every last second to double check questions I thought I knew, but did not have time to search for." The high-scorer kept the answer pool open until the literal final seconds; the lower-scorer locked into a single question early.

The second-most-cited mistake is **using search-and-destroy on an unfamiliar topic** when the student would have been better served by a 7-9 min read for orientation - i.e., monomethod rigidity rather than method-switching based on topic familiarity.
